## Review of seven parts

**Part 1: What is “computing”? **

We asked a seemingly simple question: What is "computing"? Then I discovered that the problem was much deeper than imagined. From Leibniz's dream of using symbols to deduce all truths, to Hilbert's use of 23 questions to construct "the entire foundation of mathematics", to Gödel's incompleteness theorem to knock a crack in the foundation of Hilbert's building ("Any sufficiently strong formal system has propositions that are not self-provable"), to Turing's use of an infinitely long paper tape and a finite state automaton to cleanly define the boundary of "computability". Von Neumann then moved the drawing from the mathematician's desk to the circuit engineer's workbench. He defined an architecture that allowed programs and data to share the same memory, and the concept of software was born.

**"Calculation" is the "ultimate" inquiry that human beings try to use to seek the infinite. ** The "calculations" we have been able to do so far have only scratched the surface of this inquiry. But you, as an engineer who writes C code every day, makes CAN messages rush on the bus at 500kbps, and makes the SPI flash spit out data in tens of microseconds. You don't need to know Gödel's incompleteness theorem. Turing and von Neumann have done the low-level work of abstraction for you. You stand on their shoulders, and every line of `if`, every `while` loop, and every function call in the code already contains those brain insights.

**Part Two: From Sand to Chips**

You press the brake pedal and the brake lights come on. Behind this half-movement is a silicon rod that starts from sand, is smelted at a high temperature of 1420°C in a quartz crucible, is pulled into a single crystal silicon rod, cut into 0.7mm thick wafers with a diamond wire saw, and undergoes dozens of photolithography (DUV 193nm light source passes through the reticle Densely patterned windows are used to hit the photoresist layer on the wafer), etching (plasma bombardment to remove the areas exposed by the photoresist), ion implantation (using a million-volt accelerator to drive boron or phosphorus atoms into the precise depth of the silicon lattice), CMP (chemical mechanical polishing. Grinding the surface of each layer to atomic level flatness) cycles, and finally the chip that becomes S32K144 is responding.

**Chips are the ultimate carving of the most ordinary substances by humans. They are the result of the joint efforts of dozens of disciplines and the collaboration of thousands of companies around the world. ** Excavator drivers at an Australian mine dig quartz ore, which contains silica, from the ground. The chemical plant reduces quartz to metallurgical grade silicon and then purifies it through the Siemens process to 99.9999999% (nine nines) electronic grade polysilicon. Japan's Shin-Etsu Chemical or Germany's Wacker pulls polysilicon into 300mm single-crystal rods. The EUV lithography machine (used in advanced nodes) manufactured by ASML in the Netherlands uses extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5nm to project the design pattern from the reticle onto the wafer by bombarding tin droplets with a carbon dioxide laser to create a plasma. Fab engineers in Taiwan or Mainland China use hundreds of processes to carve billions of transistors onto a piece of silicon wafer the size of a fingernail. The packaging factory then bonds the die to the lead frame and seals it into a small black square with plastic sealant. Then you get your hands on it, write the code, and make it work on the ECU you designed.

The indicator lights on the dashboard that you look down at are behind the work of millions of people throughout their lives. This is not shocking rhetoric, this is material reality. Every chip you touch is the end point at your fingertips of a supply chain that spans five continents, involves dozens of industries, and has invested trillions in capital accumulation.

---**Part Three: From Logic Gates to Processors**

Designing a chip is not like building a building. You can't start from the first floor and build up one floor at a time, and then go back and change the columns on the first floor when the main body is finished. Chips are manufactured layer by layer. Substrate → Doped well → Gate oxide layer → Polysilicon gate → Injection source and drain → Contact hole → Metal layer → Through hole → Metal layer two... Each layer is permanently fixed on the wafer. Once it's done, you can't go back and change it. The cost of "making just one sample": a photolithography mask costs hundreds of thousands of dollars. Therefore, chip design cannot be "tried and tested". It must use simulation and verification to exhaustively confirm the behavior of each logic gate and each metal line before the first photomask is made.

From two MOSFETs, one PMOS + one NMOS, build an inverter. Build the NAND gate from the inverter. Build adders and multiplexers from NAND gates. From adders and registers build the ALU (arithmetic logic unit) and instruction decoder. Folded layer by layer, the end result is a complete CPU. This is **"Essence is not complicated, complexity is the unfolding of essence."**

Every time you understand a layer of abstraction, you are dismantling a layer of "mystery." When all the "mystery" is removed, what you see is not a simple circuit. What you see is the wonder of finite gate circuits producing infinite behavior through combinatorial explosion. The 32-bit instruction set defines about a thousand opcodes, addressing modes, and register combinations. Combined, any logic from sorting algorithms to operating systems to CAN protocol stacks can be implemented. A logic gate is nothing. But after billions of logic gates are organized into a processor through hierarchical abstraction, it can compile your code from C to binary, burn it into Flash, and control tens of millions of ignitions. It's not magic, it's engineering. But the depth and complexity of the project have exceeded the brain capacity of any single individual. And you can understand it because abstraction helps you divide the complexity into manageable modules. This is the gift of engineering.

**Part 4: How to talk between chips**

Chips don't "talk" to each other, they "exchange electrical signals." But the difficulty of "exchanging electrical signals" is magnified countless times in a car. The ECU is not placed in a clean, quiet, constant temperature environment like the development board on your desk. It is installed in the engine compartment. With temperatures ranging from -40°C to 125°C, the EMC environment is where multiple high-current PWM drivers, ignition coils, DC-DC converters and Bluetooth/Wi-Fi/4G antennas coexist in a metal cavity. Signals propagate in such an environment. The copper wire is the antenna, the GND plane is not truly zero potential, and the contact impedance of the connector drifts with temperature. Getting a group of MCUs to "speak" to each other in such a world requires not only protocols, but a deep understanding of the physical world.

The internal handshake of the AHB/APB bus: high-speed pipeline communication between the CPU core and on-chip peripherals, completed on a few millimeters of copper lines within the same silicon die. SPI's four-wire full-duplex: With a clock of more than a dozen megahertz, the equal length between your data lines and clock lines is controlled to the sub-millimeter level, because the timing tolerance is measured in nanoseconds. I²C's two-wire multi-slave: There are only two wires, one for clock and one for data, but it can construct a master-slave control network between dozens of sensors and actuators on a board. The cost is slow speed but the wiring harness is saved to the extreme. Twisted-pair priority arbitration of the CAN bus: two differential lines cancel out common-mode noise in the electromagnetic hell of the car, and the 11-bit identifier does both address and priority arbitration. There is no need for a master node or clock synchronization. The arbitration is self-organized during the transmission of each bit.Every agreement has its own temperament, and no agreement is "ultimate". Placing the right protocol in the right place is a core decision in system design.  SPI is fast but not suitable for long distances and is suitable for inter-board communication. CAN is slow but robust to noise and suitable for body networks and power domains. I²C is wire-saving but has extremely low bandwidth, making it suitable for on-board sensors. FlexRay is accurate but expensive, suitable for brake-by-wire. Automotive Ethernet is fast but requires AVB/TSN to ensure clock synchronization, which is suitable for ADAS and infotainment. Only when you know their temperaments can you know where to put them. This is the architecture. Architecture is not about drawing frames on a diagram. Architecture is to find a topological solution that allows all signals to reach their destination on time and by value within the constraints of physical laws.

**Part 5: How the software is organized**

Bare metal `while(1)` + interrupt. There is only one engineer, no RTOS, no heap protection, and no MPU, but you use timer interrupts for periodic patrols, SPI interrupts for transceiver polling, and GPIO interrupts for button debounce. Each function runs in its own time slice. This is the simplest and true you. Every embedded engineer starts with bare metal: you start from the moment the system powers up and the PC register jumps to the reset vector address, to the first loop of `while(1)`. Every line of code you write in this process directly operates peripheral registers.

Then RTOS came. Task scheduling, priority preemption, semaphore, mutex lock, message queue, software timer. Each piece gives you "one more thing to do." One more task requires one more stack. One more semaphore requires one more critical section protection. But what RTOS gives you in return is **time determinism**: the switching delay of the highest priority ready task is constant, no matter how many lower priority tasks are waiting. For real-time control systems, it is not how much performance you optimize, but how much you ensure that tasks will never miss deadlines.

Then AUTOSAR came. SWC, BSW, RTE, MCAL, ARXML configuration. Each layer is a **reuse framework** that was summed up by predecessors at a huge price of tuition. SWC cannot see the application logic on the upper layer of the underlying hardware. The BSW service layer modularizes functions such as CAN communication, NVM storage, diagnostics, and watchdogs. RTE is the bonding layer between SWC and BSW. SWC does not directly call BSW, it communicates through virtual function bus (VFB) through RTE. MCAL encapsulates specific MCU peripheral registers into standard APIs. AUTOSAR is "heavy" not because someone wants to make you miserable, but because it solves a real problem: when 30 Tier-1s provide 80 ECUs for the same car, and the OEM needs a unified way to diagnose, flash, calibrate, and upgrade all these ECUs. Without AUTOSAR, the software and communication of each module of each Tier-1 company would be an independent system. Integration costs are astronomical.

**Standards are a symbol of industry civilization and maturity. ** AUTOSAR is not "to make the software heavier", but to allow different Tier-1 software to run on the same ECU, preventing OBD diagnostic tools from being unable to read DTCs due to different manufacturers. Its value will be felt the first time you integrate it across vendors. When you plug in an ECU from another vendor, it responds correctly to your UDS request because it also uses AUTOSAR's Dcm module to implement diagnostic services.

**Part 6: Unique Challenges of Automotive Electronic Systems**

A car doesn't just have to run. It also needs to be able to diagnose itself (UDS), not be attacked (HSM), and not lose data (NVM storage and OTA refresh). These three problems are almost non-existent on PC software. The PC does not need to diagnose "why it cannot be turned on" (the user will re-insert and unplug the memory stick), does not need to encrypt the firmware (the user trusts the app store), and does not need to care about the Flash erasing and writing life (the wear leveling of SSD is done by the operating system, and the cost of replacing a hard drive is much lower than replacing an ECU by the OEM). But not cars. The code on the car runs for 15 years. There will be no visible functional safety failure in the entire temperature range and throughout the entire life.A diagnosis is a letter from the engineer to future repairers. "When you get this car, I am no longer here. There are 57 DTCs on this ECU, and the environmental data (freeze frame) of each DTC records the vehicle speed, engine speed, coolant temperature, battery voltage when the fault occurred... If you see the fault code 0x0607, check the CAN communication first, and then check the power supply. Your multimeter hits pin 12 first." When you write a UDS diagnostic service, you are not filling out a form. You are writing a **remote help manual** for a repairman who does not know you and will never meet you, but is worried about a faulty light in a 4S shop.

Security (whether HSM, SecOC, or Secure Boot) is the technical foundation of the social contract. You wrote Secure Boot on a car. Your ECU verifies the application's RSA signature every time it is powered up using the PKA (Public Key Accelerator) inside the HSM. If the signatures do not match, the bootloader refuses to jump to the App and the ECU does not work. You may be thinking: Wouldn’t this make the user “unable to drive”? Yes. But if your ECU is flashed with malicious firmware. The attacker gained control of the braking system. What users face is not "cannot drive", but "brake failure while driving". You make the ECU "refuse to work" at startup in order to prevent the ECU from "working incorrectly" at runtime. This is security and protection for users.

OTA is a profound proposition in another dimension. You write the software, install the ECU on the car, and the car leaves the factory. Your code will be on the car for 15 years. In these 15 years, you will find new bugs, customers will come up with new requirements, and standards organizations will update new specifications. How do you **continuously deliver** your wisdom and experience to a car that has already traveled hundreds of thousands of kilometers on the road? OTA is your only channel. OTA is not just "upgrading software", it is your ongoing responsibility for the product over time. You didn’t give up after you went into production. You're watching it, maintaining it, updating it. This is the long-term responsibility of engineers.

## The essence of engineering: finding the optimal solution in the noiseOne thing I learned while working on yield analysis at a panel factory: You can never control all the variables. You cannot control the initial quality of the glass substrate (from production fluctuations in upstream glass factories), you cannot control the viscosity differences between batches of photoresist (from raw material fluctuations in chemical plants), and you cannot control the optical distortion caused by the lens heating effect of the photolithography machine (after a reticle pattern is repeatedly exposed tens of thousands of times, the lens absorbs heat and causes slight changes in the refractive index). You can only find a statistically optimal process window through repeated measurement, modeling, analysis, and verification in a huge system that is changing everywhere.

This is the entire essence of engineering: it is not the pursuit of perfection, but the pursuit of the optimal solution under given noise conditions. 

This essence is established in the panel factory and is also established in your code. You cannot control the temperature drift of the crystal oscillator, the impact of the surface roughness of the PCB copper wire on EMI, and the timing of message transmission by other ECUs on the CAN bus. But you can use differential signals, bit error rate statistics, timeout retransmission, and CAN's Bus-off recovery mechanism to find a statistically optimal communication window in this changing system.

## You are not a craftsman, you are a deliverer.

I've been thinking about how to define the work you do in one word. You write code, you adjust PCB, you do architecture, and you gnaw on standards. You think you're a "techie" but that's not enough. Even if you think you are an "engineer", this is not enough.

I thought about it for a long time. I think there is a word closest to your essence: **passer. **

**You are not the end. You are the deliverer. **

> *This is the signature sentence of this book, and it is also the sentence that the author wants to engrave into your heart. *

You are not a "craftsman". What a "craftsman" does is to control an entire work (a chair, a knife, a painting) from beginning to end, and all the processes are completed in his own hands. But what you do is not. The ECU you build is just a module on a larger system (entire vehicle). The entire vehicle is just a node in the transportation system. The transportation system is only a subsystem of the entire human industrial civilization. The work you do is not isolated, it is in an invisible but indestructible chain.

**You are a link in the middle. **

You caught the stick handed to you by the person in front of you. You are running your segment. Now you have to pass the baton.

How to deliver? Use **text**.

Your tools are not just a keyboard and a compiler. Your tool also has **logging**. You took over the abstract concept of "computability" from Turing; you took over the multi-master priority arbitration mechanism of the CAN protocol from Bosch; you took over the modular architecture of SWC/BSW/RTE/MCAL from the AUTOSAR standard setter. You stand on the shoulders of thousands of others. And what you are left with is these: the Lessons Learned written after the end of the new project, the 8D report written after the accident investigation, the line of comments added at the key point of the code explaining "Why global interrupts are disabled here", and the "CAN Bus-Off Troubleshooting Guide" posted on the team Wiki. The words will become another shoulder. The next engineer you’ve never met will be standing on your shoulders.

## You can do more than you think

You can blog. It’s not the kind of marketing account that says “Shocked! CAN bus can be used like this”. It’s a real problem you encountered in a real project and your real solution process. Be specific: chip model, register value, oscilloscope screenshot, wrong assumptions, corrected understanding. Even if you only write 1,000 words, as long as you write about your own thought process, it has value. Because there must be another engineer in the world who is facing the exact same problem as you late one night. Your article will appear in his search results. He'll click on it. He can read. He will be saved.You can write internal technical documentation. There must be some knowledge in your team that "only you know and is not in the documentation." For example, the ECR register of the CAN controller will return an error value under certain conditions, and it must be read twice to confirm. You know the pit now. But if you don’t write it down, the next person who takes over your module will spend three days rediscovering it. Three days for you and three days for him. It’s not three days for one person, but six days for two generations of engineers. And all you have to do is spend half an hour writing a Wiki entry. Just write. For that colleague you don’t know but will definitely show up.

You could write a little book. It’s not as big as this one, maybe 10 pages. Just write about the module you are responsible for: a complete implementation guide for CAN diagnostics, practical configuration of the AUTOSAR NVM module, and embedded implementation of the motor control FOC algorithm. There is no requirement for publication. Post it on the company Wiki, post it to GitHub, or upload it to your personal blog. You don't need to be an expert, you just need to be someone who has thought about this problem seriously and written down your thinking process. 

You can write an open source project and write clear code comments and README. A simplest CAN transceiver packaging library. A set of Python scripts for DBC file parsing and C code generation. A minimal RTOS task scheduler for teaching. It's not that important whether the code runs perfectly or not. The most important thing is that every line of comments you say is true. Why do you design the size of the buffer like this. What alternatives have you considered. Which one did you choose and why. These comments outlast your code. The code will be outdated, but your engineering decision-making thinking will travel through time.

**Be a light, even if it only illuminates the corner where you are. **
