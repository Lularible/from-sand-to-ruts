# 4.5 Time Synchronization and PTP

## One hundred ECUs, one hundred kinds of "now"

There are 100 ECUs in your car: engine control unit, ABS controller, ADAS domain controller, instrument panel, T-Box, gateway. Each one has a crystal oscillator, and each crystal oscillator oscillates with its own independent physical beat.

The nominal accuracy of the crystal oscillator is ±20ppm (20 parts per million). Two identical crystal oscillators are installed on adjacent ECUs. Due to the different PCB temperatures (95°C in the engine compartment vs. 25°C in the cockpit), the slight difference in load capacitance (22pF vs 22.5pF), and the different aging rates, the actual frequency difference may be ±30ppm.

what does that mean? After 1 second, the clock deviation of the two ECUs is about 30 microseconds. After 1 minute, it's close to 2 milliseconds. 1 hour later, 0.1 seconds.

The 60-2 teeth of the crankshaft generate 58 tooth pulses per revolution; the tooth pulse interval at 6000RPM is about 170 microseconds. If the time deviation between the injection ECU and the ignition ECU exceeds 100 microseconds, the spark plug will ignite at the exhaust stroke instead of at the top of the compression stroke, causing cylinder misfire. For ADAS sensor fusion, the forward-looking camera captures the pedestrian at the T_camera moment, and the millimeter-wave radar scans the target at the T_radar moment. If the two timestamps are not synchronized to the microsecond level, the fusion algorithm will get the wrong correlation.

At a vehicle speed of 100km/h (≈28m/s), a time deviation of 1 microsecond ≈ a position deviation of 28 microns, which can be ignored. A time deviation of 1 millisecond ≈ a position deviation of 28 millimeters. 28mm itself is not big. But the problem is not the absolute position, but the target association. The forward-looking camera and angle radar are each time stamped. If the two sets of time axes differ by 1 millisecond, the same pedestrian will be offset by 28 millimeters in the two sets of data. When the fusion algorithm faces two "targets with a difference of 28 mm", it may judge them as two different obstacles, or worse, mismatch the target tracked by the A sensor with the target of the B sensor. Once the association is wrong, subsequent tracking, trajectory prediction, and collision time calculation are all based on the wrong premise.

**The bottom-level consensus of a distributed system is not data, but time. ** Before all ECUs can reach a consensus on "the master cylinder pressure is 30bar", they must first reach a consensus on "what time is it now?"

## The magic of four timestamps

The core concept of PTP (Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588) can be explained in one paragraph:

The master clock (Grandmaster) periodically sends Sync messages. At the moment when the first symbol after the SFD of the Sync message leaves the MII interface, the hardware timestamp unit of the master clock captures the current time t1. When Sync is received from the clock, the reception time t2 is captured at the instant when the first symbol enters the MII interface after SFD. The master clock then tells the slave clock t1 in the Follow_Up message (or carries t1 in the originTimestamp field of the Sync message itself, which is called one-step mode).

The slave clock also actively sends a Delay_Req message to the master clock, capturing the sending time t3. After the master clock receives it, it captures the reception time t4 and sends t4 back to the slave clock in the Delay_Resp message.

```
master clock slave clock
  |                                       |
  |------- Sync (t1 capture) --------------->| (t2 capture)
  |------- Follow_Up (carry t1) ---------->|
  |                                       |
  |<------ Delay_Req (t4 capture) ----------| (t3 capture)
  |------- Delay_Resp (carry t4) --------->|
   |                                       |
```

These four timestamps are not "the time a certain software read at a certain moment." t1 is the value latched by the D flip-flop of the main clock's PHC at the moment the first symbol leaves the MII interface after the SFD of the Sync message, accurate to nanoseconds. t2 is the value that the PHC of the slave clock is latched in the same way at the moment when the first symbol after the SFD of the Sync message arrives at the MII interface. Two hardware snapshots, captured by two independent free-running counters at both endpoints of the same Ethernet frame. The difference between them (t2-t1) contains two pieces of information: the offset between the master and slave clocks, and the network transmission delay. The calculation formula of PTP is to separate these two pieces of information.

Assuming a symmetric round trip path (upstream delay = downstream delay), calculated from the clock:

```
mean_path_delay = [(t2 - t1) + (t4 - t3)] / 2
offset_from_master = (t2 - t1) - mean_path_delay
                   = [(t2 - t1) - (t4 - t3)] / 2
                   = (t2 - t1 - t4 + t3) / 2
```

Simplified classic formula:

```
offset = (t2 - t1 - t4 + t3) / 2
delay  = [(t2 - t1) + (t4 - t3)] / 2
```

The slave clock subtracts `offset` from its current clock value to align it with the master clock.**Four timestamps. Two sets of messages (Sync+Follow_Up counts as one interaction, Delay_Req+Delay_Resp counts as one interaction). One assumption (path symmetry). ** This is the entire mathematical core of PTP. The remaining protocol specifications: BMCA election master clock, port state machine, TLV extension, and profile definition are all engineering supports built around these four sentences.

Path asymmetry is the largest source of error in PTP. If the uplink delay = 1 μs and the downlink delay = 2 μs (for example, because the uplink fiber wavelength and the downlink fiber wavelength are different and the dispersion is different), then the offset calculation will introduce a system deviation of 0.5 μs. The PTP protocol itself cannot detect path asymmetry, it can only believe that the physical layer is (approximately) symmetric. For automotive Ethernet, the network segment is usually within 15m, the delay is on the order of tens of nanoseconds, and the path asymmetry is on the order of sub-microseconds, which is basically acceptable.

## PI servo: from "pair once" to "continuous pair"

After the offset is calculated, do you directly set the slave clock back to the offset? Too rough. If your clock is the time base of the engine ECU, a sudden time jump will cause the timer output to jump, the injection time to be misaligned, and the CAN message cycle to be messed up. What you need is "smooth correction", not "jump".

There is a PI servo controller (Proportional-Integral Servo) inside the PTP slave clock. Its input is the offset calculated for each sync cycle (usually 0.125 seconds, or 8Hz). The output is the frequency correction value: the voltage-controlled oscillator applied to the local clock (or in a software implementation, a multiplier factor applied to the clock rate).

Two components of the PI controller:

**Proportional term (P_term)**: Proportional to the current offset. The larger the offset, the stronger the correction. The P term is responsible for fast convergence, which allows the slave clock to "quickly move closer" to the master clock.

**Integral term (I_term)**: Proportional to the historical cumulative sum of offset. If offset is always positive (the slave clock is faster than the master clock), I_term will accumulate a negative frequency offset, making the slave clock permanently slower. The I term is responsible for eliminating the steady-state deviation, it allows the frequency of the slave clock to be "precisely synchronized" with the master clock.

Governing equation of PI (discrete form):

```
P_term = Kp × offset
I_sum  = I_sum + Ki × offset
freq_correction = P_term + I_sum
```

Among them, Kp and Ki are gain coefficients, which need to be debugged according to actual network conditions. Kp is too large → overshoot and oscillation. Ki is too large → the integral effect is too strong and overshoot/oscillation will occur; Ki is too small → the steady-state error converges slowly. The optimal values ​​of Kp and Ki depend on the length of the sync cycle, the jitter characteristics of the network, and the stability of the crystal oscillator.

Let’s take a pair of commonly used parameter values (software timestamp: Kp=0.1, Ki=0.001; the hardware timestamp gain of linuxptp is Kp=0.7, Ki=0.3, because the hardware timestamp jitter is small, a hotter gain can be used) to illustrate:

What does Kp=0.1 mean? If the current offset is 1 microsecond, the proportional term gives a frequency correction of 0.1 microsecond; spread over a sync period of 125 milliseconds, the frequency adjustment is about 0.8ppm. The direction is opposite when the offset is negative (the slave clock is faster than the master clock). It's "proportion", immediate response, no delay.

What does Ki=0.001 mean? For each sync cycle that offset lasts, the integral term only accumulates for 1 nanosecond; it slowly eliminates the steady-state frequency difference. Just like when you are driving and find that the car keeps turning to the right, Kp means that you manually turn the steering wheel (one-time), and Ki means that you continue to turn the steering wheel to the left after you find that it has turned to the right (continuous force).

**PI controller is one of the simplest and most extensive applications of automatic control theory**. It does not need to know the precise model of the controlled object and does not require complex system identification. Only two parameters and a feedback loop are needed to achieve continuous convergence of the slave clock. From automatic thermostats to car cruise control to PTP clock synchronization, PI controllers are everywhere.

## Penetration: A simplified PTP servo loop

Below is a simplified to the extreme PTP slave clock servo code. It does not rely on any real PHY hardware timestamp, it just uses software to simulate the offset calculation process, but its PI logic is completely consistent with the real PTP slave clock servo loop:

```c
// ========== Simplified version of PTP slave clock servo ==========
#include <stdint.h>
#include <math.h>

typedef struct {
    int64_t  local_time;
    double   freq_ratio;
    double   Kp, Ki;
    double   I_sum;
    int64_t  last_sync_time;
} ptp_slave_t;

void ptp_slave_init(ptp_slave_t *s)
{
    s->local_time = 0;
    s->freq_ratio = 1.0;
    s->Kp    = 0.1;      // Software timestamp gain, corresponding to linuxptp SWTS_KP_SCALE
    s->Ki    = 0.001;    // Software timestamp gain, corresponding to linuxptp SWTS_KI_SCALE
    s->I_sum = 0.0;
    s->last_sync_time = 0;
}
```

```c
// Called every time after receiving Sync+Follow_Up+Delay_Resp
// All timestamp units: nanoseconds (ns)
void ptp_slave_update(ptp_slave_t *s,
                       int64_t t1, int64_t t2,
                       int64_t t3, int64_t t4)
{
    int64_t t2_minus_t1 = t2 - t1;
    int64_t t4_minus_t3 = t4 - t3;

    int64_t offset = (t2_minus_t1 - t4_minus_t3) / 2;
    int64_t delay  = (t2_minus_t1 + t4_minus_t3) / 2;
```Each of these four timestamps: t1, t2, t3, t4 is an 80-bit PTP Timestamp. In hardware, when the clock captures the transition of the RX_DV signal on the MII interface, a set of D flip-flops (the circuit discussed in Chapter 3.2) instantly latches the current value of the PHC counter. So the t2 you read is not a "time recorded by software", it is a hardware snapshot, accurate to nanoseconds.

```c
    if (offset >  1000000L) offset =  1000000L;
    if (offset < -1000000L) offset = -1000000L;

    double offset_d = (double)offset;
    double P_term = s->Kp * offset_d;
    s->I_sum     += s->Ki * offset_d;

    if (s->I_sum >  100000.0) s->I_sum =  100000.0;
    if (s->I_sum < -100000.0) s->I_sum = -100000.0;

    double freq_correction_ns = P_term + s->I_sum;
    int64_t sync_interval_ns  = t2 - s->last_sync_time;
    if (sync_interval_ns <= 0) sync_interval_ns = 125000000L;

    double freq_ratio_adjust = freq_correction_ns / (double)sync_interval_ns;

    s->freq_ratio -= freq_ratio_adjust;

    if (s->freq_ratio > 1.000200) s->freq_ratio = 1.000200;
    if (s->freq_ratio < 0.999800) s->freq_ratio = 0.999800;

    s->last_sync_time = t2;
}
```

`offset = (t2-t1 - t4+t3) / 2` This formula has an implicit assumption: network delay is symmetrical. But in reality, upstream and downstream latencies can differ by a few microseconds because the switch's queue depth can be different in both directions. This is why gPTP (IEEE 802.1AS) requires peer-to-peer delay measurements, since link-by-link measurements limit the effects of path asymmetry to a single link.

**Although this code is simplified, the core logic of the PI servo: offset calculation, P_term, I_sum accumulation, freq_ratio correction, is completely consistent with the ptp_lite source code (see the companion article ["PTP Technical Book"] (https://github.com/Lularible/ptp-book)). ** The production code will also add: filtering (median filtering or Kalman filtering to remove network jitter spikes), state machine (LISTENING→UNCALIBRATED→SLAVE→MASTER), master clock switching processing involving BMCA, and delay compensation for hardware timestamps.

## Why hardware timestamps are necessary

Software timestamp: You call `clock_gettime()` in the driver's receive interrupt to "record" the time when the message arrives. The problem is that from the time when the preamble of the message reaches the PHY → the PHY parses the frame → DMA moves the frame to the memory → the CPU responds to the interrupt → the interrupt delay (depends on whether the currently executed instruction is a long delay instruction such as division and interrupt priority) → your ISR is executed to `clock_gettime()`, during which an uncertain delay of several microseconds passes. Interrupt nesting, cache misses, bus contention, each cause timestamps to drift.

The target accuracy of PTP is nanosecond level. Nanoseconds mean that you need to know the precise moment after the SFD of the frame when the first symbol reaches the MII interface pin.

Modern automotive Ethernet MAC/PHYs (some PHY models, or MAC timestamp unit within the SoC) have built-in hardware timestamp units. When a valid start of frame delimiter (SFD) is detected on the rising edge of MII's RX_CLK, the timestamp unit immediately latches the current value of the hardware counter. This counter is driven by a local clock source and is not affected by CPU load, interrupt latency, or bus contention. The timestamp is written to the timestamp register of the MAC/PHY and the driver reads it out through the register interface (MDIO/SPI or memory map).

Uncertainty in hardware timestamps: tens of nanoseconds (mainly caused by PLL jitter and signal propagation delays inside the PHY). Uncertainty in software timestamps: tens of microseconds. **A thousand times worse. **

This one thousand times is the fundamental reason why PTP can change from "laboratory paper" to "production line deployment". The IEEE 1588 standard was released in 2002. But it was not until 1588v2 (2008) and the large-scale mass production of Ethernet PHY that supports hardware timestamps after 2010 that PTP truly entered the fields of industrial automation, telecommunications, and automobiles.

**Standards are ideas, silicon wafers are physics, both are indispensable. **

PTP's hardware timestamp capture is the direct application of the D flip-flop discussed in Chapter 3.2. The transition of the RX_DV signal on the MII interface is the clock edge, and the current value of the PHC counter is the D input. PTP did not invent new hardware, it just used the most basic circuits in the digital logic class in the right place. The PI controller in the PTP servo shares the same mathematical skeleton as the RTOS task scheduling you will see in Chapter 5.2: Proportional Integral Derivative (PID) control, except that PTP controls the clock frequency, and RTOS controls the CPU occupancy rate.

## BMCA: Let 100 ECUs choose their own "officials"

The time in the PTP network is not configured by humans, but is automatically selected by the BMCA (Best Master Clock Algorithm).The core rule of BMCA is simple: each PTP node compares its own clock quality with the clock quality in the Announce messages received from other nodes. If your clock is better, become the Master yourself. If someone else's is better, become a Slave yourself. If it is as good as the one with smaller ID than clockIdentity (usually MAC address) wins.

"Clock quality" is defined by a data structure: `clockQuality`, including: `clockClass` (clock category, such as "ordinary crystal oscillator", "GPS lock", "atomic clock"), `clockAccuracy` (accuracy, such as "<100ns"、">10μs"), `offsetScaledLogVariance` (statistical stability).

BMCA network effect: Each node continues to periodically send Announce messages (usually 1Hz) to announce its clock quality. In the startup phase, all nodes are Master candidates and send Announces to each other. After a few seconds, the node with the highest quality in the network is recognized by all other nodes, it becomes the Grandmaster, and the other nodes are automatically reduced to Slave. If the Grandmaster fails (Announce times out, usually not received within 3 cycles), its direct downstream will find that the Announce times out, triggering BMCA to rerun, and the second node with the best quality takes over the Grandmaster role.

**The entire process has no central controller, no manual configuration, and is completely self-organized. ** This is the elegance of distributed systems: a single point of failure does not affect the whole. BMCA allows 100 ECUs to "find out" who is the time authority by themselves in a few seconds.

Let’s go to a specific election. There are three candidate master clocks in the network: A (connected to GPS, clockClass=6, priority1=128), B (connected to Beidou, clockClass=6, priority1=100), C (free running, clockClass=52). BMCA comparison in order: priority1 (B’s 100<A's 128→B leads)→clockClass (B=6, C=52→C out)→clockAccuracy→offsetScaledLogVariance→priority2→stepsRemoved (when the same GM)→clockIdentity. In the end, B wins because it has the lowest priority1. Although A has a GPS, its priority1 is not as good as that of B, so he withdraws from the election. C's clockClass is the worst, ranking last.

## gPTP (IEEE 802.1AS): Automotive customized PTP Profile

Universal PTP (IEEE 1588) is designed for industrial automation and telecommunications. It has too many options (two-layer/three-layer, one-step/two-step, unicast/multicast, various profiles) and is too heavy for cars.

IEEE 802.1AS (gPTP: generalized Precision Time Protocol) is a PTP Profile developed by the IEEE 802.1 Audio and Video Bridging (AVB) working group. The first version of 802.1AS-2011 was released in 2011. It is a "lite and enhanced version" of PTP, removing options that the car does not need and enhancing the features that the car does.

The difference between gPTP and standard 1588:

1. **Force layer 2, multicast, one-step or two-step. ** Layer 3 (IP) is not supported because the in-vehicle network does not require IP routing to transmit time synchronization messages. All time synchronization messages are sent via Ethernet multicast.

2. **Force Peer-to-Peer delay measurement. ** The Delay_Req/Delay_Resp mechanism of standard PTP only applies to the end-to-end (E2E) delay between the master clock and the slave clock. gPTP instead uses Pdelay_Req/Pdelay_Resp/Pdelay_Resp_Follow_Up to measure link delay between each pair of adjacent nodes. This is a "hop-by-hop" delay measurement and is more suitable for switched network topologies because the intermediate nodes (switches) of Automotive Ethernet introduce variable delays and E2E measurements cannot isolate the contribution of each link segment.

3. **Grandmaster redundancy. ** For standard PTP, when the Grandmaster fails, BMCA may take several seconds to re-elect. In ADAS scenarios, timestamps of several seconds without fusion are unacceptable. gPTP supports "hot backup" Grandmaster: the main GM and the hot backup GM work at the same time (usually belonging to two gPTP domains). The switching time depends on the implementation. The standard does not specify a fixed upper limit of <100ms.

4. ** Stronger media independence. ** The clock synchronization of 802.1AS is independent of the underlying physical medium. It defines a "media-independent time synchronization service" and runs on IEEE 802 media such as Ethernet (100BASE-T1/1000BASE-T1) and Wi-Fi.

The core philosophy of gPTP designers is: **"Let time synchronization become infrastructure, not a burden for applications."** Application software only needs to call the clock API provided by gPTP. The rest (BMCA, hop-by-hop delay measurement, frequency synchronization, time synchronization) is done automatically by the gPTP protocol stack.

## PTP in the car: three scenes, deeper details

**1. ADAS sensor fusion**

The measurement frames of the forward-looking camera (Mobileye EyeQ4), forward-facing millimeter wave radar (Bosch MRR), four-corner radar, and twelve ultrasonic waves are bound to a common gPTP time base through timestamps. It's not aligned to milliseconds, it's aligned to **microseconds**.

On the highway, at a speed of 100km/h (≈28m/s), the distance between two adjacent vehicles may be shortened by 14 meters in 0.5 seconds. If the target timestamp of the front camera and the target timestamp of the corner radar differ by 10ms, the relative positions of the two vehicles will differ by 28cm. In the fusion algorithm that determines whether to "follow the car", "overtake" or "emergency braking", an error of 28cm may cause the target to fall into the wrong target list. The result: the car made the right reaction at the right moment, but to the wrong obstacle.

The current gPTP synchronization accuracy requirement for ADAS sensors is: <1μs. On a 100BASE-T1 network, this is achievable through hardware timestamping.

**2. Car Audio (AVB/TSN)**

7.1-channel digital audio, each speaker is driven by an independent amplifier ECU. If the playback time difference between the left front channel signal and the right rear channel signal exceeds 10ms, the human ear can perceive a sound image drift (precedence effect / Haas effect) when locating the sound source. IEEE 802.1AS synchronizes the DAC clocks of all audio amplifier ECUs in the vehicle to the same time base, ensuring that "playing now" is physically "playing at the same time".

Specific requirements: sampling rate 48kHz → sampling period ≈ 20.8μs. Time alignment accuracy between 12 audio channels is <5μs.

**3. XCP calibration and data recording**

The calibration engineer debugs the engine map on the bench: ignition advance angle, injection pulse width, and boost pressure. The high-frequency variables inside the ECU (knock intensity, cylinder pressure peak, fuel injection under-injection compensation value) are time-stamped at a rate of 100Hz to 1kHz, and uploaded to the calibration tool through XCP (CAN/Ethernet). If the knock record of ECU_A and the injection correction timestamp of ECU_B are not synchronized, the calibration engineer will see "knock occurred after the injection correction" when analyzing the data. But in fact, it may be that "the fuel injection correction has no time to respond, and knocking occurs." This is a data association error. Map parameters adjusted based on this erroneous data may push the engine away from the optimal operating point.

## No common physical clock - but create a common logical clock

What PTP does is very profound at a philosophical level.

You have a distributed set of ECUs, each with its own crystal (each physically oscillating at a slightly different frequency). This is a physical fact and cannot be changed. You can't make two independent crystal oscillators exactly at the same frequency. Even if they come from adjacent positions on the same wafer, there will be ppm-level differences.

The solution of PTP is not to "eliminate differences", but to create a common logical clock through continuous protocol exchange. 

Each slave clock receives the t1/t2/t3/t4 timestamp at a certain period, calculates the offset, and corrects the frequency of the local clock through the PI servo. Correction is not a one-time operation, but a continuous closed-loop control. The PI servo filters out the noise of network jitter and only tracks the true frequency deviation. The time difference between the master clock and the slave clock is continuously compressed to the nanosecond level. It is not to "adjust them to the same", but to "make them continue to be closer to each other."

**This has the same pattern as the distributed consensus of the blockchain. ** What the blockchain wants is "the same ledger": each node maintains a copy and reaches a common state through constant exchange and verification. What PTP wants is "the same clock": each node maintains a crystal oscillator, and achieves a common time through constant exchange and correction.

Both: reaching a perfect consensus through exchange and correction in an imperfect distributed network. The blockchain consensus is **state**. The PTP consensus is **time**.

The number of automotive ECUs has increased from 20 to 100, and the scale of distributed systems has expanded, but the underlying consensus mechanism has not changed. No matter how many ECUs there are, as long as they periodically exchange timestamps and run PI servo, the entire vehicle can share a timeline. 100 crystal oscillators, one time axis.

## From Huygens to Silicon Wafers

In 1665, Dutch physicist Huygens conducted an experiment.

He hung two pendulum clocks on the same beam. At first, they went their separate ways, like two strangers. But dozens of minutes later, an astonishing phenomenon occurred: the swings of the two clocks were completely synchronized: in opposite directions, but with exactly the same frequency. The extremely weak torsional vibration of the beam, which is invisible to the naked eye, has become a secret "messenger" between them, allowing the two independent oscillators to reach a tacit understanding in silence. This is the first time humans have observed "spontaneous synchronization." Order can come not from external instructions but from the collective behavior of weakly coupled systems.

Hundreds of years later, the PTP protocol running on your ECU is essentially doing the same thing. But instead of using a mechanical crossbar, use four timestamps, a PI controller, hardware timestamps, 100BASE-T1 Ethernet frames. The accuracy also spans nine orders of magnitude from Huygens' second level to nanosecond level.

Huygens saw that the two pendulum clocks would be synchronized, but he would not have known that hundreds of years later, billions of transistor-packaged chips would perfectly reproduce the logic of his beam: feedback, coupling, and convergence in silicon wafers.

Huygens' beam is a mechanical physical coupling, and PTP's Sync message is a logical coupling of electromagnetic waves. But the core math is the same: **Feedback + Convergence = Synchronization. **

When Huygens wrote down his findings in his 1673 book "On the Pendulum," he had no idea that his ideas would be traveling at 100Mbps over automotive Ethernet in the 2020s. And for every line of code you use when adjusting the PI gain in an MCU today, engineers a few hundred years from now may look back and be as surprised as you were when you looked at Huygens.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: **Answer "what is simultaneous"? Create a common logical clock through protocol exchange between a group of ECUs that each have independent crystal oscillators. **

Key takeaways:
1. **PTP does not eliminate physical differences, but creates a common logical clock by continuously exchanging timestamps**: four timestamps t1/t2/t3/t4, PI servo loop continuously corrects the local clock frequency, 100 crystal oscillators, and one time axis.
2. **Hardware timestamp is the key to accuracy**: Software timestamp is affected by interrupt delay and protocol stack jitter, and the accuracy is at the microsecond level. The hardware timestamp is marked at the MAC layer, with an accuracy of up to nanoseconds, spanning 3 orders of magnitude.
3. **The core mathematics of PTP is the same as Huygens’ pendulum clock synchronization**: feedback + convergence = synchronization. From mechanical beams in 1665 to 100BASE-T1 Ethernet frames in the 2020s, the ideas remain the same, but the media has changed.
