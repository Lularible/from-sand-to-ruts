# 1.2 The Dream and Collapse of Computation

## Leibniz’s Wild Dreams

In the last years of the 17th century, a German philosopher sat in his study, lost in thought in front of a table full of manuscripts.

His name is Leibniz.

What he was thinking about was this: Can human knowledge be "calculated" like mathematics?

Specifically: If all concepts can be encoded into symbols and all reasoning rules can be written into formulas, then when two people have different opinions, they only need to sit down and say **"Let's do the math"** to figure out who is right and who is wrong.

He called these two things Characteristica Universalis and Calculus Ratiocinator. He hoped to create a formal symbolic language to express all concepts and define a set of mechanical reasoning rules to process these symbols. Any truth can be proven through purely formal manipulation of symbols.

You are not "thinking", you are "calculating".

Leibniz even imagined a "logical calculator": spheres engraved with symbols and connected by pipes and rails. But 17th-century technology was completely unachievable. At that time, he only had brass gears and stepped drums, not even a stable power source.

But please note: **Leibniz's dream already contained all the core elements of a universal ideogram. ** Symbol = word of language. Rules of inference = the grammar of a language. Compute = operate on symbols under rules. This framework: syntax + semantics + reasoning later became the skeleton of the entire modern mathematical logic. Gödel's incompleteness theorem is a self-referential construction completed in this framework, and the Turing machine is also a computing model defined in this framework. **Leibniz was the Lincoln of computer science. He didn't see the end, but he drew the map. **

## Boolean: turning logic into algebra

More than a hundred years after Leibniz, the mathematical world was slowly advancing on this road. The first real breakthrough came from an unexpected person.

George Boole's father was a shoemaker, but he was extremely obsessed with science (he made his own telescopes, microscopes, and coronagraphs at home). Boole did not go to university and by the age of 16 he had mastered Latin, Greek, French, German and Italian by self-taught. At the age of 16, he worked as a teaching assistant in a primary school. At the age of 19, he opened his own primary school and supported his family by teaching. **He studies mathematics in his spare time,** and it's not an easy study. He deduced the invariant theory on his own without a mentor, a university library, or a peer, for which he won the Royal Medal of the Royal Society.

In 1847, at the age of 32, Boole published a slim pamphlet, The Mathematical Analysis of Logic. He put forward an extremely bold point of view in the book: **Logical propositions are not abstract speculations in philosophy, they are mathematical objects that can be operated with algebraic rules. **

AND, OR, NOT are no longer colloquial terms for “and”, “or” and “not”. They are operations.

```
Let A = "It's sunny today"
Let B = "I'm going for a walk"

Then "A AND B" = "It's sunny today and I'm going for a walk" → A × B
     "A OR B" = "It's sunny today or I'm going for a walk" → A + B
     "NOT A" = "It's not a sunny day" → 1 - A

true = 1, false = 0
```

In this system, A + A = A (repeating the same proposition does not add information), A × A = A (the same proposition is the same if it is said twice), A × (1 - A) = 0 (a proposition cannot be true and false at the same time, this is the law of contradiction).

In 1854, he expanded this system into the most important work of his life, The Laws of Thought. The subtitle is "On which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities". He wanted to prove that thinking is essentially mathematical operations, and logical reasoning can be reduced to algebraic calculations.But Boole's book was virtually ignored at the time. Logicians think he is too mathematical, mathematicians think he is too philosophical. Bull died at the age of 49. He walked two miles to a college class on a rainy day, got soaked to the skin, developed a high fever, and died of pneumonia. His widow, believing in folk remedies to "treat the cause in the same way", hastened Boole's death by laying him on wet sheets. **During Boole's lifetime, few understood the value of his work. **

This value waited more than 80 years to be rediscovered.

In 1937, MIT master's student Claude Shannon wrote a master's thesis "A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits." Shannon pointed out: **The opening/closing of the relay, the on/off of the switch, the high level/low level of the circuit, all these binary physical states can be perfectly mapped to TRUE/FALSE of Boolean algebra. ** AND gate = series switch, OR gate = parallel switch, NOT gate = normally closed contact.

This master's thesis is recognized as the most important master's thesis in the 20th century. It laid the foundation for the discipline of digital circuit design. From now on, when you design digital circuits, you are not drawing "wires and relays", you are doing Boolean algebra. And Boole, the poor shoemaker's son, the country teacher who secretly worked out logical equations in his primary school classroom, had already written the grammar 80 years ago.

The reason why you can use transistors to build logic gates, the reason why you can write `if (a && b)` in C code, and the reason why you can deduce program behavior by looking at the high and low levels on the logic analyzer is all because a self-taught Irish mathematics teacher invented Boolean algebra in the mid-19th century, and in the mid-20th century there was an MIT graduate student who mapped Boolean algebra to circuits.

**Thinking → Algebra → Circuit. The road to materialization of thoughts takes a hundred years. **

## Frege: The first foundation of logic was destroyed by an earthquake

Boolean's system is very powerful, but it has a fatal flaw: it can only handle the "relationship between propositions" and cannot handle the **logical structure within the proposition**. The proposition "all people are mortal" can only be represented by one variable A in Boolean algebra. But what is the intrinsic connection between "all men are mortal" and "Socrates is a human → Socrates is mortal"? Boolean algebra can't see it. It treats propositions as atoms (indivisible basic units).

This limitation was broken by Gottlob Frege.

Frege was a withdrawn German mathematics professor who taught at the University of Jena and remained virtually unknown throughout his life. He almost single-handedly invented **modern predicate logic** with the publication of a book called Begriffsschrift ("Notional Words") in 1879.

Predicate logic has one more thing than propositional logic: it can break propositions apart. No more “P means everyone dies.” Instead:

```
∀x (Human(x) → Mortal(x))
```

“For all x, if x is a person, then x is mortal.”

Frege invented the quantifiers: ∀ (for all) and ∃ (for existence). He invented strict rules of inference that defined what kinds of symbolic transformations were "legal proofs" and what kinds were not. **Leibniz's "universal ideogram" had its first complete, precise, and operational implementation in Frege's Begriffsschrift. **

Frege spent the next 20 years trying to use this logical system to lay the foundation for the entire arithmetic. His plan was to start from pure logic and derive the definitions of natural numbers, addition, and multiplication, and ultimately prove that all mathematical truths can be reduced to logical truths. He wrote "The Fundamental Laws of Arithmetic" for this purpose, the first volume of which was published in 1893, and the second volume is about to be printed in 1902.

Then he received a letter.

The sender is the young British man Bertrand Russell. The letter contained only two pages, but it contained a sentence that completely shattered the edifice of logic that Frege had spent 20 years building.

**"Please consider the set of all 'sets that do not contain themselves'. Does this set contain itself?"**

This is **Russell's Paradox**:```
Let R = { x | x ∉ x }, that is: R is the set of all "sets not including itself".
Question: R ∈ R?

If R ∈ R, then by definition, R does not contain itself → R ∉ R, a contradiction.
If R ∉ R, then by definition R should be contained in R → R ∈ R, a contradiction.
```

That is to say: **There is a set that can be legally defined using Frege's system. But its very existence leads to contradictions. ** Frege's system is inconsistent.

Frege's response was heartbreaking. He wrote in the postscript to the second volume of "The Fundamental Laws of Arithmetic": "The last thing a scientist wants to see is to find that his foundation has collapsed after completing his work. And my situation is even more embarrassing, because not only did my foundation collapse, but it was pointed out by Mr. Bertrand Russell before I was about to go to print."

Frege never published another important work on logic. He spent the rest of his life in isolation and depression. Nine years later, the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein visited Frege, who persuaded Wittgenstein to study with Russell at Cambridge. ** His own system was destroyed by Russell, but Frege still sent his children to study with the destroyer because he said that Russell was the only real genius in this field. **

This magnanimity is as great as his tragedy.

## Russell and Whitehead: An Impossible Project

Russell's paradox made the mathematical community realize that set theory cannot be "defined however you want". You need to strictly limit how sets are constructed to prevent self-referential paradoxes.

Russell's own solution is Type Theory: the "type level" of a set must be higher than the type level of its elements, and self-referential constructions such as "containing itself" are prohibited. He outlined the basic ideas of type theory in The Principles of Mathematics, published in 1903, and then co-authored the three-volume Principia Mathematica with his teacher Alfred North Whitehead between 1910 and 1913.

This set of books is the most ambitious formal project in human history. Russell and Whitehead's goal was to rigorously derive all known mathematical theorems step by step from a set of the most basic logical axioms. There are no leaps, no intuitions, no "obviousnesses," every step must follow strictly the rules of logic.

How crazy is this book?

- It took the first 379 pages of Volume 1 to finally prove **1 + 1 = 2**.
- When the proof finally arrived, the author wrote a dry remark: "The above proposition is occasionally useful."
- The book is about 2,000 pages long, uses hundreds of specially designed symbols (many invented by Russell himself), and was typesetting at a cost so high that Cambridge University Press refused to bear it.
- Russell and Whitehead each financed publication with £50 of their own money. Russell later laughed at himself: "This is the only mathematical classic in human history that has a net loss."

Principia Mathematica demonstrates the feasibility of formalization, at least in part of mathematics. But it also exposes the hellish difficulty of formalization: it takes you 379 pages to even figure out 1+1=2, so what about advanced mathematics? **Russell proved that he was right, but he also proved that this road would not work in practice. **

However, Principia Mathematica was an unexpected success in at least one respect: it laid the foundation for type theory, which a century later became the core theoretical foundation of programming language type systems. You write `fn foo(x: &T) -> &T` in Rust and use template type deduction in C++. This mechanism of "rejecting compilation if the type does not match" was invented by Russell to prevent the paradox of "a set contains itself". **The protective wall of the self-referential paradox became the type checker in the compiler a hundred years later. **

## Hilbert: Must know, must know

David Hilbert was the greatest mathematician of the early 20th century, not just because of his personal achievements, but because he defined the agenda of the entire mathematical community.In 1900, at the age of 38, Hilbert delivered a historic lecture at the Second International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris. He listed **23 unsolved mathematical problems**, ranging from the "continuum hypothesis" to the "general theory of the calculus of variations", covering almost all frontiers of mathematics at the time. These 23 problems defined the research direction of mathematics in the 20th century, and almost all those who solved some of these problems won the Fields Medal or equivalent honors. **Hilbert was not asking questions, he was handing out test papers to the 20th century mathematics community. **

Of the 23 questions, the second one is particularly critical: **"Prove the compatibility of the axioms of arithmetic."** In simple terms: Can you prove within mathematics that "mathematics does not produce contradictions"?

Hilbert's answer to this question is: It can, and it must be possible. He proposed a comprehensive program in the 1920s that became known as Hilbert's Program:

1. Formalize all mathematics: turn mathematical language into a strictly defined symbol system, where axioms are symbol strings and inference rules are symbol transformation rules.
2. Prove that this formal system is complete: all true propositions can be proven.
3. Show that this formal system is consistent: it is impossible to prove both P and NOT P at the same time.
4. Prove that this formal system is decidable: there is a mechanical program that can determine whether any proposition is a theorem.

The first three are the "ultimate foundations" of mathematics, and the fourth, **decidability**, is directly related to calculations. Hilbert asked: Is there a mechanical algorithm that can automatically determine whether any mathematical proposition is true? **

This problem later had a German name: **Entscheidungsproblem** (decision problem).

In his retirement speech in 1930, Hilbert said the words that are engraved on his tombstone:

> *"Wir müssen wissen, wir werden wissen."*
> *"We must know, we will know."*

When he said this, his voice was loud and confident. The audience applauded.

**What he didn't know was that this dream had been shattered that same fall, at another meeting just a few days before. **

## Gödel: One sentence breaks everything

September 1930, Königsberg, Germany. Hilbert had just delivered his famous retirement speech during the Second Conference on the Epistemology of Exact Sciences. During another roundtable discussion at the same conference, a thin 24-year-old stood up and said in a barely audible voice:

"In formal systems involving arithmetic, there are undecidable propositions."

The audience was quiet for a while. Von Neumann sat in the front row and, according to those who were present, was the only person who immediately understood the full meaning of these words. His face turned pale.

The young man who spoke was Kurt Gödel.

A year later, in 1931, Gödel published a complete proof paper "On the Formal Undecidable Propositions of "Mathematical Principles" and Related Systems" (Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme). This is **Gödel's Incompleteness Theorem**.

The essence of the theorem can actually be summarized as:

**In any consistent axiomatic formal system that is powerful enough to represent basic arithmetic, there must be a proposition that can neither be proven nor falsified. **

How did Gödel do it? He used an extremely subtle method: **Gödel coding** (Gödel numbering).

Didn't you say "all mathematics can be represented by strings of symbols"? good. Then I assign each symbol a unique natural number:

```
symbol encoding
¬      1
∨      2
∀      3
=      4
0      5
S (successor) 6
(      7
)      8
x1     9
x2     10
...
```Then, **encode the formula into a number**. For example, in the formula "(x1 = 0)", replace each symbol with a code and obtain the sequence [7, 9, 4, 5, 8]. These codes are then multiplied as prime exponents: 2⁷ × 3⁹ × 5⁴ × 7⁵ × 11⁸. This yields a huge natural number,** but it's unique. ** A formula corresponds to a unique number, and the original formula can be uniquely derived from a number.

Similarly, ** proofs can also be encoded in the same way. ** A proof is a sequence of formulas, each formula is encoded as a number, and the entire sequence of proofs can be encoded as a larger number.

In this way, the arithmetic system has an extremely precious property: **"The proposition P is provable", this metamathematical statement, can be encoded into an arithmetic proposition within the system. ** That is, the system can "talk about itself".

Gödel then constructed a proposition G which said:

> **"G is not provable in this system."**

Note the self-referential structure here: G is talking about G himself. Like the Liar's Paradox "This statement is false." But Gödel wrote it out in a rigorous mathematical form, and he used the formal symbolic language requested by Hilbert himself!

Now look at the consequences:

- If G is **provable**, then G is true (G says "G is not provable", since it is provable, G is false), and the system proves the false proposition → **system is inconsistent**.
- If G is **unprovable**, then G is true (because G says "G is not provable", which happens to be true), but the true proposition is not provable → **The system is incomplete**.

So: **No consistent system is complete. **Hilbert's dream is mathematically unattainable.

## Code demonstration of formal system

Let's contextualize Gödel's ideas. Instead of using complete Peano arithmetic (too complicated), we use an extremely simplified formal system to feel how "self-reference" comes in:

```
Formal system MiniMath:

Vocabulary: 0, S, =, (, )

Axiom 1: ∀x (S(x) ≠ 0) "The successor of nothing is not 0"
Inference rule 1: S(A) can be deduced from A

We want to "talk" about the system itself. First code:
symbol encoding
0     1
S     2
=     3
(     5
)     7
x     11

Then the formula "S(0) = S(S(0))" is encoded as:
Symbol sequence: [S, (, 0, ), =, S, (, S, (, 0, ), )]
Coding sequence: [2, 5, 1, 7, 3, 2, 5, 2, 5, 1, 7, 7]
Gödel number: 2^2 × 3^5 × 5^1 × 7^7 × 11^3 × 13^2 × 17^5
            × 19^2 × 23^5 × 29^1 × 31^7 × 37^7
          ≈ 2.3 × 10^53 (a huge number)
```

This number is unique, and every formula has a unique Gödel number. You don't need to "see" the number, you just need to "manipulate" it. A proof (a sequence of formulas) also has a Gödel number. **The relationship "X is the proof of the formula Y" can be expressed as an arithmetic proposition within the system, because "X can be represented by the product of some prime powers" and "Y can be represented by the product of other prime powers" are essentially number theory propositions. **

Gödel's insight is: **If you can talk about "provability" in the system, you can construct a "proposition that says 'I am not provable'" in the system. ** This proposition G exists in the system, can be written out, and is a legal formula. But the system can never prove it, and it can never disprove it. It is the "blind spot" of the formal system, just like the physiological blind spot (optic disc) on your retina that has no photoreceptor cells: **You use it to see the world, but you cannot see itself. **

## ISO 26262: The modern heir to Hilbert’s dream

You may ask: What does this story of mathematics from the 19th century to the early 20th century have to do with the car software I wrote?

The relationship is very direct.

**ISO 26262 is essentially the reappearance of Hilbert's plan in the field of automotive safety, except that the scope has been greatly reduced. **

Hilbert wanted to cover all mathematical truths in a formal system. ISO 26262 wants to use a set of formal processes and methodologies to cover all software defects that may lead to automotive functional safety accidents.

How to cover?

First, **object limitation**. Failure to prove "the arbitrary nature of any program" has been sentenced to death by Rice's theorem. Only the "safety-related nature of this particular ECU software" is demonstrated. Give a specific C file, a specific control flow graph, and a specific security goal, and then analyze exhaustively within this limited scope.Second, **Tool Assistance**. Model checkers (such as BTC EmbeddedValidator) and abstract interpreters (such as Astrée) are complete on specific subproblems. Given a finite state space, all possible execution paths can be mathematically exhausted. Astrée once proved that the flight control software of the Airbus A380 has no runtime errors, which is much more pragmatic than Russell and Whitehead's proof that 1+1=2.

Third, **constrained regression**. If it cannot be proved fully automatically, use constraints to narrow it, prohibit recursion, limit the upper bound of loops, limit the use of pointers, and reduce the dimensionality of the problem to a range that can be eaten by a tool. The rules of MISRA C prohibit dynamic memory allocation, recursion, and function pointers. They are not meant to make you uncomfortable when writing, but to allow static analysis tools to give a definite answer within a limited time.

This is the compromised version of Hilbert’s spirit in engineering: **“What we can prove, we say ‘safe’. What we can’t prove, we use constraints to prevent it from happening.”**

But Gödel's ghost still hovers above. You have more than 100 parameters on the ECU, each parameter will take hundreds of possible values ​​at runtime, and the state space is of the order of 100^100. **You can never exhaust everything. ** You can only sample, you can only analyze the worst path, and you can only add a safety margin. Just like online testing in panel factories: you can't measure the threshold voltage of every TFT on every panel unit. You can do TEG (Test Element Group) testing at a few points on each substrate and use statistics to infer the yield of the entire batch. **All "safety demonstrations" in engineering are essentially statistical compensation for formal incompleteness. **

But the engineering response was also like Hilbert's: not throwing in the towel. Didn't say "forget it". Give you 64KB SRAM and you still write a complete UDS diagnostic stack. Given a 10ms period, you still forward the wheel speed message to all nodes that need it before the deadline. ** Seek no limit by having limit. **

## Why does this matter to you?

You are now debugging an ECU and encounter a seemingly random hardware failure, an occasional bit flip, a race condition with a probability of 0.001%, or a peripheral register exception in a specific temperature zone. You may not be able to fix it, because the underlying physical volatility itself declares that certainty is limited.

**Bit flip in SRAM is not an abstract theory. ** Under the deep sub-micron process, the SRAM cell inside the chip consists of a 6T (6 transistors) structure. Each bit is maintained by two cross-coupled inverters. If an alpha particle (from the decay of trace amounts of uranium/thorium in the packaging material) passes through an SRAM cell, it ionizes atoms in the silicon lattice, creating electron-hole pairs, and these extra charges can instantly flip the state of the inverter. **The physical world itself rolls the dice on the logical world. **

This is precisely the physical counterpart of Gödel's theorem: **At the physical level, certainty is approximate. On a logical level, completeness is impossible. ** On both levels, "ultimate certainty" does not exist.

But in the process of tracking down this fault, you learned about power integrity analysis, learned about soldering oscilloscope probes to chip pins, and learned about interpreting chip factory erratums. **This learning process is the realization of value. **

The story of Gödel and Hilbert is not actually about the success or failure of a mathematical theorem. It is about: **Human beings set a great goal and then, with equally great honesty, prove that this goal is impossible to achieve. **

Hilbert wanted to fit all truth into a formal system. He failed. But this failed process gave birth to the foundation of modern mathematical logic and computer science, as well as the emergence of the generation of geniuses such as Gödel, Church, and Turing.

Hilbert's epitaph said: "We must know, we will know." Gödel's response was: "There are some things we will never know. But this does not prevent us from continuing to ask."

Both are correct.

## Summary

Today we did something: ** tracked a dream that lasted for 250 years, mechanized human reasoning, and then watched it be shattered. **Key takeaways:
1. **Leibniz drew the map, Boole wrote the grammar, and Frege laid the foundation**. Formal thinking is built through generations.
2. **Gödel broke through Hilbert’s dream with a self-referential proposition**: Any sufficiently powerful formal system must have undecidable propositions.
3. **"Undecidable" is not the end point, but the starting point**: ISO 26262, MISRA C, and static analysis are all engineering practices that trade constraints for certainty under this ghost.
