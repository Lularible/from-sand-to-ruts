# 4.3 Board-Level Communication: CAN and CAN-FD

## In 1983, Bosch engineers said “enough is enough”

1983. The wiring harness of the Mercedes-Benz W126 exceeds 50 kilograms.

Engine control, ABS, instrument panel, air conditioning, electric windows, each new electronic function requires a pair of signal lines to be pulled to another ECU. The wiring harness grows like vines in the car body. Bosch engineers made a calculation: According to this trend, by 1990, the wiring harness of luxury cars will consume 15% of the cost of the entire vehicle.

They said: **Enough is enough, we need a bus. **

It's not faster, it's not bigger, it's "enough". A pair of twisted pairs to connect all ECUs. A bandwidth of 1Mbps was enough, because the most complex real-time signals at that time were only a few hundred bytes per second. Node costs must be extremely low because each ECU's MCU has only a few KB of RAM and no room to handle complex protocol stacks.

Their answer is CAN (Controller Area Network), first announced at the SAE conference in 1986. Today, every car on the planet comes from the factory equipped with at least one CAN bus.

Bosch engineers in 1983 handed over a baton. 40 years later, you're still using it. Every line of CAN driver code you write is taking up this stick and continuing to run.

## Explicit performance covers recessiveness - this is the core secret of the physical layer

CAN uses a pair of differential lines: CAN_H and CAN_L. Connect 120Ω terminal resistors at both ends of the bus.

Transmitting a "dominant" bit (logic 0): the transceiver drives CAN_H to approximately 3.5V, CAN_L to approximately 1.5V, and the differential voltage ΔV≈2V. The typical driving capability of the transceiver is the ability to inject at least 1.5V differential voltage into a 60Ω load (two 120Ω terminals in parallel + cable losses), which is equivalent to a drive current of at least 25mA.

Send the "recessive" bit (logic 1): the transceiver releases the bus, and the internal bias network pulls both CAN_H and CAN_L to about 2.5V (VCC/2, usually VCC=5V); two 120Ω terminal resistors in parallel are equivalent to 60Ω, and their main function is to absorb reflections. Differential voltage ΔV≈0V.

**The key is: explicit performance covers implicit performance. ** If there are two nodes transmitting at the same time, one transmitting dominant (0) and the other transmitting recessive (1), the bus must be dominant. This is an inevitable consequence of the laws of physics. The differential driver actively injects current into the 60Ω load, and the current produces a voltage drop across the termination resistor. This is Ohm's law. A weak driver with an implicit bias cannot counter an explicit driver.

This physical characteristic allows CAN to implement **CSMA/CR** (Carrier Sense Multiple Access/Conflict Resolution). Different from Ethernet's CSMA/CD: CAN has no data loss and no backoff delay in case of conflict. The loser of arbitration will automatically quit sending and switch to receiving. Frames are not corrupted. This is the core feature that distinguishes CAN from all other buses. It allows the bus utilization to be close to 100% in theory (in actual engineering, it is usually <60% to achieve acceptable latency).

## Physical intuition of differential signals

Why use differential signaling? Because cars are an electromagnetic hell.

The breakdown voltage of spark plug discharge is 10-30kV, the discharge current can reach ampere level, and the rise time is less than 1ns. For PWM driven motors, di/dt can reach 1A/ns. Large currents create magnetic fields on the body's metal structure, and the flux changes couple to any nearby conductors.

**Single-ended signal (such as SPI's MOSI):** The noise voltage induced by the external magnetic field on the signal line is directly superimposed on the signal. If the noise amplitude exceeds the VIL/VIH threshold, it is likely to cause bit errors.

**Differential Signaling (CAN_H and CAN_L):** External electromagnetic fields induce nearly equal amounts of common-mode noise on both wires. Because the two wires are twisted together, their distance from the noise source is nearly equal at every point in space. A differential receiver only cares about the difference CAN_H - CAN_L because the common mode noise is canceled out. The degree of this cancellation is measured by CMRR (common mode rejection ratio): CMRR of CAN transceiver>60dB (1000 times), that is, 1V common mode noise has only 1mV residual at the differential output end. Automotive-grade CAN transceivers are usually >70dB.

**The twisting frequency of the twisted pair is also carefully designed. **Typical CAN wire harnesses are twisted 2-4 times per inch. If the stranding is too dense, the wire will be too hard and the cost will be high. If the stranding is too sparse, the common mode suppression effect is poor. 2-4 times/inch is the engineering optimum between noise immunity and mechanical flexibility.

**Why is the terminal resistance 120Ω? ** Because the typical CAN twisted pair characteristic impedance is about 120Ω. The value of the terminating resistor must be equal to the characteristic impedance of the cable, otherwise the signal will be reflected when it reaches the end of the cable. The reflected wave is superimposed on the original signal, creating an overshoot or step. CAN arbitration relies on all nodes seeing consistent bus levels within the same bit time. Ringing caused by reflection will destroy the certainty of arbitration. 120Ω is not "specified by a certain standard", it is specified by the physical properties of the cable.**Common Mode Choke. ** In severe EMI environments (such as in the engine compartment), a common mode choke is placed between the CAN transceiver and the bus. It consists of two coils wound on the same magnetic core. The magnetic flux generated by the differential mode signal (CAN_H-CAN_L) cancels each other, and the choke coil presents low impedance. The magnetic flux generated by common mode noise (CAN_H and CAN_L are in the same direction) superimposes on each other, and the choke coil presents high impedance (usually >500Ω in the 1-50MHz range), effectively attenuating common mode noise. This allows CAN to communicate stably in the engine compartment even if it is less than 30cm away from the spark plug.

## ID arbitration: one field, two missions

There is an 11-bit (standard frame) or 29-bit (extended frame) ID field in the CAN frame. It does two things at the same time:

**1. Identify the content of the frame. ** The protocol itself does not specify the meaning of ID, which is customized by the OEM. But industry practice is: each CAN ID corresponds to a specific set of signals. ID 0x3E8 = Engine status frame (contains speed, coolant temperature, throttle position, etc.), ID 0x180 = Wheel speed frame (speed of four wheels).

**2. Arbitration priority. ** During the arbitration phase, all pending nodes push out their IDs at the same time. After each bit is sent, the actual level of the bus is read back immediately. If you send recessive (1) but read back explicit (0), it means that another node is sending an ID with a higher priority (ID is smaller, 0 is preferred to 1), then exit immediately and switch to listening mode.

**Use a specific example. **

Node A wants to send ID=0x3E8 (= 01111101000b), and node B wants to send ID=0x180 (= 00110000000b).

| Bit order | Node A | Node B | Bus actual | Result |
|------|-------|-------|----------|------|
| 10 (the first bit after SOF) | 0 | 0 | 0 | Both bits are 0 (dominant), both continue |
| 9 | 1 | 0 | 0 | A sends 1 (recessive), B sends 0 (dominant) → B's dominant covers A's recessive → the bus is 0. A read back and saw 0, but it sent 1: **A failed the arbitration and exited immediately. ** |
| 8 | None | 1 | 1 | A has exited. B continues to send 1. |
| ... | None | ... | ... | B sends the entire frame completely unhindered. |

The entire process is completed within the first few digits of the ID field. **No frame was damaged**, A just found that it had insufficient priority and took the initiative to give way. B doesn't even know that there is someone competing with him. This is the elegance of CSMA/CR: conflicts are resolved instead of being detected and retransmitted.

The entire process is completed within the ID domain without losing any frames. Extremely deterministic: the worst-case delay of the highest priority frame = frame time + intermission of 3 recessive bits.

**This is CAN’s most genius design. ** The ID field uses the same field to solve the two problems of what data is this and who said it first. There is no central scheduler, no token passing, no master polling, the hardware does everything by itself. This is information theory-level elegance, with one field assuming two orthogonal semantics of addressing and scheduling.

## Penetration: Track an engine speed signal

You see this line in the DBC (CAN database) file provided by the OEM:

```
BO_ 1000 EMS_1: 8 Engine
  SG_ EngineSpeed : 23|16@0+ (1,0) [0|8000] "rpm" IPC
```

Translation: In the frame of CAN ID 0x3E8 (decimal 1000), the start bit is 23, the length is 16 bits, Motorola format (big endian), unsigned, factor 1.0, offset 0. Range 0-8000rpm. Dashboard (IPC) receive.

The following is a complete C function that uses DBC to describe the analysis of CAN signals:

```c
// DBC signal parsing: extract a signed or unsigned integer from the 8 bytes of the CAN frame
// Layout: Intel (little-endian) or Motorola (big-endian)
// start_bit: Motorola is the MSB sawtooth bit number, Intel is the LSB bit number
// length: signal length (bits)
// is_signed: 1 = signed, 0 = unsigned
// is_motorola: 1 = Motorola format, 0 = Intel format

uint64_t can_extract_signal(const uint8_t data[8],
                            uint8_t start_bit, uint8_t length,
                            uint8_t is_signed, uint8_t is_motorola)
{
    uint64_t raw = 0;

    if (is_motorola) {
// Motorola: First convert the sawtooth starting bit to the position in the big-endian bit stream
        uint8_t net = (uint8_t)(8 * (start_bit / 8) + (7 - (start_bit % 8)));
        for (uint8_t i = 0; i < length; i++) {
            uint8_t pos = net + i;
            uint8_t byte_idx = pos / 8;
if (byte_idx >= 8) return 0; // Out-of-bounds protection
            uint8_t bit_in_byte = 7 - (pos % 8);
            raw = (raw << 1) | ((data[byte_idx] >> bit_in_byte) & 1u);
        }
    } else {
// Intel: Starting from LSB, bit number increases
        for (uint8_t i = 0; i < length; i++) {
            uint8_t pos = start_bit + i;
            uint8_t byte_idx = pos / 8;
if (byte_idx >= 8) return 0; // Out-of-bounds protection
            uint8_t bit_in_byte = pos % 8;
            raw |= (uint64_t)((data[byte_idx] >> bit_in_byte) & 1u) << i;
        }
    }

// sign extension (signed signal)
    if (is_signed && (raw & (1ULL << (length - 1)))) {
        uint64_t sign_mask = ~((1ULL << length) - 1);
        raw |= sign_mask;
    }

    return raw;
}

// Usage example: Parse engine speed from CAN frame
void can_rx_callback(uint32_t id, uint8_t *data, uint8_t dlc)
{
    if (id == 0x3e8) {
        // EngineSpeed: start_bit=23, length=16, Motorola, unsigned
        uint64_t raw = can_extract_signal(data, 23, 16, 0, 1);
        float rpm = raw * 1.0f + 0.0f;  // factor=1.0, offset=0.0
        update_tacho_needle(rpm);
    }
}
```

**What’s happening behind this code, where you can’t see it? **

Engine ECU side:

**Application layer:** The EMS control software writes the current speed of 1847 rpm into the CAN sending buffer (actually the Data field of a mailbox).

**CAN controller hardware:** Encode 1847 as 0x0737, fill in data[2]=0x07, data[3]=0x37. Assemble frame header: SOF (1 bit dominant) → ID = 0x3E8 (11 bits) = 01111101000b → IDE = 0 (standard frame) → R0 (reserved bit) → DLC = 8 (4 bits = 1000b) → 8 bytes of data → CRC15 → CRC delimiter → ACK slot (1 bit, the sender sends recessive, the receiver pulls low to indicate receipt) → ACK delimiter → EOF (7 bits recessive). Then Bit Stuffing: If there are 5 consecutive identical bits, an opposite stuffing bit is automatically inserted. The receiver is automatically removed. This is to ensure sufficient edge density for each node's PLL clock recovery to lock.

**Bit timing:** Each bit is divided into 4 periods, Sync segment (fixed 1Tq), Prop segment (compensating bus propagation delay + transceiver delay), Phase Seg1 and Phase Seg2 (used to fine-tune the sampling point position). 1 Tq (Time Quantum) is the clock cycle of the CAN controller. The sampling point is at the junction of Phase Seg1 and Seg2, usually set at 75%-87.5% bit width. This is a trade-off between latency and noise immunity: the further back the sampling point is, the greater the bus delay can be tolerated (long cables); the further forward the sampling point is, the smaller the signal vibration can be tolerated (high-noise environment).

You can understand these four segments intuitively: the Sync segment is when the referee blows the whistle and all nodes start at the same time. The Prop segment is the time for the signal to run. It takes time for the signal to run from one end of the bus to the other. During this time, sampling cannot be done. PS1 and PS2 are sampling windows. Sample the bus level at the end of PS1. If the sampling point is too early, the signal is not stable yet; if it is too late, the next bit has already started. The CAN sampling point is usually set at 75%-87.5%. The optimal range has been verified by a large number of actual vehicles.

**CAN Transceiver:** Converts the single-ended logic (0/3.3V) of the TX pin to a differential drive. Dominant bit (0) → drives CAN_H to about 3.5V and CAN_L to about 1.5V. Recessive bit(1)→release the bus. The main circuit inside the transceiver is a waveform shaper + push-pull output stage (driven by two high-power MOS tubes, CANH and CANL).

**Physical Layer:** Differential voltages propagate across twisted pairs. The propagation speed of signals on twisted pairs is about 0.55c-0.65c (c=speed of light), because in the medium of FR4 PCB or PVC cable, the propagation speed of electromagnetic waves = c/√εr. Typical insulating materials εr≈3-4, so the speed is about 0.5c-0.6c. Stranding slightly increases the electrical length, and the equivalent speed is about 0.55c (about 16.5cm/ns). Based on this calculation, for a 5-meter-long cable, the one-way signal propagation delay is about 30ns. Arbitration requires two-way propagation. One party sends a bit, and the signal is transmitted to the other party. The other party may send it at the same time after sampling. So the worst-case round-trip delay is about 60ns. The CAN bit time must be greater than this round-trip delay, otherwise the arbitration will fail. This is why the maximum bus length of 1Mbps CAN is about 40 meters, while the data segment of 5Mbps CAN-FD can only be implemented on the short bus (<5m).

Your ECU side (in reverse order):

**CAN Transceiver:** Receives differential voltage. The threshold of the internal comparator is usually set at 0.5V-0.9V differential: ΔV>0.9V→dominant (0), ΔV<0.5V→recessive (1). It is converted to single-ended logic to send the RX pin. The transceiver also performs bus fault protection, detects the short circuit of CAN_H and CAN_L to power/ground, and detects whether the dominant bit duration exceeds the limit. **CAN controller hardware:** Receive bit by bit and perform CRC check while receiving. If the CRC is correct, a dominant bit is sent in the ACK slot. Store the complete frame in the hardware RX mailbox and set the receive interrupt flag. When an error is detected, the node sends an error frame (6 consecutive dominant bits) to interrupt the current frame; at the same time, the error counter (TEC and REC) is checked: when TEC or REC exceeds 127, it enters the Error Passive state, and when TEC exceeds 255, it enters the Bus Off state (automatically disconnects from the bus). This is the fault isolation mechanism of CAN. One node cannot bring down the entire bus because it keeps sending errors.

**Interrupt Service Routine:** The NVIC (Nested Vectored Interrupt Controller) pulls the CPU out of the main loop and jumps to the CAN receive ISR. ISR reads the mailbox, gets ID=0x3E8, DLC=8, data[8]={...,0x07,0x37,...}, and then calls your can_rx_callback.

**Your code:** can_extract_signal spells out 0x0737=1847 from data[2] and data[3]. Multiply by factor 1.0, add offset 0.0, and finally update the dashboard pointer.

**Writing 1847 from the EMS software to the movement of the dashboard pointer: passing through the bit timing state machine of the CAN controller, the bit filler, the CRC checker, the differential driver of the transceiver, the electromagnetic field propagated at 0.55c on the twisted pair, the comparator of the transceiver at the other end, the controller's hardware mailbox, the NVIC interrupt routing, and your callback function. Less than 10 milliseconds, ten layers of hardware, and one line of C code. **

## Configuration and sending and receiving of FlexCAN on S32K

The FlexCAN module is used on S32K14x. The following is the complete register-level code to configure CAN communication, send a frame, and receive a frame:

```c
// ========== FlexCAN0 initialization ==========
// S32K144: FlexCAN0 base address = 0x40024000
#define CAN0_BASE  0x40024000

#define CAN_MCR     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x00))  // Module Config
#define CAN_CTRL1   (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x04))  // Control 1
#define CAN_TIMER   (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x08))  // Free Running Timer
#define CAN_RXGMASK (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x10))  // Rx Global Mask
#define CAN_IFLAG1  (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x30))  // Interrupt Flags 1
#define CAN_IMASK1  (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x28))  // Interrupt Mask 1

// Mailbox area: 4 32-bit registers per MB (CS, ID, WORD0, WORD1)
// MB0-MB7 base address = CAN0_BASE + 0x80
#define MB_CS(n)    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x80 + (n)*0x10 + 0x0))
#define MB_ID(n)    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x80 + (n)*0x10 + 0x4))
#define MB_WORD0(n) (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x80 + (n)*0x10 + 0x8))
#define MB_WORD1(n) (*(volatile uint32_t *)(CAN0_BASE + 0x80 + (n)*0x10 + 0xc))

// Bit time configuration in CAN_CTRL1
//Suppose PE clock=48MHz → 1 Tq = 1/48MHz ≈ 20.83ns
// Target 750kbps: Bit time ≈ 1.33μs = 16 Tq @ 12MHz Sclock

void flexcan0_init_750kbps(void)
{
// 1. Enter freeze mode
CAN_MCR |= (1 << 30); // FRZ = 1 (request freeze)
    CAN_MCR |= (1 << 28);    // HALT = 1
while (!(CAN_MCR & (1 << 24))); // Wait for FRZACK = 1 (entered freeze)

// 2. Enable module (exit soft reset)
CAN_MCR &= ~(1 << 31); // Clear MDIS (enable module)
while (CAN_MCR & (1 << 21)); // Wait for LPMACK=0 (exit low power consumption)

// 3. Configuration bit timing
// CTRL1 can only be modified in freeze mode. The bit position of each field varies with FlexCAN version/device.
// Different, when writing, you should look up the table according to the reference manual of the model used; only the field values ​​are listed here:
    //      PRESDIV = 3  → Sclock = 48MHz / (3+1) = 12MHz，Tq = 83.33ns
// PROPSEG = 3 → propagation segment 4 Tq
// PSEG1 = 5 → phase segment 1 6 Tq
// PSEG2 = 4 → phase segment 2 5 Tq
// RJW = 3 → synchronous jump width 4 Tq
// SMP = 0 → single sampling
//Total Tq/bit = 1(Sync) + 4(Prop) + 6(PS1) + 5(PS2) = 16
// Sampling point = (1+4+6)/16 = 68.75%; bit rate = 12MHz / 16 = 750kbps
//The following uses board-level auxiliary functions to assemble according to the bit segments of the reference manual:
    CAN_CTRL1 = flexcan_ctrl1_value(/*PRESDIV*/3, /*PROPSEG*/3,
                                    /*PSEG1*/5, /*PSEG2*/4,
                                    /*RJW*/3, /*SMP*/0);

// 4. Configure MB0 to receive (RX) and MB1 to transmit (TX)
// MB0 receives all IDs (the global mask is cleared first)
CAN_RXGMASK = 0x00000000; // Global mask = 0 (no bits masked)

// MB0: Receive mailbox, CODE=EMPTY(0x4), automatically receive after activation
    MB_CS(0) = 0x04000000;  // CODE=Rx Empty (0x4 << 24)
MB_ID(0) = 0; // ID=0 (will not be filtered by RXGMASK, receive all frames)

// MB1: Send mailbox, CODE=INACTIVE(0x8)
    MB_CS(1) = 0x08000000;  // CODE=Tx Inactive (0x8 << 24)
// SRR=1 (replace the remote request bit, used for standard frames)

// 5. Clear all interrupt flags and enable MB0 receive interrupt
CAN_IFLAG1 = 0xffffffff; // Write 1 to clear all interrupt flags
CAN_IMASK1 = (1 << 0); // Enable MB0 receive interrupt

// 6. Exit freeze mode and enter normal mode
CAN_MCR &= ~(1 << 30); // Clear FRZ
CAN_MCR &= ~(1 << 28); // Clear HALT
while (CAN_MCR & (1 << 24)); // Wait for FRZACK=0

// Wait for the module to be ready
while (CAN_MCR & (1 << 27)); // Wait for NOTRDY=0 (the module exits reset and enters normal mode)
}
```

The PROP_SEG+PHASE_SEG1+PHASE_SEG2 you just calculated eventually becomes a hardware timer chain inside the CAN controller. Each time segment corresponds to a series of triggers, and when the preset TQ number is reached, it switches to the next segment. The bit timing of all nodes on the entire CAN network is added up to determine who can seize the bus at the next bit.

```c
// ========== CAN sends a frame ==========
void can_send_frame(uint32_t id, uint8_t *data, uint8_t dlc)
{
// Wait for MB1 to be free (check that the CODE field is not in TX status)
    while (((MB_CS(1) >> 24) & 0xf) == 0xc);  // CODE=0xc=TX In Progress

// populate ID
MB_ID(1) = (id << 18) & 0x1ffc0000; // Standard frame: ID is placed in bit[28:18]
// (1 << 14); // If extending the frame

// Fill data: FlexCAN’s Byte order is Motorola
    // MB_WORD0 = data[0-3], MB_WORD1 = data[4-7]
    MB_WORD0(1) = (data[0] << 24) | (data[1] << 16)
                | (data[2] << 8)  | data[3];
    MB_WORD1(1) = (data[4] << 24) | (data[5] << 16)
                | (data[6] << 8)  | data[7];

//Set CODE=Tx (0xc) + DLC + data segment length
//DLC is placed in bit[3:0] of CS
    MB_CS(1) = (0xc << 24)         // CODE=TX Once
| (dlc & 0xf) // DLC (data length)
| (0x0 << 16); // Do not use RTR, IDE=0 (standard frame)
}
```

`CAN0->IFLAG1` This register is an interrupt flag. When the CAN controller successfully receives a frame (the CRC is correct and the ACK slot is normal), the hardware automatically sets the corresponding IFLAG bit to 1. When you read this bit, what you read is the level on an AHB bus, which was a voltage jump at the output of the CAN transceiver comparator less than 100 nanoseconds ago.

```c
// ========== CAN receive interrupt processing ==========
// (Enable CAN0_ORed interrupt in NVIC)
void CAN0_ORed_IRQHandler(void)
{
// Check the interrupt flag of MB0
    if (CAN_IFLAG1 & (1 << 0)) {
//Read the received data
        uint8_t data[8];
        uint8_t dlc = MB_CS(0) & 0xf;  // DLC
uint32_t id = (MB_ID(0) >> 18) & 0x7ff; // Standard ID

        data[0] = (MB_WORD0(0) >> 24) & 0xff;
        data[1] = (MB_WORD0(0) >> 16) & 0xff;
        data[2] = (MB_WORD0(0) >> 8)  & 0xff;
        data[3] = MB_WORD0(0) & 0xff;
        data[4] = (MB_WORD1(0) >> 24) & 0xff;
        data[5] = (MB_WORD1(0) >> 16) & 0xff;
        data[6] = (MB_WORD1(0) >> 8)  & 0xff;
        data[7] = MB_WORD1(0) & 0xff;

// Process received frames
        can_rx_callback(id, data, dlc);

//Clear the MB0 interrupt flag and reactivate reception
CAN_IFLAG1 = (1 << 0); // Write 1 to clear
MB_CS(0) = 0x04000000; // Reset to Rx Empty
    }
}
```

**In this FlexCAN driver code, each write operation of `MB_CS(1)` drives the state machine inside the CAN controller to retrieve data from a mailbox, assemble frames, push to the bit engine, drive the transceiver, and generate differential voltage on the twisted pair line. ** And you may only write a `can_send_frame(0x3E8, rpm_data, 8)` in the application layer.

## CAN-FD: same pair of lines, five times the rate, eight times the data

In 2012, Bosch launched CAN-FD. It keeps the twisted pair physical layer unchanged (the same twisted pair; the data segment speed requires a transceiver that supports CAN-FD), but makes two key changes:

**1. Data segment speed change. ** The original rate (such as 500kbps) is still used during the arbitration stage so that all nodes can participate in arbitration. But after the arbitration is over, the sending node unilaterally switches the rate to a higher frequency (such as 5Mbps). Why can data segments be sped up? Because the arbitration phase requires all nodes to monitor every bit on the bus synchronously, the upper limit of the rate is ≈ 1 / bus round-trip propagation delay. However, the data segment is only driven by the sending node, and the receiving node only needs to sample, and does not need to "read back" every bit to determine arbitration, so it can be accelerated.

Switching mechanism: After the arbitration phase, recessive bits are sent in the BRS (Bit Rate Switch) bit. After the CAN-FD controller detects BRS=recessive, it switches the clock divider between the sampling point of the BRS bit and the CRC delimiter. The receiving node also switches at the same moment. The entire process is completed in 1 bit. Completely transparent to the transceiver, all the transceiver sees is a faster differential voltage transition. There is an ESI (Error State Indicator) bit after the BRS, which is used to tell the receiver whether the sending node is currently Error Active or Error Passive.

**2. The data length is extended to 64 bytes. ** Classic CAN maximum 8 bytes. The DLC field is recoded in CAN-FD, and non-linear coding is used when DLC>8 (9→12, 10→16, 11→20, 12→24, 13→32, 14→48, 15→64). The CRC has also been enhanced: 17-bit CRC (data ≤ 16 bytes) or 21-bit CRC (data > 16 bytes), plus 4-bit stuff count and parity check, and the error detection probability is increased from about 4.7×10⁻¹¹ of CAN to about 10⁻²⁷.

Effect: Transmission of 64 bytes is compressed from ~1.3ms (CAN 1Mbps) to ~170μs (CAN-FD 5Mbps data segment). This is a qualitative improvement for OTA diagnosis and firmware flashing.

The data segment rate is increased from 1Mbps to 5Mbps, which means that each bit is compressed from 1μs to 200ns. In these 200ns, the transceiver must complete: drive the differential voltage to a dominant level (>1.5V), allow the signal to propagate to the other end of the bus (~25ns for 5m cable), the receiver's comparator makes a decision, and is ready for the next bit. At 5Mbps, the eye diagram of the signal begins to close. The root cause is that the RC time constant of the physical layer and the slew rate of the transceiver cannot keep up. This is why CAN-FD is only accelerated in the data segment, while the arbitration segment is still used at low speed, because arbitration requires all nodes to see the same level at the same time. There is no arbitration in the acceleration segment, only two nodes are talking.

## Optimal solution for limited resources

Let’s review all the constraints Bosch engineers faced in 1983:- The weight of the wiring harness must be reduced to one tenth → a shared bus must be used.
- Transceiver chip cost must be extremely low (<$1)→ Twisted pair + differential driver, the simplest and most reliable.
- Engine control and ABS require deterministic delays (<10ms worst case) → there must be hardware priority arbitration, no random backoff.
- MCU only has a few KB of RAM and dozens of MHz → IP protocol stack is impossible, and complex state machines are impossible. The CAN controller must be implemented directly into the silicon using a hardware state machine.
- Extremely harsh electromagnetic environment (30kV spark plug discharge) → Differential signal + 120Ω terminal + twisted pair, the physical layer is naturally resistant to common mode interference.

**CAN is the intersection of all these constraints. ** It is not fast (1Mbps), not flexible (no address addressing, no routing), and not versatile (8 bytes of data, 11/29-bit ID). But in the constrained space of the car, it is optimal.

This is not a textbook comparison of the pros and cons of various protocols, this is finding the only solution with limited resources. Bosch engineers did not choose CAN. They derived CAN.

Today, 40 years later, CAN is still alive in every new car. There is a CAN controller welded in each MCU, and there are dozens of frames on each powertrain CAN that are repeatedly broadcast in different time periods. It can run for 20 years without communication failure, and its error rate is so low that it requires special testing tools (such as CANoe's Error Frame Injection function) to artificially create it.

The protocol drawn on paper by Bosch engineers 40 years ago is now running faithfully in every bit in your S32K's FlexCAN Silicon, in your PCB's CAN transceiver, and in the twisted pair of the car body.

**Limited resources + correct design = a car can run for 20 years without any communication problems**. This isn't luck, this is a triumph of engineering.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: **Restore all the constraints that Bosch engineers faced in 1983, and understand how CAN was derived under the constraints. **

Key takeaways:
1. **CAN is the only intersection point in the automotive constraint space**: the weight of the wiring harness must be reduced to one-tenth, the transceiver cost must be <$1, and the delay must be deterministic. Every CAN design decision answers a specific physical and economic constraint.
2. **The arbitration of the ID field is a genius design of the protocol layer**: one field simultaneously determines what data it is and who said it first. Bit-by-bit arbitration completes the priority decision at the physical layer without the need for a central scheduler.
3. **CAN-FD greatly increases the speed while keeping the physical layer unchanged**: the data segment is increased from 1Mbps to 5Mbps (BRS bit trigger), and the data length is extended from 8 bytes to 64 bytes, which is a qualitative improvement for OTA diagnosis and firmware flashing.
