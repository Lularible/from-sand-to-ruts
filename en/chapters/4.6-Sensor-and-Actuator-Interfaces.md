# 4.6 Sensor and Actuator Interfaces

## The "eyes" and "hands and feet" of ECU

Turning over an ECU circuit board, the MCU is in the center, where most engineers' eyes stop. But look further outside.

On the edge of the PCB are rows of terminals. The terminal on the left is connected to the steering wheel angle sensor: a three-wire cable, 5V power supply, analog signal output (0.5-4.5V, corresponding to -780° to +780°), and ground. The terminal on the right is connected to the accelerator pedal position sensor: two three-wire cables, redundancy is a hard requirement for functional safety (ASIL-D). Two independent sensors give the pedal position at the same time, and the main MCU and the monitoring MCU are cross-compared. The lower terminal is connected to the injector solenoid valve. A two-wire cable, 12V battery voltage PWM drive, peak current up to 10A.

**These are the "eyes" and "hands and feet" of the ECU: sensors and actuators. Without them, the MCU is just a silicon chip playing with its registers. **

With them, the ECU is a machine that can sense the environment and act on it. Your code "makes sense" not because it is logically correct, but because it actually injects gasoline into the cylinder, clamps the brake pads to the brake disc, and turns the steering wheel from the return position to the left position.

You are the one standing in the middle of the bridge. You have one hand extended to the physical world: temperature, pressure, rotational speed, voltage. Your other hand reaches out to the digital world: registers, interrupt vectors, CAN IDs, CRC checks. Your code doesn't run in an ideal environment, it's on a bridge, constantly being pulled by the noise, uncertainty, and chaos of the physical world.

## Signal chain (forward): from physical quantity to register value

For a sensor signal to go to your C code, the path it takes is called **signal chain**. Let's track the coolant temperature for this example.

**Physical quantity**: Coolant temperature in the cylinder head water jacket, 85°C.

**Sensor**: NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient) thermistor is embedded in the water jacket wall. Material: sintered metal oxide (oxide of Mn, Co, Ni), β constant ≈ 3900K. The resistance is 10kΩ at 25°C and approximately 1.1kΩ at 85°C. The ECU is connected to the 5V reference voltage through a precision pull-up resistor (1kΩ±1%, 100ppm/°C temperature drift) to form a resistive voltage divider. Voltage across the thermistor = 5V × R_ntc / (1000 + R_ntc). At 85°C: 5V×1100/(1000+1100)=2.62V.

## Why is it 1kΩ? Sensitivity analysis of pull-up resistors

The resistance of NTC is about 1.1kΩ at 85°C (continuing the previous example). If the pull-up resistor is 1kΩ, the divided voltage =5V×1100/(1000+1100)=2.62V. If the pull-up resistor is changed to 10kΩ, the divided voltage =5V×1100/(10000+1100)=0.50V. The voltage of the latter is only about 1/5 of the former, but the quantization noise of the ADC (about 0.8mV LSB) is the same. Therefore, the SNR of the 0.50V signal is much lower than that of the 2.62V signal, causing the temperature reading to be full of noise. Then why don't you choose a smaller pull-up resistor, such as 100Ω? Because 5V÷(100+1100)=4.2mA current. In the normal power environment of the car, this 4.2mA continues to flow through the NTC, which consumes a lot of power (about 21mW) and generates self-heating on the NTC. The NTC itself is heated by Joule heat, and the measured temperature is not the temperature of the coolant. 1kΩ is the result of a trade-off: the voltage signal is large enough (2.62V is much greater than the 0.8mV noise), the power consumption is low enough (about 2.4mA is negligible in an automotive environment), and self-heating is negligible (about 6mW of heat is instantly taken away in the coolant flow).

**Signal Conditioning**. This 2.62V signal needs to undergo three stages of conditioning before entering the ADC.

Pass 1: A passive RC low-pass filter: 1kΩ series resistor + 10µF ceramic capacitor to ground. Cutoff frequency f_c = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π×1000×10⁻⁵) ≈ 15.9Hz. The coolant temperature changes extremely slowly (time constant is tens of seconds), and the high-frequency components are all noise. Most of them come from coupling from adjacent PWM traces and frictional charge induced on the sensor cable by engine vibration. The output of the RC filter goes to the next stage.

The second channel: a voltage follower (buffer) built from an op amp. With a part such as the TLV9002 (automotive grade, input bias current <1 pA, offset voltage <2 mV), it is wired as a follower. Output = input. Its job is not amplification but isolation. The thermistor's resistance is in the kΩ range and has output impedance; if the ADC sampled charge directly from it, the voltage would droop. The follower's input impedance is >100 MΩ, so it draws almost no current from the thermistor. Its output impedance is <1Ω, so it can quickly charge the ADC sampling capacitor. This isolation prevents ADC sampling from disturbing the measured signal itself.

Third channel: A protection circuit: TVS (Transient Voltage Suppression) diode and 10kΩ current limiting resistor at the input of the follower to prevent high current transients near the CPU from inducing high voltage pulses on the sensor cable to breakdown the op amp or MCU pins.

**ADC sampling**. 12-bit Successive Approximation (SAR) ADC inside the MCU. Reference voltage 3.3V (VDDA/VREFH, accuracy ±1%). LSB = 3.3V / 4096 ≈ 0.806mV. But the effective number of bits (ENOB) is usually only about 10 bits because of thermal noise, reference noise, and power supply ripple. The lower 2 bits are noise.

Voltage 2.62V at 85°C. 2.62 / 0.806×10⁻³ ≈ 3251. The ADC conversion result is 3251 (ideal value, actual value fluctuates between 3240-3262).

**Number Crunching**. MCU DMA automatically moves from the ADC data register to the array in SRAM. Your C code reads adc_raw=3251 from SRAM.

**your code**:

```c
float voltage = adc_raw * (3.3f / 4096.0f);          // 2.619V
float resistance_ntc = 1000.0f * voltage / (5.0f - voltage); // 1100Ω
float temperature = ntc_lookup(resistance_ntc);       // Look up table: 1100Ω -> 85°C
```

**NTC table lookup**: The R-T relationship of NTC is exponential: R = R25 × exp[β(1/T - 1/T25)]. Among them, R25=10kΩ, β=3900K, T25=298.15K (25°C). At 85°C (358.15K), the theoretical value R=10k×exp[3900×(1/358.15-1/298.15)]≈1118Ω (take 1100Ω in the table). The code does not calculate the index every time, but presets an R-T table and uses linear interpolation to check the temperature; the example code is 14 points with an interval of 10–25°C. The interpolation error near 85°C is about ±1°C. When encrypted to 1°C, the interval can be ±0.1°C.

**Physical Quantity → Thermistor → Voltage Divider → RC Filter → Voltage Follower → TVS Protection → ADC Sampling → DMA → Your C Code. ** The coolant temperature of 85°C, after 10 physical and digital steps, becomes `float temperature = 85.0f`. Each step of the signal chain introduces errors: nonlinearity of the thermistor (β constant ±1%), temperature drift of the pull-up resistor (100ppm/°C, 165°C → ±1.65% from -40 to 125°C), op amp input bias (±2mV, corresponding to temperature error of ±0.1°C), ADC quantization noise (±0.5LSB RMS → corresponding temperature error is about ±0.04°C), but the final error can be controlled within ±1°C, which is enough to control the start and stop of the cooling fan and thermostat.

## Configure ADC to read the temperature sensor on S32K

> Note: The following register base address and offset are given according to the common mapping of the S32K144 reference manual; different models, packages or SDK versions may be different, and the actual reference manual of the device used shall prevail.

```c
// ========== ADC0 initialization (S32K144) ==========
// ADC0 base address = 0x4003b000
#define ADC0_BASE  0x4003b000

#define ADC_SC1A    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x00))  // Status/Control 1A
#define ADC_CFG1    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x10))  // Config 1
#define ADC_CFG2    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x14))  // Config 2
#define ADC_RA      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x18))  // Data Result A
#define ADC_SC2     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x2c))  // Status/Control 2
#define ADC_SC3     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x30))  // Status/Control 3
#define ADC_SC1B    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x04))  // Status/Control 1B
#define ADC_RB      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(ADC0_BASE + 0x1c))  // Data Result B

// ADC -> C (temperature sensor) mapping parameters
#define R_PULLUP 1000.0f // Pull-up resistor 1kΩ
#define V_REF 3.3f // ADC reference voltage 3.3V
#define V_SUPPLY 5.0f // Sensor supply voltage 5V
#define ADC_BITS 12 // ADC resolution

void adc0_init(void)
{
    // Enable ADC0 clock in PCC (omitted)

    // 1. Configure ADC clock and resolution
    //    CFG1: ADIV=2(÷4), MODE=01(12-bit), ADICLK=00(bus clock)
    //    Assume Bus clock=48MHz → ADC clk=48/4=12MHz
    //    (S32K ADC requires <16MHz)
    ADC_CFG1 = (2 << 0)   // ADIV = ÷4 (ADC clock = 48/4 = 12MHz)
             | (1 << 2)   // MODE = 12-bit single-ended conversion
             | (0 << 6);  // ADICLK = Bus Clock

    // 2. Sampling time
    //    CFG2: SMPLTS=20 → sampling time = (20+1) × ADCK period
    //    12MHz → 1.75μs sampling time (enough to charge the sampling capacitor to 99.9%)
    ADC_CFG2 = (20 << 0);

    // 3. Hardware averaging (optional, reduces noise, improves effective resolution)
    //    SC3: AVGE=1 (enable averaging), AVGS=11 (32 times average)
    //    32 times average → effective resolution increased by log₂(√32) ≈ 2.5 bits
    ADC_SC3 = (1 << 2)   // AVGE = 1 (hardware averaging enabled)
            | (3 << 0);  // AVGS = average of 32 times
}

// ========== Read ADC channel (single conversion, software trigger) ==========
// channel: 0-31 (ADC channel number of S32K)
// Return: 12-bit ADC result (0-4095)
uint16_t adc_read_channel(uint8_t channel)
{
    // 1. Select channel and start conversion
    //    SC1A: ADCH=channel, AIEN=0 (no interrupt)
    ADC_SC1A = ((uint32_t)channel & 0x1f) << 0;

    // 2. Wait for the conversion to complete (COCO=Conversion Complete)
    while (!(ADC_SC1A & (1 << 7)));  // Wait for COCO=1

    // 3. Read the result (12-bit right-justified)
    //    RA: D[11:0] = conversion result
    return (uint16_t)(ADC_RA & 0xfff);
}

// ========== Calculate temperature from ADC raw value ==========
float adc_to_temperature(uint16_t adc_raw)
{
    // 1. ADC raw value → thermistor voltage
    float v_ntc = adc_raw * (V_REF / (float)(1 << ADC_BITS));

    // 2. Voltage → thermistor resistance (voltage dividing formula)
    float r_ntc = R_PULLUP * v_ntc / (V_SUPPLY - v_ntc);

    // 3. Resistance → temperature (look-up table interpolation)
    float temp = ntc_lookup(r_ntc);
    return temp;
}

// ========== NTC table lookup (simplified version) ==========
// Precomputed R-T table: each entry is a temperature point (10–25°C intervals in the table)
typedef struct { float resistance; float temp; } ntc_point_t;

ntc_point_t ntc_table[] = {
    // R(Ω) ,  T(°C)   (β=3900K, R25=10kΩ)
    { 335000,   -40 },
    { 176000,   -30 },
    {  96000,   -20 },
    {  54000,   -10 },
    {  33100,     0 },
    {  20000,    10 },
    {  10000,    25 },
    {   5300,    40 },
    {   2900,    55 },
    {   1700,    70 },
    {   1100,    85 },
    {    720,   100 },
    {    370,   125 },
    {      0,   150 },  // End of table (0Ω corresponds to 150°C)
};
#define NTC_TABLE_SIZE (sizeof(ntc_table)/sizeof(ntc_table[0]))

float ntc_lookup(float r)
{
    if (r <= 0) return 150.0f;

    // Find which two entries r falls between
    for (int i = 0; i < NTC_TABLE_SIZE - 1; i++) {
        if (r >= ntc_table[i+1].resistance) {
            // Linear interpolation (resistance-temperature is approximately linear in a narrow range)
            float t_low  = ntc_table[i+1].temp;
            float t_high = ntc_table[i].temp;
            float r_low  = ntc_table[i+1].resistance;
            float r_high = ntc_table[i].resistance;

            if (r_high == r_low) return t_low;
            float ratio = (r - r_low) / (r_high - r_low);
            return t_low + ratio * (t_high - t_low);
        }
    }
    return -40.0f;  // Very high resistance → extremely low temperature
}

// ========== Use ==========
void adc_example(void)
{
    adc0_init();

    // The coolant temperature sensor is connected to Channel 12 of ADC0 (assumed)
    uint16_t raw = adc_read_channel(12);
    float coolant_temp = adc_to_temperature(raw);

    if (coolant_temp > 95.0f) gpio_set(COOLING_FAN_PIN);  // Turn on the cooling fan
}
```

This code starts from the charge accumulation on the thermistor and ends with the cooling fan turning on. It passes through the voltage divider, filter, op amp, ADC successive approximation logic, 12-bit SAR comparator, the DMA and ADC configuration you wrote, your interpolation lookup table, your threshold judgment, and GPIO output. Physical heat of 85°C → physical action of fan rotation. The digital chain is in the middle, and the physical world is at both ends.

## Reverse signal chain: from register value to physical action

Now in turn, your code controls the injector spray.

**your code**:

```c
uint16_t desired_pw_ticks = lookup_injection_map(rpm, load);
// 1800rpm, 60% load → desired_pw_ticks = 1344 (about 28μs @48MHz)
FTM_C3V = desired_pw_ticks;  // Write to compare register
```

**Timer Peripheral**: FTM0 counts up at 48MHz. When counter CNT matches C3V, the output comparison channel flips. Configured as PWM mode 1: The output is high when CNT < C3V, and the output is low when CNT ≥ C3V. High level duration = 1344 / 48MHz ≈ 28.0μs.

In fact, the injector drive is "Peak & Hold" current control:

- Peak stage: 100% duty cycle (or high duty cycle), allowing the coil current to quickly rise from 0 to valve opening current (~10A) within tens of microseconds, and the valve core begins to move.
- Holding phase: The duty cycle is reduced to about 30%, and the maintaining current is about 3A. Just enough to keep the spool open but not overly hot.

This is not a simple "on 5ms off 0ms", but a digital PWM synthesis of precise current waveforms. The timer comparison unit of your MCU continuously flips the pin level in the background, and cooperates with the external MOSFET driver and current sampling resistor to form a closed-loop current control loop.

**MOSFET driver**: The GPIO pin of the MCU outputs 3.3V and has a driving capacity of only a few mA. It cannot directly drive high-power MOSFETs. A gate driver is required (such as Infineon TLE92104, quad-channel low-side pre-driver). The gate driver converts the 3.3V logic level into a 12-15V gate drive voltage, providing a peak gate charge and discharge current of more than 1A (quickly charging the gate capacitance Ciss of the MOSFET, with a typical value of several nF). From 3.3V to 12V, from a few mA to 1A, this is "drive capability amplification".

**Power MOSFET**: After the gate voltage exceeds Vgs(th) (typically 2.5V), the drain-source channel begins to conduct. After the gate reaches 12V, Rds(on) reaches a minimum value (typically a few mΩ). The 12V battery voltage goes through the injector coil and MOSFET to ground. The current rises quickly with a slope of V_bat/L_coil (coil inductance L is usually about 1-2mH).

**Injector**: The coil is energized to generate a magnetic field (B = μ₀×N×I/l, the iron core amplifies hundreds of times). Electromagnetic force = (B²×A) / (2μ₀), lifting the valve core against the spring force. The valve core is lifted about 0.1mm, and high-pressure fuel (GDI in-cylinder direct injection can reach 200bar) is injected into the cylinder from micropores (about 100-200μm diameter). The amount of fuel injected is determined by the injection duration, fuel pressure, and hole area. A typical injection is about 5-15mg.

**Your C code → Timer compare register → PWM output pin → Gate pre-driver → Power MOSFET → Coil current rise → Magnetic field build-up → Spool displacement → Fuel injection. ** The `=1344` you wrote becomes 5mg of precise gasoline atomization in the cylinder, which is mixed with the intake air and ignited in the spark plug to push the piston.

## S32K is configured with PWM driven injector

```c
// ========== FlexTimer (FTM0) PWM initialization ==========
// S32K144: FTM0 base address = 0x40038000
// FTM0 Channel 3 controls the low side of the injector MOSFET

#define FTM0_BASE  0x40038000

#define FTM_SC      (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x00))  // Status/Control
#define FTM_CNT     (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x04))  // Counter
#define FTM_MOD (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x08)) // Modulo (period)
#define FTM_C3SC    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x24))  // Channel 3 Status/Control
#define FTM_C3V (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x28)) // Channel 3 Value (duty cycle)
#define FTM_COMBINE (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x64))  // Combine control
#define FTM_DEADTIME (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x68)) // Dead-time control
#define FTM_EXTTRIG (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x6c))  // External Trigger
#define FTM_CONF    (*(volatile uint32_t *)(FTM0_BASE + 0x84))  // Config

// Dead-time Insertion, required for H-Bridge safety
// When two MOSFETs are on the same half-bridge (high side/low side), if both are turned on at the same time during switching
// → "shoot-through" → Instantaneous high current burns out the MOSFET
// Dead time = short window during which both MOSFETs are off
#define DEADTIME_NS 200 // Dead time 200ns (typical value 100-500ns)

void ftm0_pwm_init(uint32_t pwm_freq_hz)
{
    // 1. PCC enables FTM0 clock (omitted)

    // 2. Set the timer period (Modulo)
    //    Assume FTM clock = Bus clock = 48MHz (the specific clock tree is subject to the S32K144 reference manual)
    //    Prescaler = ÷1 (highest resolution)
    //    MOD = FTM_clk / pwm_freq_hz - 1
    // Target PWM frequency 20kHz → MOD = 48M/20k -1 = 2399
    FTM_SC = (0 << 0)       // PS = ÷1 (prescaler = 1)
           | (1 << 3);      // CLKS = System Clock (start counter, CLKS field is bits 4:3)

    uint32_t mod_val = (48000000UL / pwm_freq_hz) - 1;
    FTM_MOD = mod_val;      // cycle

    // 3. Configure Channel 3 as Edge-Aligned PWM (Edge-Aligned)
    //    MSB:MSA = 10 (edge aligned)
    //    ELSB:ELSA = 10 (high-true pulse, that is, output high when CNT<C3V)
    FTM_C3SC = (2 << 2)    // MS=10 (edge alignment)
             | (2 << 4);   // ELS=10 (high-true, initially low)

    // 4. Dead time configuration (if complementary output mode)
    //    For H-Bridge driver, dead zone can be inserted in Combine mode
    //    It is assumed here that it is a single-ended low-side driver (no dead zone is required, but the configuration method is shown)
    //    FTM_DEADTIME: DTPS=÷1, DTVAL=48M×200ns = 10; DTEN is set in Combine mode
    uint32_t dt_val = (DEADTIME_NS * 48UL + 500) / 1000UL;  // 48M × 200ns + rounding → 10
    FTM_DEADTIME = (0 << 6)   // DTPS = ÷1 (dead-band prescaler)
                 | dt_val;    // DTVAL = dead time (number of FTM clock cycles)

    // 5. Initial duty cycle = 0 (no fuel injection first)
    FTM_C3V = 0;
}

// ========== Set injection pulse width ==========
// pulse_width_us: Injection pulse width (microseconds)
//   0 = No fuel injection
//   Peak phase typical: 50-200μs @ 100% duty cycle
// Typical hold phase: Follow with low duty cycle (achieved by adjusting CnV)
void injector_set_pulse_us(uint16_t pulse_width_us)
{
    uint32_t mod_val = FTM_MOD;  // cycle

    if (pulse_width_us == 0) {
        FTM_C3V = 0;            // Duty Cycle = 0 → Off
        return;
    }

    // CnV = pulse_width_us × FTM_clock_MHz
    // FTM clk=48MHz → every μs=48 counts
    uint32_t cv = pulse_width_us * 48UL;

    // Cannot exceed MOD
    if (cv > mod_val) cv = mod_val;
    FTM_C3V = cv;
}

// ========== Hold Phase - Low Duty Cycle Current Control ==========
// After the peak, switch to the hold phase:
//   Set PWM duty cycle ≈ 30% → CnV = 0.30 × MOD
void injector_hold_phase(void)
{
    uint32_t mod_val = FTM_MOD;
    FTM_C3V = mod_val * 30 / 100;  // 30% duty cycle
}

// ========== H-Bridge drives DC brushed motor (dead zone insertion example) ==========
// Two half-bridges, each half-bridge consists of two MOSFETs: high side (H) and low side (L)
// FTM0 CH2 = Half-bridge A high side, FTM0 CH3 = Half-bridge A low side (complementary output)
// Combine mode: CH2/CH3 form a complementary pair, FTM automatically inserts the dead zone
//
// Motor forward rotation: CH2 PWM ON, CH3 LOW (duty cycle control speed)
// Motor reverse rotation: CH2 LOW, CH3 PWM ON
// Dead Time: Each time switching states, both MOSFETs are off at the same time for 200ns
//
// Without implementing the complete driver code, demonstrate the Combine mode configuration principle:
// FTM_COMBINE |= (1 << 8); // COMBINE1=1 (CH2/CH3 complementary)
// FTM_COMBINE |= (1 << 9); // COMP1=1 (CH2 output negation = complement)
// FTM_C2V = duty_val; // duty cycle
// FTM_C3V = duty_val; // Automatically generate complementary + dead zone
```

When this code controls the physical action of the injector, the 48MHz clock of the FTM timer drives the counter continuously upward. When CNT matches C3V, a hardware comparator toggles the output pin in picoseconds. The 3.3V signal from the pin enters the gate pre-driver, is amplified into a 12V/1A gate drive current, and is injected into the gate capacitance of the MOSFET. The gate is charged to Vgs(th), the channel is turned on, the 12V battery voltage passes through the coil to ground, the current rises rapidly from zero, the magnetic field is established, the valve core moves, and high-pressure fuel is injected.

The whole process: `FTM_C3V = 1344` in software → about 10μs timer comparison delay → about 100ns gate drive delay → about 200ns MOSFET switching delay → about 20μs spool mechanical movement time. The total delay is about 30μs. For an engine running at 6000RPM (approximately 28μs for each crankshaft rotation of 1°), this delay corresponds to a crankshaft angle of about 1°, which is accurate enough.

## Sensor type: starting from the scene

Each sensor type is not an isolated technology option, they are choices that emerge naturally in a specific measurement scenario.

## Temperature measurement: NTC and PTC thermistor

**Resistive type (NTC/PTC thermistor). **NTC (Negative Temperature Coefficient): As the temperature increases, the carrier density of the semiconductor increases and the resistance decreases. The β material constant defines the R-T relationship. The signal conditioning of resistive temperature sensors only requires pull-up/pull-down resistors and analog-to-digital conversion. It is the simplest, cheapest, and most reliable, so it is most used in automotive temperature measurement.

## Position Measurement: From Potentiometer to Hall Effect

**Potentiometer (resistive). ** Physical displacement changes the position of the sliding contact, and the resistor voltage dividing ratio changes accordingly. Steering wheel angle sensor and accelerator pedal position sensor are typical resistive position sensors.

**Hall Effect. ** When current flows perpendicular to the magnetic field in a semiconductor, the charge carriers are deflected by the Lorentz force, generating Hall voltage on both sides perpendicular to the direction of the current and magnetic field. Integrated Hall sensors (such as Infineon TLE4953) package the Hall chip, amplifier, threshold comparator, and output driver together, and add a back magnet. When the target wheel tooth tip passes by, the magnetic field intensity changes → the Hall voltage changes → the comparator triggers → a square wave pulse is output. Camshaft position sensor (CMP) and wheel speed sensor (ABS) usually use Hall effect sensors. Compared with resistive sensors, Hall sensors are non-contact and wear-free, and have obvious life advantages in automotive position measurement. However, it requires a magnet when used, and the power consumption is higher than that of passive sensors.

## Speed Measurement: From Reluctance to Variable Reluctance

**Variable reluctance (inductive). ** Crankshaft position sensor (CKP): A ferromagnetic target wheel (such as 60-2 teeth, 58 teeth, 2 missing teeth) is installed on the crankshaft and rotates with the crankshaft. The sensor head is a permanent magnet + coil. When the teeth of the target wheel pass the sensor, the air gap decreases → the magnetic resistance of the magnetic circuit decreases → the magnetic flux increases → a voltage is induced in the coil (Faraday's law: V = -N × dΦ/dt). The VRS interface chip of the ECU shapes the sine wave into a square wave. The MCU captures and measures the time interval between adjacent teeth through the input of the timer, and calculates the instantaneous crankshaft position and speed. Advantages of inductive sensors: no external power supply required, reliable in extreme temperatures (-40 to 180°C in the engine oil pan), resistant to contamination (sludge, metal debris). The disadvantage is that the output amplitude changes with the rotation speed. At low speed, the signal amplitude is very low (<100mV), requiring a high-sensitivity interface chip.

## Acceleration and Pressure: From MEMS to Piezoelectrics

**Capacitive (MEMS accelerometer). ** Microelectromechanical accelerometers (such as the Bosch SMI230) have a suspended silicon mass inside. Acceleration causes the mass block to displace, and the capacitance difference between the comb-shaped electrode plate and the fixed electrode plate on the mass block changes. ASIC converts the capacitance difference into voltage through a switched capacitor circuit, and then converts it into a digital signal through a Σ-Δ ADC. The resolution can reach μg level. The advantages of capacitive sensors: no friction, no contact, high sensitivity, low temperature drift. The disadvantage is that the capacitance value is extremely small (~pF order) and signal conditioning requires a high input impedance amplifier and shielding. Susceptible to parasitic capacitance and electromagnetic interference.

**Resistive type (strain gauge). ** When metal foil is stretched under stress, the length increases, the cross-sectional area decreases, and the resistance increases (ΔR/R = GF×ε, GF=gauge coefficient≈2, ε=strain). It is attached to the metal elastomer to form a load/pressure sensor.

**Piezoelectric effect. ** Knock Sensor: The piezoelectric element is pressed against the outer wall of the engine block. When detonation (a pressure shock wave caused by abnormal combustion) occurs in the cylinder, the pressure wave propagates through the cylinder wall to the sensor, and the piezoelectric element generates a charge output proportional to the vibration amplitude. The knock detection chip of the ECU converts the charge into voltage and integrates it within a specific crankshaft angle window. If it exceeds the threshold, it is judged as knocking and the ignition advance angle is immediately delayed. Knock sensors are typical "passive sensors" that do not require external power supply.

Piezoelectric sensors are used for actuator direction: Piezoelectric injectors: The piezoelectric stack produces micron-scale displacements under voltage, which directly drives the injector needle. The response speed is much faster than that of the solenoid valve (about 100μs from signal to valve opening vs more than 500μs of the solenoid valve). It is used in diesel high-pressure common rail multiple injection systems and can do 5-7 micro-injections in one working cycle.

## Actuator type: four ways from electric to force

**1. Solenoid valve (Solenoid)**

The fixed coil is energized → the iron core is magnetized → electromagnetic force is generated → the valve core is attracted or pushed open. Fuel injectors, ABS hydraulic valves, transmission shift solenoid valves, variable valve timing (OCV) solenoid valves.

Driving mode: PWM current control (peak-hold mode). Response time: approximately 0.5-2ms from power on to valve core movement. Advantages: simple structure, mature control and low cost. It is the most common type of actuator used in automobiles.

**2. Brushed DC Motor**

Permanent magnet stator + wound rotor + commutator and brushes. Change the PWM duty cycle to change the speed, and change the switching sequence of the H-Bridge to change the steering.

- Electric power steering (EPS) motor: 12V, 80A peak, approximately 0.8kW level mechanical output (12V×80A approximately 0.96kW input). Accurate torque output is achieved through high-precision current sampling + PI current loop controller (the BLDC solution uses FOC).
- Window lift motor, seat adjustment motor: 12V, lower power, simple PWM open-loop control.

The mechanical commutator of brushed motors generates sparks during operation (a major source of EMI), and sensor traces near brushed motors require additional shielding.

**3. Brushless DC motor (BLDC = Brushless DC)**

Permanent magnet rotor + wound stator + Hall sensor (or encoder) detects the rotor position + 3-phase bridge drive composed of 6 MOSFETs. Electronic commutation replaces mechanical commutation: no brushes, no sparks, higher efficiency and longer life.

Electric water pump, electric air conditioning compressor, main drive motor (electric vehicle/hybrid vehicle). FOC (vector control) technology decouples the three-phase current into a magnetic field component (id) and a torque component (iq), which are controlled separately, just like controlling excitation and torque separately.

**4. Stepper Motor**

The rotor is a multipole permanent magnet and the stator has multiple independent windings. Each time a pulse is given, the rotor moves to the next stable position (the step angle is usually 1.8°). The advantage is that it can achieve precise positioning in open loop (no encoder feedback required).

Instrument pointer drive, air conditioning outlet damper adjustment, LED headlight height adjustment (stepper motors are used for these light load precise position scenarios). Driving method: Apply different combinations of current directions to the double H-Bridge through a controller IC (such as ST L9942) to generate a rotating magnetic field.

## What's going on in your car - from buttons to speakers

You press the ‘Volume +’ button on the steering wheel. This action completes the following journey in a few milliseconds: Membrane switch under button closes → GPIO detects edge → Interrupt triggers → ISR reads button status via I2C → Body controller sends ‘volume + request’ via CAN → Infotainment ECU receives → Audio DSP on application processor adjusts digital gain → New audio sample is sent to DAC via I2S bus → DAC outputs analog signal → Class D amplifier amplifies → Speaker cone vibrates → Sound waves travel to your ears.

The entire process, from your fingertips to your eardrums, takes less than 100 milliseconds. In these 100 milliseconds, at least 6 analog-to-digital/digital-to-analog conversions, 4 different communication protocols (GPIO, I2C, CAN, I2S), 2 different chips (MCU and DSP), and 1 digital signal processing (gain adjustment) occurred. Every step is accurate to microseconds. Not because someone 'carefully designed' the entire link (in fact the modules came from 5 different vendors), but because each layer adheres to its own protocol specifications and timing constraints.

This is ‘architecture’: instead of one person controlling the overall situation, each layer makes its own limited commitments, and then trusts other layers to fulfill their commitments. Layering is not a ‘design pattern’. Layering is humanity’s only weapon against infinite complexity. Your brain processes the world in layers (retina → visual cortex → associative cortex → prefrontal lobes), and your chip processes signals the same way. This is no coincidence, this is the basic law of information processing in the physical world.

## The physical world continues to fight back

The code output of pure software engineers is images, text, and network messages. These complete the entire life cycle in the digital domain, from memory to screen, from CPU to network card, the entire process is in the world of silicon.

Embedded engineers are different. Your code runs in the digital domain, but its effects fall in the physical domain.

The LSB of the ADC is 0.8mV. If a 10mA current flows through the analog ground wire on the PCB (for example, the return path of the adjacent PWM signal includes this section of ground wire), and the impedance of this section of ground wire is 0.1Ω (a 1cm-long 1oz copper trace is about 0.5mΩ, but the combined contact resistance of vias and connectors may reach tens of mΩ), the resulting voltage difference is 1mV. This 1mV is 1.25 LSB, and the value read by your ADC is 1.25 LSB cheaper than the real value. The ENOB of the 12-bit ADC drops directly from the theoretical 10.5 bits to 9 bits.

High current PWM driven switching transient, EPS motor peak value is 80A, di/dt can reach 0.5A/ns. Noise voltages are induced on adjacent sensor analog signal lines. If the analog signal line is single-ended (no differential pair), the noise is directly superimposed on the signal, and the ADC will collect the noise and the signal together. A sampling value may jump from the normal 85°C to 145°C. The "fault diagnosis" in the code misjudges that the coolant is overheated, triggering the power reduction protection. The engine suddenly lost power, but the driver didn't know it was caused by ground wire impedance.

There is a mechanical delay of tens of microseconds from the moment the solenoid valve of the injector receives the electrical signal to actually opening the valve. This delay varies with coil temperature (temperature coefficient of copper resistance 0.39%/°C). The coil resistance is about 0.5Ω when cold starting at -40°C, and about 1.2Ω when operating at high temperature of 125°C. With the same 12V drive, the current drops from 24A to 10A, causing the electromagnetic force to decrease and the valve opening delay to increase from 30μs to 70μs. Without delay compensation based on current feedback, the fuel injection amount deviation can reach more than 10% under -40°C and 125°C operating conditions.

Low-frequency thermal cycling fatigues solder joints. Temperature cycling increases the ESR and decreases the capacitance of MLCC (multilayer ceramic capacitors) (the capacitance of Y5V dielectric is only 20% of the nominal value at -40°C). Humidity causes a micro-conductive film to form on the PCB surface, causing the bias voltage of high-impedance analog channels to drift. Electromagnetic radiation causes bit flipping in SPI communication, requiring ECC or a retransmission mechanism to correct errors.

**Your code is not running under ideal circumstances. It is in the center of the bridge, constantly fighting against the noise, uncertainty, and chaos of the physical world. ** This is the unique perspective of an embedded engineer: you will always be thinking in two domains at the same time: the bit domain and the physical domain. Each of your `ADC_RA`, `FTM_C3V`, and `PTx_PSOR` is a pillar of a bridge.

## The center of the bridge

Stand in the center of the bridge. On your left is the digital world: C code, compilers, instruction sets, AHB bus cycles, register bit operations. On your right is the physical world: the charge carriers on the thermistor, the bias current of the op amp input stage, the charging of the ADC sampling capacitor, the charging and discharging of the MOSFET gate capacitor, the establishment of the magnetic field of the injector coil, the spring force resistance of the valve core, and the atomization of high-pressure fuel.

Every line of code you write passes cause and effect between these two worlds. `float temperature = adc_to_temperature(raw)` is the bridge from physics to digital. `FTM_C3V = pulse_width_us` is the bridge from digital to physical. You're not typing characters on a keyboard. You are designing a signal path between two worlds.

This perspective, that is, "penetration", is your fundamental ability as an automotive electronics engineer. You don’t just understand “software”, you also understand physics. You don’t just understand “digital”, you also understand analog. You don’t just understand the “protocol”, you also understand the physical noise in each link of the signal chain.

This is the metaphor from sand to ruts: the tracks left by your fingers as they slide across the sand are the common imprint of all industrial workers, from the CVD deposition in the fab to the PWM output of the ECU.
