# 4.1 Whispers Inside the Chip

## Office hall with hundreds of people: I’ll give you a design question

Imagine an open office hall the size of a football field, with hundreds of people working at their respective workstations and occasionally needing to exchange information with people sitting far away.

If it were you, how would you design communication in this space?

Option 1: Everyone stands up and shouts loudly, simple and rude. But when hundreds of people shout at the same time, you can't hear anything. This is called a "broadcast storm."

Option 2: Everyone pulls a line to the information center. To whomever you want to talk to, first send the message to the operator, who will forward it for you. Effective, but the operator becomes the bottleneck. This is called a "star topology".

Option 3: Divide by region. A dozen people next to each other share a regional line. Each area has a regional switchboard, and the regional switchboards are interconnected by trunk lines. This is called a "hierarchical bus".

Inside your MCU, there are CPU cores, SRAM, Flash controller, DMA (Direct Memory Access, direct memory access, data can be transferred without going through the CPU) engine, and dozens of peripherals. They need to communicate with each other. If each module is wired point-to-point. The amount of wiring is O(N²), and the chip area is directly exploded. Therefore, chip designers only use one solution: solution three, hierarchical bus.

But this is only an architectural level answer. The real devil is in the details: What exactly is the "backbone line from area A to area B" on the silicon chip? How wide is it? How long does it take for the signal to complete? When two people want to talk at the same time, who speaks first?

## Two roads: expressway and ordinary road

ARM's AMBA (Advanced Microcontroller Bus Architecture) specification is the most mainstream bus specification for embedded MCUs. The internal buses of S32K and STM32 in your hand all follow AMBA; the AHB crossbar switch of S32K is connected to the AIPS peripheral bridge (functionally corresponding to APB), the AURIX uses SRI/SPB, and the RH850 uses a private bus, but the hierarchical idea is the same.

In MCUs, AMBA is most commonly used as a two-layer bus: AHB and APB (higher performance SoCs also use AXI).

**AHB (Advanced High-performance Bus): "Highway"**

AHB serves high-speed modules: CPU core, SRAM, Flash controller, and DMA.

- Support burst transmission. Multiple data at consecutive addresses are completed in a single transfer. The CPU fetching instructions from Flash is a typical AHB burst: four 32-bit words (4-beat incremental burst) are read out at a time for the Cortex-M4 instruction fetch unit to continuously fetch instructions.
- Supports multiple master devices. Multiple masters can initiate transmissions, and the arbiter decides who occupies the bus. CPU Core, DMA, and Ethernet MAC can all become AHB masters.
- Address and data pipelining. The address phase and data phase are in different cycles, and the address phase of the current transmission can overlap with the data phase of the last transmission. This is called "address pipelining" (pipelining) and is the key to AHB being able to complete one beat transfer per clock cycle.

**APB (Advanced Peripheral Bus): "Ordinary Road"**

APB is a low-speed, low-power bus that serves peripherals: GPIO (general-purpose input and output pins), UART (universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter, serial communication), SPI (serial peripheral interface, high-speed full-duplex serial bus), I2C (inter-integrated circuit bus, two-wire low-speed serial bus), timers, and watchdogs.

- Burst is not supported. Each transfer is completed independently.
- One-way single-master device, APB bridge is the only master. All APB peripherals are slaves and can only be accessed by the bridge.
- Simplified handshake protocol, the state machine has only three states: IDLE → SETUP → ACCESS → IDLE.APB is an "ordinary road" with a speed limit, no passing lane, and only one entrance and exit. But its advantage is extremely low power consumption and no need to maintain the eye diagram of high-speed signals.

Why does APB save power than AHB? Because its clock tree is simple. APB peripherals are hung on the APB clock domain, and this clock can be turned off by clock gating when the APB is not transmitting. AHB is different: the AHB master may initiate transmission at any time, and the AHB clock is almost always running. In low-power MCUs, AHB power consumption can account for 15%-20% of the total chip power consumption. APB peripherals typically consume less than 1% power.

**AHB-APB Bridge**

The bridge between AHB and APB converts the access initiated by the CPU on AHB to the timing of the APB protocol. At the same time, it serves as the slave of AHB and the master of APB.

The bridge from AHB to APB is a highway off-ramp. Slow down the 120km/h traffic flow (AHB 112MHz) to 60km/h (APB 56MHz), and then merge into the ordinary road.

```
        CPU (AHB Master)
             |
      +------+------+
      | AHB Matrix  |
      +--+---+---+--+
         |   |   |
    +----+ +-+-+ +----+
    |SRAM| |Bridge| |Flash|
    +----+ +--+---+ +----+
              |
         +----+----+
         | APB Bus |
         +-+--+--+-+
           |  |  |
       +---+ +-+-+ +---+
       |GPIO| |UART| |SPI|
       +---+ +---+ +---+
```

Each peripheral is mapped into a unified memory address space. The registers of GPIOA start from `0x400FF000`; the registers of SPI1 start from `0x4002C000`. These addresses are not "happened", they are derived from the partitioning rules of the AMBA spec: base address selection, offset superposition.

## AHB Burst: A complete journey of fetching

The best way to understand a bus is to look at a specific transaction. When the Cortex-M4 fetches an instruction from Flash, such as `STR R0, [R1]` at 0x08000100 (the CPU's instruction prefetch unit initiates a read transaction on the AHB). But this is not just a single transfer: the Cortex-M4 instruction fetch path will initiate a 4-beat incremental burst (INCR4), reading four 32-bit words at a time.

The complete AHB burst timing is as follows:

AHB's pipeline burst transmission is like a relay race. The address phase is pre-run (the baton has not been received but has begun to accelerate), and the data phase is baton reception (the moment when the baton is passed). The address phase of the current transfer and the data phase of the previous transfer overlap in the same clock cycle. This is the "address pipeline". Imagine four runners on the track: the first has handed over the stick (data phase), the second is reaching out (address phase), the third has just stepped onto the starting line, and the fourth is still in the waiting area. In each cycle, there is one person passing the baton and one person taking over.

**Cycle 1 (Address Phase #1)**: `0x08000100` is sent on the address bus. HTRANS=NONSEQ (non-consecutive, first transmission of burst). HBURST=INCR4 (4-beat incremental burst). HSIZE=WORD (32 bits). HWRITE=0 (read). HSEL_Flash=1.

Cycle 2 (data phase #1 + address phase #2): The Flash controller drives HRDATA when HREADY=1 and returns the content of `0x08000100-0x08000103` (the encoding of the first instruction). At the same time, `0x08000104` is sent on the address bus, HTRANS=SEQ (continuous, subsequent transmission of burst).

Cycle 3 (data phase #2 + address phase #3): HRDATA returns the contents of `0x08000104-0x08000107`. HADDR sent `0x08000108`. HTRANS=SEQ.

Cycle 4 (data phase #3 + address phase #4): HRDATA returns the contents of `0x08000108-0x0800010B`. HADDR sent `0x0800010C`. HTRANS=SEQ.

**Cycle 5 (Data Phase #4)**: HRDATA returns the contents of `0x0800010C-0x0800010F`. HTRANS=IDLE. Transfer completed.

AHB read back 16 bytes in 4 data cycles (5 bus cycles total because there was 1 address pipeline overlap cycle). This is the power of burst. There is no need to restart a bus transaction for each 32-bit word. NONSEQ appears only once. The next three are all SEQ.If the Flash controller needs to wait for a cycle (for example, the Flash access delay is 3 AHB cycles), it will pull HREADY low during the data phase. The AHB master samples HREADY on each rising edge of HCLK. If it is low, the current data phase is extended by one cycle. This is why Flash access delay will directly affect the CPU execution speed. When AHB inserts a wait cycle, the CPU pipeline is waiting.

## Penetration: Track a register write operation

When you execute:

```c
*(volatile uint32_t *)0x400FF000 = 0x00000001; // Set GPIOA pin 0 high
```

This operation goes through a long "supply chain" inside the chip. We track it layer by layer.

**Level 1: Cortex-M4 store instruction**. The compiler translates this C statement into `STR R0, [R1]`, which is a register indirect addressing store instruction. R1 contains `0x400FF000`. This instruction enters the three-stage pipeline of Cortex-M4: fetch, decode, and execute. During the execution phase, the store instruction is sent to the AHB bus interface unit (BIU).

**Layer 2: AHB-Lite bus cycles**. BIU initiates a write transaction (Non-Sequential, no waiting) on ​​the AHB bus. `0x400FF000` is sent on the address bus (HADDR), `0x00000001` is sent on the write data bus (HWDATA), and the control signal HTRANS=NONSEQ, HWRITE=1, HSIZE=WORD (32-bit). BIU waits for the slave device to pull HREADY low, otherwise a wait cycle is inserted.

**Level 3: AHB bus matrix lookup**. The high bits of address `0x400FF000` enter the address decoder. The decoder is a set of combinational logic, essentially an address range comparator composed of hundreds of NAND gates. It determines that this address falls within the address window of "APB Bridge 1" (usually 0x40000000-0x400FFFFF), and then routes the HSEL signal to APB Bridge 1.

**Layer 4: Protocol conversion of AHB-APB Bridge**. Bridge receives AHB write transaction. The APB protocol does not support pipelines, and each transfer is an atomic operation. Bridge starts the APB state machine:

- IDLE: Default state.
- SETUP: Bridge pulls PSEL high (peripheral selection), sends the low bit of the address `0xF000` on address PADDR, sends `0x01` on write data PWDATA, and writes control PWRITE=1.
- ACCESS: In the next APB clock cycle, Bridge pulls PENABLE high. On the rising edge of this cycle, the selected APB peripheral (GPIOA) samples PWDATA, PADDR, and PWRITE.
- Back to IDLE, transfer completed.

**Level 5: APB address decoding**. There is another layer of address decoding inside GPIOA. The low bits of this group of addresses of `0xF000` select the PDOR (output data register) of GPIOA. `0x01` is written to bit0 of PDOR, which means that Pin 0 of GPIOA is set to high level.

**Level 6: IO Pad output**. Bit 0 of the GPIOA output data register (PDOR) flips from 0 to 1. This logic 1 is pushed to the IO Pad through the output buffer (a series of buffer chains with gradually increasing drive capabilities). The PMOS tube on the Pad is turned on, pulling the pin voltage from 0V to VDD (3.3V). The pin charges the load capacitance of the PCB trace (typically 5-20pF). The voltage rises in an RC curve. The time constant is on the order of a few nanoseconds.

All this: from the Cortex-M4 executing the STR instruction, to the physical pin voltage changing from 0V to 3.3V, within a few AHB/APB clock cycles. If AHB runs at 112MHz (approximately 9ns period) and APB runs at 56MHz (approximately 18ns period), the whole process takes less than 100ns.

** From C code to physical voltages, less than 100 nanoseconds, but you pass through six layers of abstraction. **

## Bus Matrix: ArbitrationThe Bus Matrix is ​​the most overlooked part of AMBA, but also the most prone to problems. When multiple AHB masters want to access the same slave at the same time, for example, the CPU wants to read Flash, the DMA wants to write to SRAM, and the Ethernet MAC wants to read SRAM, who will serve that slave?

Arbiter decides.

Two common arbitration strategies:

**Fixed Priority**. CPU has the highest priority, DMA comes second, and Ethernet MAC is the lowest. Simple, but fatal disadvantage is: a high-priority master can starve a low-priority master. If the CPU continuously initiates AHB burst, the DMA will never get the bus, causing the SPI receive FIFO to overflow and data to be lost.

**Round-Robin**. After each transmission, the master that has just received service is reduced to the lowest priority, and the next master comes up. Fair, but adds a layer of combinational logic with slightly higher area and latency than fixed priority.

Use a concrete example to understand the difference between round robin and fixed priority. Assume that three masters (CPU, DMA, Ethernet MAC) request access to the same Flash slave at the same time. The CPU sends a 4-beat burst read command to Flash (about 36ns at 112MHz). The DMA needs to move the SPI received data from Flash to SRAM, and the MAC needs to read the Ethernet descriptor from Flash.

**Fixed priority system (CPU > DMA > MAC)**: CPU completes the entire 4-beat burst (about 36ns). Then DMA gets the bus and completes a single transfer (about 9ns). Then the MAC gets the bus. Problem: If the CPU continuously issues bursts (the instruction prefetch unit keeps fetching instructions), the DMA and MAC may wait for hundreds of nanoseconds. For DMA, this means that the SPI receive FIFO is overflowing. For MAC, it means that the receive descriptor cannot be updated in time and the frame is lost.

**Round-robin priority system**: After the CPU sends the first beat of the burst, it is reduced to the lowest priority. DMA is minimized after completing the transfer. The MAC is lowered after completing the transfer. The three masters alternately receive services, and no one will starve to death. The burst is "interrupted": the CPU's 4-beat burst is divided into three segments 2+1+1, with DMA and MAC transmission inserted in the middle. Total latency is increased, but fairness is guaranteed. The overall data throughput of the system remains unchanged, but there is no master backlog.

S32K's Crossbar Switch (AXBS) uses a hybrid strategy: two round-robin groups with fixed priorities within the group. CPU and DMA are grouped together. This design is a typical engineering trade-off, finding a "good enough" solution between fairness, area, and latency.

**But the arbiter may also fail. **


## Physical connections: Traces on the silicon are not ideal

On a chip, an AHB bus might span 2 millimeters of silicon.

2mm sounds short. But under the 28nm process, the thinnest metal traces are only a few tens of nanometers wide, and the aspect ratio is as high as 100,000 to one. This is not a "wire", this is a super long RC distributed network.

The resistance per unit length of the trace depends on the metal layer. The lower metals (Metal 1-3) are usually thin, narrow, and have high resistivity. The resistance per unit length can reach 1Ω/μm. The upper layer metal (Metal 6-8) is thick and wide, with low resistivity (about 0.1Ω/μm). Chip designers will place global buses like AHB on high-level metal.

The capacitance per unit length of the trace is determined by the metal spacing and the interlayer dielectric (usually SiO₂, k≈3.9). Typically 0.15-0.25 fF/μm. 2mm trace, total capacitance is about 300-500fF. Adding the input capacitance of each slave (about 50fF/slave), a typical AHB bus total capacitance is about 1pF.RC delay: 1pF × 200Ω (total trace resistance + drive transistor output resistance) ≈ 200ps. If AHB runs at 150MHz (cycle 6.67ns), this RC delay only accounts for 3% of the cycle, no problem. But if the bus frequency is doubled to 400MHz, RC delay accounts for 8%. Timing closure becomes difficult. Then consider the PVT (process, voltage, temperature) variation. The trace resistance is 30% higher at the slow process corner, and the resistance is 20% higher at high temperatures, and the total RC delay may reach 400ps. Accounting for 16% of the 400MHz cycle, the setup time is not enough.

(Note: The above RC analysis of the 28nm process is used as a teaching example. S32K actually uses the 55nm/90nm process node, and the wiring RC characteristics are different, but the quantitative analysis logic is the same.)

This is why you see in the reference manual of S32K: AHB maximum frequency is 112MHz. It's not "ARM's design can only run at 112MHz", it's the physical limit of this process node, this trace length, and this RC delay.

On the chip, every step of your C code is physical. Your `*reg = val` corresponds to not only an AHB write cycle, but also hundreds of femtofarads of capacitor charge and discharge, tens of microamps of gate-level leakage current, and metastable probability under the influence of thermal noise. These physical effects are what chip designers and yield engineers contend with every day.

## Clock tree: bus metronome

The bus cycle you see, each rising edge of HCLK, does not fall from the sky, it comes from the chip's clock tree (Clock Tree).

The clock source of the chip is an external crystal oscillator (or internal RC oscillator). This original clock is multiplied to the target frequency through a PLL (Phase Locked Loop). For example, the SYSCLK of S32K is up to 112MHz. But 112MHz cannot be directly sent to thousands of registers on the chip, because the clock signal needs to reach all registers at the same time, and the wiring delay on the chip is uneven.

A clock tree is a network of buffers distributed in an H-shaped or herringbone shape. The clock signal from the PLL passes through level-by-level buffers (clock buffers) and is evenly distributed to every corner of the chip. The delay of each level of buffer is accurately designed and simulated to ensure that the clock arrival time difference (clock skew) from the PLL to any register is controlled within tens of picoseconds.

Imagine an upside-down tree. The root of the tree is a PLL (Phase Locked Loop) that multiplies the frequency from an external 8MHz crystal to 112MHz. The trunk branches out into dozens of "branches" (clock buffer chains), and each branch branches out into hundreds of "treetops" (leaf clock pins). Each treetop drives the clock input of a register or a logic gate. Every "leaf" of the tree, that is, every clock-driven transistor gate, must receive the same rising edge in the same clock cycle. Clock Tree Synthesis is the most patience-testing step in chip physical design. EDA tools spend much more time than you think, just to ensure that the skew of the clock edge reaching every point on the chip does not exceed tens of picoseconds.

APB's clock gating occurs here. When the APB bridge is not transmitting, the first-level clock buffer on the APB clock domain is turned off by an AND gate, and the clock signal is not propagated backward. The dynamic power consumption of dozens of peripheral registers, state machines, and decoders in the entire APB domain is reduced to close to zero. The physical implementation of gate control is a "latch + AND" structure. The latch prevents glitches from changes in the gate signal during the high level of the clock.

This is why the power consumption of APB is less than 1/20 of AHB. It is not because APB runs slowly, but because APB is not "running" at all when it is not transmitting. The buffer on the clock tree does not flip, the gate capacitor does not charge or discharge, and the leakage current is only on the order of a few microamps.

Each of your `*reg = val` drives hundreds of clock buffer flips and thousands of gate capacitors to charge and discharge on the silicon chip. 0 and 1 are not just logical concepts, they are changes in the distribution of electron density on a silicon chip of several square micrometers.

## Why is your SPI speed stuck at 5MHz?You configure the MCU in SPI master mode on the debugging platform, and the S32K runs at 112MHz. You want to run 20MHz SCLK, but the SCLK on the oscilloscope is only 5MHz.

Check the reference manual and find that the SPI module is hung on the APB bus. The maximum clock frequency of APB may be SYSCLK/2 = 56MHz on S32K. But the SPI baud rate depends on the actual clock given to the SPI module (the clock source inside the SPI module), and the division coefficient of the baud rate divider. If you miss the clock gating enable for the SPI module in the clock configuration code, then the SPI module does not get the clock at all, and the baud rate divider works at the default lowest clock, and 5MHz is the ceiling.

Worse case: SPI's FIFO depth is only 4 bytes. If the CPU does not fill the FIFO in time, SCLK will stretch or directly create gaps between bytes. The SCLK you see is not a continuous square wave, but a cluster of pulses.

The root cause of all this is not in the SPI register itself, but in the clock tree, the signal path from AHB to APB, and the arbitration delay in the bus matrix.

This is direct feedback from the bus architecture on the performance of your code.

## You are talking to the spec author from 30 years ago

The "whispers" inside the chip are not magic, but rules agreed upon by engineers decades ago and faithfully implemented by billions of MOSFETs.

The designers of AHB and APB, the group of engineers who defined "multi-master arbitration", "APB low-power handshake" and "address decoding area" at the workstation of ARM's Cambridge office in the 1990s, will not know that you are debugging the speed of SPI on S32K today. But the rules they defined are being implemented precisely on the silicon chip under your fingers.

The address `0x400FF000` you see is not "by chance". It is derived from the partitioning rules of AMBA spec: base address selection, offset superposition. Every line of `volatile` pointer operations you write activates the state transition diagram they drew in the SPEC document thirty years ago.

Every time you look up the register address in the data sheet, every time you use an oscilloscope to capture the SPI waveform, every time you discover "why SCLK is only 5MHz", you are talking to the spec author from 30 years ago.

A tribute to all those who have defined fundamental protocols and architecture deep within history. You may have retired today, but your labor is faithfully run on billions of chips every day.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: understand how the on-chip bus-AHB/APB allows the CPU and peripherals to "quietly speak" on the silicon chip.

Key takeaways:
1. **AHB is a high-speed backbone**: multi-host arbitration, pipeline transmission, and DMA support. The CPU and DMA can take turns as the host to access the same set of peripherals.
2. **APB is a low-speed branch**: low-power handshake, no pipeline, single host. Hang UART, I2C, SPI, timer and other low-speed peripherals.
3. **The bus matrix is ​​the traffic dispatch center**: Arbitration logic determines who goes first. The specific use of fixed priority, round robin, or mixed strategy is determined by the chip design. Your SPI is robbed of bandwidth by DMA. This is not a bug, but the result of arbitration.
