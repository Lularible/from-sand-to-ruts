# Preface: Fear Not the Infinity of Truth

## "Why" write this book?

This idea goes back to when I was in college.

At that time, a roommate was writing C code. I was watching and said on a whim: **It would be great if we could know how the electronics are controlled in the computer when the code is running. **

The roommate looked surprised and said: This is too ridiculous.

Later, when I was at a panel factory, I saw how circuit diagrams were grown layer by layer on the substrate. In embedded companies, the underlying software and application layer code are debugged every day. The question has been hanging in my mind: How deep is this system? 

Later, I was deeply impressed by debugging a flash writing problem. The background is that NvM writes data to flash through FEE. FEE manages space by sector. When the data is updated, it writes to the erased area. When the sector is full, turn the page, erase a new sector, copy the valid data, and then continue writing. My scenario is to periodically write 4K data blocks. After running about 20 times, the write always returns failure.

I first went to the logic analyzer to capture the SPI waveform and found that the erase command used 0x21, which is 4K erase. However, the expected sector on the FEE side is 64K, and only 4K is wiped at a time. A large area is not wiped clean when turning pages. Before writing, FEE will read the flash to confirm whether it is all 0xFF, and will directly report a failure when reading the old data. Change the erase command to 64K and fix the page turning problem.

Next, I found that the written data was wrong: the first 1K was normal, and the next 3K all became 0xFF. I set a monitoring breakpoint on FEE's internal buffer and found that after the first 1K chunk was written, FEE's security mechanism proactively cleared the buffer to 0xFF to prevent residual data from leaking. This was the design intention. The problem is that the internal buffer is only 1K, and my data block is 4K. The buffer is expanded from 0x400 to 0x1000, and the problem is solved.

The erasure granularity is a deviation in the understanding of the hardware manual, and the buffer clearing is a configuration conflict between the AUTOSAR security mechanism and the block size. The two bugs belong to different layers of the software stack, but the symptoms are mixed together. It took a few days to figure it out. At that moment, I asked myself: Just an operation of "storing data into flash", how many layers of things are hidden underneath? 

Every chip on the circuit board is made of sand. There is silicon dioxide in the sand, which is purified to 99.999999999%. It is pulled into single crystals, cut out into wafers, photolithography, etching, doping, and deposition. There are more than a dozen processes and hundreds of steps. The operation accuracy reaches the atomic level. On this piece of silicon, billions of transistors switch at GHz frequencies to execute instructions, move data, and respond to interrupts. The code then drives the peripheral, the peripheral drives the actuator, and the actuator drives the wheels. The car started running on the road.

From sand, to chips, to automotive electronics.

There are dozens of disciplines including physics, chemistry, materials science, electronic engineering, computer architecture, operating systems, network protocols, real-time control, and functional safety. No one person can know it all. But in automotive electronics, you have to master quite a few of them. Because the ECU bug may not be in the code, but in the power integrity. The jitter of time synchronization may not be a protocol problem, but a temperature drift of the crystal oscillator.

I began to systematically sort out what I had learned and slowly wrote down notes. Over the years, the notes turned into drafts, and the drafts turned into this book.

After writing this book, I still don't fully know what the electrons are doing when the software is running. But at least I understand a little bit about the system.


This is the ultimate metaphor of the book: engineering is not the creation of one person, but a relay race. Turing handed the first stick, and von Neumann caught it. Shockley held out the transistor, and Kilby caught it. Bosch handed over the CAN protocol, and AUTOSAR picked it up. Now, the baton is passed to you. This book is the notes I wrote while running my own bat. I hope you can run further after taking it.

### A 30-second experiment for everyone

Before you start reading, do one thing. Stretch out your hands, palms up. A clenched fist is 0, an extended fist is 1. The right hand is the low position and the left hand is the high position.

Try it:

0 (make fists with both hands)
1 (only stretch your right hand)
2 (only extend left hand)
3 (with both hands stretched out)You have just completed a digital encoding with your body: physical state (hand shape) → information (number). Everything this book is about: the holes in the Turing tape, the on and off in the transistors, every bit in the ECU registers, is the same encoding, just expanded from two hands to billions of transistors.

## What kind of book is this?

It is not a textbook. The textbook talks about "what", this book talks about "why".

It is not a technical manual. The manual gives you conclusions, this book gives you questions.

This is an engineer's understanding. It starts from the philosophical question "What is computing" and goes all the way to diagnostic responses on the CAN bus. It attempts to connect the knowledge thread from the underlying hardware to the top-level application.


Computers are the crystallization of human collective wisdom. From Leibniz's dream, to Turing's paper tape, to the invention of the transistor, to the mass production of integrated circuits, to automobile electronics, this is the result of countless people's relay work over hundreds of years. I want to talk it out.

## Who is this book written for?

Written for embedded engineers in the automotive electronics field. You work with registers, interrupt vector tables, CAN matrix, and UDS diagnostic services every day. You know how, but you probably don’t have time to think about the “why.” This book helps you think.

Also for anyone curious about the "nature of computing". You don’t need to have ever built an ECU, as long as you’ve ever wondered: How do chips come out of sand? "Why" can computers "compute"? How do dozens of ECUs in a car talk to each other? This book is for you.

### You are already participating

You get in your car every morning and turn the key. What happened behind this action? The body controller detects the key insertion and notifies the engine ECU to wake up through the LIN bus. When the ECU is powered on, the HSM verifies the firmware signature. After passing, the CPU starts executing the code: initializing the CAN controller, configuring the ADC, and turning on the PWM output. When you step on the brakes and the brake light comes on, this half-movement has gone through the cooperation of at least five ECUs. You don't think about anything, but billions of transistors are already working for you. You are already participating in the largest industrial collaboration in human history, you just don’t know it yet. This book is to help you "know".

## What is this book not about?

- Does not write a complete mathematical derivation (that is not the position of this book)
- Do not write complete product-level code (read the source code and spec)
- Do not write "Learn xxx in 30 days" (efficiency is important, but not the most important)

The goal of this book is: **to make you understand**. Understand it deeply enough that you can have in-depth discussions with any peer, you can confidently debug circuit boards and code, and you can see the bigger picture behind the problem.

## Reading suggestions

The book is divided into seven parts, each part has a progressive relationship, but each chapter is relatively independent.

- **Beginners**: Read in order. Start with Part 1 and build your intuition for “computation.”
- **Experienced Engineers**: You can skip reading. The second part (chip physics) and the fourth part (communication protocol) may be the parts you use the most but understand the least.
- **Readers who need inspiration**: Skip directly to Part Seven.

The structure of each chapter is consistent: starting from a **thought experiment or engineering problem**, then conducting **hard core technical analysis**, and finally introducing **engineering practice or historical cases**.

### This book is my Lanting

Wang Xizhi said in the "Preface to the Lanting Collection" that "list the people of the time and record what they said": record today's people and events so that future generations can resonate with them when they read them. This was my original intention in writing this book. I have "recorded" my insights in semiconductor factory yield analysis, pitfalls in embedded development, questions while reading standard documents, and late nights spent in front of the oscilloscope in this book. I don't know who will read it. Maybe it is a new automotive electronics engineer, maybe a computer science student, maybe a high school student who is curious about "what computing is." But I know: when you read a certain passage in the book, are touched, and feel that "this person thinks exactly the same as I think," we have completed a "unity as one" that spans time and space. This is my Lanting. Hope it can become yours too.
