# 7.3 Resource Scarcity Is Eternal

## Value of glass substrate

I just entered the panel factory after graduating from my undergraduate degree. On the first day I walked into the production line and saw the glass substrates being transferred between the equipment. The first thought that comes to mind is: **How ​​much are these glass substrates worth? **

Based on the internal cost accounting of the panel factory at that time, a glass substrate, starting from cleaning and going through dozens of photolithography and etching, CMP, deposition, evaporation, and packaging, cost about several thousand dollars per piece. For a panel unit the size of a mobile phone screen, about a hundred can be cut out of a Gen 4.5 glass substrate. So the value of one substrate is about the price of a second-hand compact car.

This is the first lesson in resource scarcity. Scarcity is not about having no money in your pocket when you first graduate. Scarcity is about the **real material cost** of everything in your hands. When you write data into Flash, behind each bit is a Si-O bond broken down by electrons in the tunnel oxide layer. After writing hundreds of thousands of times, that bit can no longer be maintained reliably. For every mA of current you use, the MOSFET in the voltage regulator is consuming static power, which is a tiny subtraction from the battery's SOC. Every CAN frame you write takes up more than 200 μs of the bus. During these more than 200 μs, another higher-priority message must wait in the queue. Resources are not scarce at the "top level", they are already scarce from the moment you touch the glass substrate.

## Eight minutes in the lab

The laboratory of the panel factory is the most crowded place in the entire factory.

Microscopes, lighting test stands, microtome, FIB (focused ion beam), SEM (scanning electron microscope), there are only one or two of each equipment. Shared by six departments: yield group, failure analysis group, process development group, R&D group, quality group, and equipment maintenance group. When the door opens at eight o'clock every morning, there are already three or four people standing at the door. Some people brought notebooks, some brought coffee, and some simply brought a small bench. They came at 7:30.

"Why didn't your department come earlier?" I asked the engineer standing in line next to me.

He smiled: "Our department is even worse. The leader said that the budget is not enough and he cannot afford these equipment. He can only come and grab the public ones. Look at the coffee guy, he is from the failure analysis team. Their department has two microscopes, but he thinks the resolution of those two is not enough, so he insists on using the public 5000x optical microscope."

I handed my panel unit to the manager in the window. The administrator glanced at the number and wrote in the registration book: "Yield team, SEM observes defects, estimated to be 30 minutes."

"30 minutes?" I felt nervous.

The panel unit I have has a dark spot defect in a specific pixel. I need to find the physical coordinates of the pixel on the SEM, magnify it to thousands of times, observe the microstructure, and see if there is a pinhole in the gate insulation layer or a bridge in the metal layer. Is 30 minutes enough?

not enough. If I just took the panel and went directly into the SEM chamber, I would get lost in that matrix of millions of pixels.

Each pixel is only tens of microns square. There are thousands of rows × thousands of columns of pixel arrays (millions of pixels) on a panel unit. I need to find a specific pixel in a sea of ​​millions of micron-level pixels. The field of view of an SEM is approximately several hundred micrometers × hundreds of micrometers at low magnification, and only a few micrometers × several microns at high magnification. I want to find one among millions of pixels, just like finding a small black dot marked with a pencil on a piece of A4 paper, and if the paper is placed under a microscope, you can only see one millionth of the paper.

If I had gone in directly, I would have spent the precious 30 minutes "finding a seat." I would constantly adjust the magnification on the SEM screen, constantly move the sample stage, and constantly switch back and forth between blurry low-magnification images and high-magnification partial magnification, like groping in a huge maze. When I finally found the pixel, the administrator was already shouting at the door: "Timeout! Next one!"

I can only take out the panel and line up again tomorrow.

**This is not a waste of time, it is a waste of opportunity. ** That defect creates new scrap on the production line every day. Every day of my delay means that some of the tens of thousands of panel substrates continue to be scrapped. My 30 minutes is not mine, it belongs to the entire production line.

I recall what my engineer said to me on my second day of employment:"Equipment time is a hundred times more expensive than your time. Your time is worthless. You can spend one hour thinking clearly, one hour preparing, and one hour checking. But every minute of equipment time corresponds to the fate of a panel worth thousands of dollars. If you want to be 'fast' in front of the equipment, you have to be 'slow' behind the equipment."

So I didn't go straight in.

The panel unit in my hand was sent by the testing department. They discovered an anomaly during the light test. After the panel is lit, a pixel on the screen displays abnormally at a specific gray level, which can be seen with the naked eye. The test engineer circled the location of this abnormal point on the screen with a marker, and then sent the panel unit to me for failure analysis.

**This is the simplest positioning method: light test + visual observation. ** When the panel is lit, millions of pixels are glowing, and a bad pixel (such as a bright spot) is very eye-catching on the screen, like a particularly bright star in the night sky. You don't need coordinate conversion or pixel row and column calculations. You just need to light up the screen and look with your eyes to find the defects.

But here comes the question: I saw the abnormal point on the screen and circled it, but how to convert this position to the SEM?

The SEM sample stage uses physical coordinates (X, Y positions). If I don’t know the physical coordinates of this abnormal point on the panel and I put the panel into the SEM, where should the sample stage be moved?

If I put the panel directly into the SEM, I will face a dilemma: there is no direct correspondence between the location of the abnormal points on the screen and the physical coordinates of the SEM sample stage. 

The size of the panel unit is approximately a few centimeters by a few centimeters. There are millions of pixels on it, each of which is only tens of microns square. The field of view of SEM at low magnification is very small (about 600μm×600μm at 500x). Finding a pixel of tens of microns on a panel unit of several centimeters is like finding a coin on a football field. I need to repeatedly move the sample stage, zoom in and out, and search back and forth. This process may take dozens of minutes.

**I would get lost on the SEM if I relied on "guessing" locations or positioning based on sample stage coordinates. My 30 minutes will be eaten up by "finding a place". **

So I need to convert the "screen position visible to the naked eye" into "physical marks visible to the SEM" so that the SEM can locate the defective area at a glance.

I went to the testing department to borrow a laser marking machine. This machine can mark laser marking points on the surface of the panel.

I placed the panel unit into the sample stage of the laser marking machine. The laser marking machine comes with a visual positioning system. It can see the panel surface. I told it "where is the abnormal point circled on the screen." It converted that position into the physical coordinates of the sample stage, and then drew a box around the abnormal point.

The box is approximately 100μm × 100μm, and contains the abnormal pixel and several surrounding pixels. The laser carves four thin lines into the surface of the panel, creating a striking box mark. This box is almost invisible to the naked eye, but under SEM, it is an extremely eye-catching navigation beacon: four bright lines outline an area on the gray panel surface, telling you: "The defect is in this box."

The laser marking machine also has to queue up. But laser marking only takes a few minutes, much faster than SEM. I waited for 20 minutes, finished marking, and came back.

Then I go back to the office. Write down the SEM observation plan in your notebook:

- **Defect background**: The lighting test found abnormal pixels, the location was circled by the naked eye, and the laser has marked it with a box (100μm×100μm)
- **Positioning Strategy**: First find the laser box (visual navigation) on the SEM, and then look for abnormal pixels within the box
- **SEM observation steps**:
  1. Open the SEM chamber and put in the sample
  2. Switch to 200x field of view and scan the entire panel unit area
  3. Look for the laser box mark (a box outlined by four thin lines)
  4. Locate the box area and confirm the pixel array within the box
  5. Switch to 1000x and observe each pixel in the box
  6. Find abnormal pixels (which may have grain, bridging, or other defective features)
  7. Switch to 5000x and observe the defect details
  8. Take photos, record and export data
- Estimated time: 3 minutes for positioning, 10 minutes for observation, 5 minutes for photo recording, a total of 18 minutesI checked again: is the laser box in the correct position? Is the box size large enough (to cover the outlier and surrounding pixels)? Is the order of magnification switching of SEM reasonable? What are my alternatives if I can't see the laser box?

Everything is ready. I took the panel and walked into the laboratory again.

This time, it only took me 8 minutes.

8 minutes: I open the SEM chamber and insert the sample. With the 200x field of view, I scanned the panel unit area, looking for the laser box within a few centimeters. A minute later, I saw four thin lines outlining a striking box on the gray panel surface, framing a small area like a picture frame. This is my navigation beacon.

I positioned the SEM in the center of the box and magnified it to 1000x. There are approximately 3×3 pixels in the box. I looked at each pixel one by one and found an anomaly on one of the pixels: a particle of about 0.5μm landed in the metal wiring area, with an irregular shape, like a broken dust.

I zoomed in to 5000x, focused on the location of the particle, and saw that the particle fell between two metal traces, forming a tiny bridge. The material of the particles appears to be metal, which may be metal debris generated by the wear of a certain pipe in the process chamber, or metal particles leftover from the etching step. This particle shorted two traces that were supposed to be insulated, causing the pixel to display abnormally during the light test. This is the abnormal point seen by the naked eye on the screen. A real particle defect, metal bridging caused by particle contamination.

Take photos, record, export data, take out samples, close the chamber, and sign for confirmation.

The administrator glanced at the registration form: "Yield group, the actual time is 8 minutes. It ends early. The next one can go in."

The engineer standing in line next to me looked at me walking out and asked, "Why are you so fast? It took you less than 10 minutes to find the defect?"

I pointed to the detailed SEM observation plan picture on my notebook. He was stunned for a moment, and then smiled: "You are 'spending time behind the equipment'."

**Yes. This is a method devised when resources are scarce. **


This method isn't just for the lab. It also holds true in your code.

When debugging a bug, if you go directly to the oscilloscope to capture the waveform, you may not be able to find the glitch after capturing it for several hours. You don’t know what timing window the glitch appears in, and you don’t know what the trigger conditions should be. You can only "try your luck": adjust the trigger threshold higher or lower, make the time window wider or narrower, and you have to re-capture the waveform every time you try and error, and it takes several minutes each time to capture the waveform.

If you first spend an hour thinking: "In what timing window may this glitch appear? According to the circuit principle, it should be at the moment when the MOSFET switch is switched, around the rising edge or falling edge of the PWM cycle. I should set the oscilloscope trigger condition to the edge trigger of the PWM signal, and set the time window to ±500ns."

Then you caught that glitch in just 5 minutes.

You are not more efficient. You have changed the position of time, from "before the device" to "after the device". You spent an hour of thinking time away from the oscilloscope, and then only spent 5 minutes in front of the oscilloscope.

You optimize the performance of a piece of code. If you directly change the code, compile, and test, you may change it ten times without finding the bottleneck. Every time you change a place, it takes one minute to compile and five minutes to test. Ten trials and errors equal 60 minutes. And you may have made the wrong correction, you have optimized an unimportant function, and the real bottleneck is still there.

If you first spend an hour drawing an execution path diagram and analyzing the most likely bottleneck location: "This loop needs to access Flash once for each iteration. The read latency of Flash is 100ns. If the loop is executed 1000 times, it is 100μs. Other calculations only use 10μs. The bottleneck should be reading Flash."

Then you only spent 10 minutes to cache the Flash data to SRAM, and the cycle time dropped from 100μs to 10μs. One change and the problem is solved.

You are not more efficient. You have changed the time, from "before the compiler and test environment" to "after drawing and analysis".You write a driver. If you write code, burn, and debug it directly, you may not be able to run it within a day of writing. You change a few lines of code each time, burn it for 30 seconds, and test it for a few minutes. If you find something is wrong, change it again, burn it again, and test it again. Ten trials and errors equals a few hours. And you may not even know what the problem is: is the register address written wrong? Is the timing configuration wrong? Is the interrupt priority set wrong?

If you first spend an hour reading the data sheet, understand the meaning of each bit of the register, draw an initialization flow chart, and write down the register configuration value of each step: "Step 1: Configure the clock division register, value 0x03 (division factor 4). Step 2: Configure the data format register, value 0x01 (8-bit data). Step 3: Enable peripheral register, value 0x01..."

Then it only takes you 30 minutes to write the driver, burn it once, and run it.

You are not more efficient. You have changed the time, from "before the debugger and programmer" to "after the data sheet and notebook".

When your resources are scarce: when you don’t have a high-frequency oscilloscope, a cloud compilation cluster, or an automatic debugging tool, you are forced to discover this substitution relationship. You are forced to learn: **Spend time behind the equipment and save time in front of the equipment. Think before you do it, prepare before you try and make mistakes. **

When you have money, when you have a high-performance oscilloscope that can capture hundreds of thousands of frames of waveforms in real time, a cloud compiler that can compile an entire project in one minute, and an automated testing environment that can run hundreds of test cases, you may forget this replacement relationship. You would just throw the code into the compiler and let it run for ten minutes, and you wouldn't care. You would grab the waveform directly onto the oscilloscope and let it scan slowly, and you wouldn't care.

But if you have experienced those days when resources were scarce, you will retain that habit. You will habitually think before you act. You will habitually draw before burning. You will habitually read the documentation before debugging.

**This is an instinct trained by scarcity. ** This instinct allows you to be efficient when resources are sufficient, not because you are "saving", but because you know: the value of thinking is always higher than trial and error, and the value of preparation is always higher than temporary response.

This is the second lesson of scarcity of resources: **Scarcity not only makes you feel distressed about the cost, but scarcity forces you to reallocate your time, and then you find that thinking is more valuable than trial and error. **

## Code from three years ago

When you first started working, you received your first independent project. The boss said: "I'll give you three months. The hardware is this. You are the only one on the team. It's okay if you can't make it, just try your best."

Three months later you made it. You are extremely proud. When you were walking on the road after get off work that day, you felt the air was sweet. You think: I am an engineer who can complete projects independently.

Three years later you look back at that code. Variable naming is not standardized: `tmp1`, `tmp2`, `flag_ok`. No error handling: CAN sends direct `while(1)` and waits for ACK, never caring about the possibility of bus-off. The state machine has a hole: if a certain interrupt sequence is reached, the state machine jumps to an undefined state. The timing margin is too tight: the setup time between the SPI clock and data only has a 3ns margin, which may not work if the temperature is changed. CAN buffer logic is unreliable when multiple nodes are concurrent: you have not done sending priority arbitration, and low-priority messages may be starved to death. **But it ran at that time. And it has been running for three years without any fatal failure. **

Would you laugh now at yourself three years ago?

You won't. Because you know: **You, given the resources and knowledge at the time, made the optimal solution. ** At that time, you had just graduated, and you didn’t know the hundreds of rules of MISRA C, the existence of AUTOSAR, how to write TSC and TSR in ISO 26262, or the three stages of CAN’s bus recovery mechanism. But before you know all this, you’ve already made the thing. You turn an MCU from a blank chip without any code into a complete system that can control actuators, read sensors, and respond to user input.

This is remarkable. No matter how rough the code looks now, it proved your ability at that moment and proved that "people can make things with limited resources."You may not know: Hidden in that rough code are three engineering methods that you unconsciously used at the time: state machine partitioning (discretizing continuous behavior), regular inspections (replacing function call chains with hardware timer polling), and minimum viable protocols (subtraction can be done in the CAN frame format). You don’t learn these methods from textbooks, they are forced out of you by lack of resources. You may not have been a qualified software engineer three years ago, but you were a natural engineering problem solver.

## “Lack” wears different clothes in different times

An engineer from the 1950s would look at a computer from the 1990s and say: fetish. An engineer from the 1990s would look at a 3nm chip from the 2020s and say: magic.

Going further back, what could engineers in the 1850s use? Steam engines, brass parts, hand filing, coal power. If they could get their hands on an S32K144, they would think it was a relic left by an alien civilization.

Engineers in every era feel that their resources are “sufficient”. But if you travel back in time to the 1950s, here’s what you’ll notice: **Deprivation didn’t disappear, it just changed clothes. **

| Era | Main scarcity | Manifestations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1850s | Materials | Good parts are scarce, steel composition is unstable (standardized steel grades only appeared in the 1880s), everything is assembled by hand filing, and interchangeability is almost non-existent |
| 1950s | Computing power | Only vacuum tubes and a few old-fashioned electronic computers, and solving equations relied on abacus. A computer's MTBF (mean time between failures) is measured in hours. Half the vacuum tube burned out, start over |
| 1990s | Capacity | 1MB SRAM costs as much as a used car today. Flash erase and write life is measured in hundreds of times. CAN bus is a configuration of high-end cars, but low-end cars still use K-Line |
| 2020s | Time + Certainty | Project deadline 6 months. The EMC environment is extremely complex. There are seven or eight wireless protocols in the car, including GSM, 4G/5G, BLE, Wi-Fi, and V2X, all coexisting in a metal cavity. Safety compliance has grown exponentially, with 50 times more ASIL-D requirements than 20 years ago. Under the hybrid architecture of automotive Ethernet + CAN FD + LIN, the analysis of end-to-end delay can no longer rely on "empirical values" |

The 1850s lacked **material**: you couldn’t even buy good bearings and file them yourself. But this has instead created a generation of craftsmen who are extremely sensitive to mechanical tolerances and material properties.
In the 1950s, there was a shortage of computing power: partial differential equations were calculated by hand, and one parameter was updated, and it took half a month to start over. But this trained a mastery of physical intuition and dimensional analysis. They must be able to use their brains to "guess" the general direction of the answer, and then use their hands to calculate it.
In the 1990s, there was a lack of **capacity**: a complete set of engine management logic running in 128KB SRAM. But this has given rise to the most compact data structures and the most optimized memory management technology in embedded software.
The 2020s lack **time** and **certainty**: there is an infinite amount of things a chip can do, but you only have 6 months. And that chip may have a 5MHz switching power supply ripple stacked on the 3.3V power rail, and a 40A motor drive loop running next to a 10ns signal line. Your "certainty" is being lost during PCB layout.

What about tomorrow? What is missing in the 2050s?

We already have some clues. The length of the transistor channel is close to the atomic scale, and a 3nm channel is only about 10-15 silicon atoms thick. Quantum tunneling makes gate leakage current impossible to eliminate, and electrons can "wall" directly through the gate oxide layer, regardless of whether you are turning off the transistor. Power density is approaching a thermodynamic hard ceiling, and you can't cram more calculations into a smaller area without burning the chip. AI safety regulations may mandate that every engineering decision be accompanied by ethical documentation, and the cost of compliance changes from "weeks" to "months." The chemical limit of battery energy density is approaching, not because the technology is not good, but because the periodic table of elements is just that big. The theoretical upper limit of energy density of lithium-ion batteries is approximately 400 Wh/kg. We have now reached 300, which is the lower edge of the ceiling.**In the 2050s, scarce clothes will be replaced by: physical limits. ** It's not "not enough money" or "not enough computing power", but "the laws of physics don't allow it". Things you will face may be: quantum tunneling no longer allows you to continue shrinking transistors; the heat density of the chip requires microchannel liquid cooling or embedded thermoelectric refrigeration; the "explainability" of AI-driven autonomous driving decision-making systems has become a legal requirement, and you cannot refuse to swerve to avoid a pedestrian without giving an explainable reason; the system complexity of vehicle communication exceeds the understanding of any single engineer, and you can only rely on another AI to help you manage the output of the first AI.

On that day, engineers in the 2050s will also say: "People in the 2020s are so happy. At least they still have classical physics to rely on. We don't even have deterministic physics anymore. They actually made L3 autonomous driving using 28nm process, 128KB SRAM, and 500kbps CAN! 28nm!? The functional density of a chip is only one thousandth of today's chip, CAN The bandwidth of the bus is only one percent of today’s automotive Ethernet! Are they crazy?”

Lack is always there, it just keeps changing clothes. **Lack itself is not far away. Scarcity takes a different form at the feet of each generation of engineers. ** If an engineer tells you that "there is no shortage of anything in our time", he most likely does not take the problem in front of him seriously.

## Four weapons for humans to deal with eternal scarcity

If we accept the fact that resources will always be scarce, then the goal is no longer to eliminate scarcity, which is impossible. The goal is **"to produce the best possible results given the conditions of scarcity"**.

How? Humanity has honed four weapons in the history of engineering:

**1. Abstract**

Encapsulate complexity into layers. You are an AUTOSAR SWC (software component) developer, and you do not need to know whether the CAN FD CRC check polynomial is 17 bits or 21 bits. You don’t need to understand which capacitors the Flash charge pump uses to step up the voltage step by step. The layer you work on has its own concepts and boundaries. As long as the boundaries are clear, you can do your best at that level.

In automotive electronics, the most typical practice of abstraction is **layered architecture**. The MCAL layer encapsulates the LPUART register operation of S32K144 into a standard API. When the upper layer SWC calls `Uart_Write()`, there is no need to know whether the LPUART FIFO depth is 4 or 8. The CAN driver layer abstracts the mailbox configuration of the CAN controller into `Can_Write()`, and the upper layer does not need to know whether the controller has 16 mailboxes or 32. Abstraction allows people to do larger-scale designs with limited knowledge.

But abstraction doesn't just make things simple, it makes things possible. Without layering, an engineer would have to understand the register mapping of the CAN controller, the erase timing of the Flash, the charge injection effect of the ADC's sampling and holding capacitor, and the break field detection of the LIN protocol at the same time, which would be beyond the brain capacity of any engineer. Abstraction breaks thousands of knowledge nodes into several layers, and each layer only needs to understand dozens of nodes in that layer. This is a weapon against "brain deficiency".

Let’s take a more specific example. You write a function in the SWC layer that "calculates the target steering torque based on vehicle speed and steering wheel angle." You don’t need to know where the vehicle speed signal comes from: it may be the wheel speed message on CAN, it may be the pulse count of the Hall sensor, it may be the acceleration integral of the IMU. All you need to know is that `Rte_Read_VehicleSpeed(&speed)` returns a float32. One day the hardware team changed the wheel speed sensor from 48 teeth to 80 teeth, and changed the MCU from S32K144 to TC389. Your SWC code does not need to change a single line. This is the value of abstraction: it allows you to prepare for the future without knowing the future.There is a further example: **AUTOSAR's Virtual Function Bus (VFB)**. In the AUTOSAR development process, when you design a SWC, you do not need to know which SWCs will eventually be mapped to the same ECU. You define the Port (supply/demand side) and Interface (set of data elements) of your SWC on a "virtual bus". You just define "I need a VehicleSpeed ​​signal, type uint16, range 0-65535, accuracy 0.01 km/h". In the integration phase, the RTE generator reads the ARXML descriptions of all SWCs and maps signals across SWCs into function calls (if they are on the same ECU) or CAN/LIN message mapping (if they are on different ECUs). When you write SWC, you have no idea of ​​the final network topology, and your code does not need to change a single line when the topology changes. This is the highest form of abstraction at the engineering organization level: **making software independent of the physical distribution of hardware. **

**2. Standardization**

Don’t invent your own standards if you can use them. The CAN protocol has been verified by billions of nodes, so do not "micro-innovate" in the CAN frame format. The SAE J1939 and OBD-II PID lists allow diagnostics from different manufacturers to be compatible without "optimizing" the standard PID numbers. When you write a UDS diagnostic service, `0x22` is ReadDataByIdentifier and `0x2E` is WriteDataByIdentifier. Don't define a new set by yourself just because "the semantics of our projects are different". Your "micro-innovation" will render all standard diagnostics blind on your ECU.

The value of standards is not just that "others have verified it for you." It also has a deeper meaning: standards allow you to collaborate with people you don't know.  ISO 11898 defines the termination resistance (120Ω) and bit timing requirements for the CAN physical layer. Tier-1s around the world adhere to this standard, so a Bosch ABS ECU and a Continental engine ECU can communicate on the same CAN network without the need for a middleman translator. Your compliance with standards allows you to join a collaborative network of engineers from all mankind. You're providing an interface to an engineer you don't know, but who exists and is doing a serious job. He can just use what you made without knowing who you are.

Don't omit the terminal resistor just because "our bus is short". The signal reflection will suddenly collapse under a certain temperature, a certain humidity, and a certain aging state. Standards are the optimal paths left by countless predecessors who have stepped through the pits. By adhering to standards, they inherit the lessons they paid to learn. This is a weapon against "lack of trial and error resources".

**3. Toolization**

Automate things you do over and over again. Tired of manually picking up CAN dbc signals? Write a Python script to parse the DBC file and generate a structure package of C code. DBC contains the signal's start bit, length, factor, offset, min/max, and uses scripts to convert this information into C struct definitions, getter/setter functions, and boundary checking codes. Every time DBC is updated in the future, you run the script once and new code is generated. Your time is freed from repetitive work.

But tooling is about more than just “saving time.” The deeper value is: **Tools eliminate human error. ** You manually typeset signals from DBC to C code. One day you worked overtime until 10 o'clock in the evening. You were a little sleepy and wrote the length of a 12-bit signal as 16. There will be no errors or crashes, it's just that the value of a signal is silently wrong under certain edge conditions. Three months later, you spent two weeks troubleshooting and discovered that the cause was a wrong bit of manual transcoding. A script can't be wrong, it's either all right or all wrong (an obvious mistake). The rigidity of the tool turns "random human errors" into "certain logical errors", and the latter is much easier to troubleshoot.

**4. Inheritance**Write down the pitfalls you have stepped on so that your teammates will not step on them again. What you write is not just a fault analysis report, what you write is "future engineers, when you see this phenomenon, don't spend three days troubleshooting like me, just look at the third item." The technology accumulation of your company is essentially to "prevent future generations from repeating the mistakes we have made". The most expensive cost in a team is not the server or the software license, but the cost of repeated discovery. The same problem was solved three times independently by three different engineers, not because they were not smart enough, but because the first person did not record the pitfalls.

When you first enter a new field, the experience documents left by your predecessors are the most valuable wealth. These documents have evolved from the personal notes of one engineer to a survival guide that has been passed down to several engineers.

Inheritance turns one person's experience into a starting point for everyone. This is a weapon against "lack of experience".

---

Abstraction, standardization, tooling, and inheritance.  Four weapons correspond to four shortages: brain power, trial and error resources, time and reliability, and experience. They all have one thing in common: use people's initiative to amplify limited resources.

## The future looks at the present, just as the present looks at the past

There is no shame in lack. It would be a shame to give up.

This sentence is not a reproach, but a reminder to yourself. If you work overtime until late at night, fight against the oscilloscope on weekends, and try to run Flash wear leveling for the Nth time, you are using your subjective initiative to fight against the eternal resource boundaries.  This is the bounden duty of engineers.

It's very difficult for you to see the seniors who have two bombs and one satellite now. But the seniors themselves don’t think so, they are just solving problems. Just like if you use 32KB Flash for OTA upgrade solution now, you don't feel the pain, you just think: 8KB is reserved for the bootloader, 22KB is reserved for the App, and the middle 2KB is used as swap buffer. Is it enough? Not even close. Then press it again.

100 years later, your descendants said to historical data:

**"Engineers in the 2020s used 28nm process, 128KB SRAM, and 500kbps CAN bus to create a brake-by-wire controller that meets ASIL D. How did they do it?"**

They will think that what we create with limited resources is great. They will have their own deprivations. Maybe it’s because quantum tunneling can’t be reduced any further, maybe it’s because power density is approaching the hard ceiling of physics, maybe it’s because AI safety regulations require all engineering decisions to be ethically justified, but they may look at us like we look at two bombs and one satellite.

Today, you are dealing with the resource allocation of 128KB SRAM, the real-time constraints of the CAN bus, and the desperate deduction of Flash wear leveling. These are not obstacles to your career. **These are the reasons why you will be remembered by future engineers. **

**"The future looks at the present, just as the present looks at the past"**. What Wang Xizhi said 1670 years ago is the most accurate prediction in the engineering world. Each generation feels the hardships of the previous generation, and each generation is lamented by the next generation. And every generation never gave up.

You may ask: If scarcity is eternal, what’s the point of all this we do?

The answer is: scarcity is not a curse. Scarcity is the driving force. It is precisely because of scarcity that you are forced to think of ways that others do not need to think of. And after your method is recorded, it becomes the starting point for the next generation.

Lack is eternal, but so is human subjective initiative.
