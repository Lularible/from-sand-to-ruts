# 2.4 CMOS: The Wisdom of Complementary Pairs

## A leak-proof water system

Suppose you have two simple things in your hand: a faucet (normally closed, water will flow out when the lever is pulled), and a plug (normally plugged, water will leak when pulled out).

You install the faucet on the water pipe, which is the "high side" and connects to the water source. You install the plug in the drain, which is the "low side" and connects the drain.

Now link these two things. Use a link to control them both simultaneously: when the faucet is turned on, the stopper blocks the drain (water does not flow directly from the water pipe to the drain. The water goes to where you want it to be used). While the faucet is closed, the plug opens the drain (draining the remaining water in the pipe without accumulating water).

What do you get? A water system that neither leaks nor accumulates water. There is no waste of water under normal circumstances. Only at the moment of switching. The moment when the faucet is not fully opened and the plug is not completely blocked. There is a brief flow of water directly from the source to the drain.

**This is CMOS. ** Two complementary switches form a pair. One is connected to VDD (PMOS) and the other is connected to GND (NMOS). One is on while the other is off. Static power consumption is almost zero.

The C of CMOS stands for Complementary. It is not the complementarity of opposites, but the complementarity of synergy. Just like the Yin Yang Tai Chi diagram. There is white in black, black in white, dynamic balance and static zero.

## CMOS inverter - the most basic building block of digital circuits

A CMOS inverter has only two MOSFETs. The above PMOS: the source is connected to VDD and the drain is connected to the output. The next NMOS: the drain is connected to the output and the source is connected to GND. The gates of the two MOSFETs are connected together. This is the input.

```
          VDD
           |
PMOS | (pull-up network)
    IN ---+--- OUT
    NMOS | (pull-down network)
           |
          GND
```

When IN = 0 (0V): V_GS of PMOS = -VDD → PMOS turns on (PMOS turns on when gate is low). V_GS of NMOS = 0 → NMOS cutoff. OUT is connected to VDD through conducting PMOS → OUT = VDD = logic 1.

When IN = 1 (VDD): V_GS of PMOS = 0 → PMOS cut-off. V_GS of NMOS = VDD → NMOS is turned on. OUT is connected to GND via conducting NMOS → OUT = 0V = logic 0.

Regardless of whether IN is 0 or 1, PMOS and NMOS do not turn on at the same time. In static state, there is no DC path from VDD to GND. Theoretically the power consumption is zero. There is a little bit of leakage in reality: sub-threshold leakage (the transistor also has a weak pA-nA level of current in the "off" state), gate leakage (electrons tunneling through the oxide layer in nanoscale processes), and PN junction reverse leakage. However, on the 40nm-90nm processes commonly used in automotive MCUs, these static leakages are at the nanoampere (nA) level at the level of a single inverter.

The CMOS inverter does not require input current (gate insulation), consumes no static power (complementary switching of P and N tubes), and has extremely fast switching speed (limited only by the charging and discharging of the gate capacitance). These three properties make it the ultimate building block for digital circuits.

## Voltage transfer characteristics - the amount of information in the VTC curve

The Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC) of an inverter is a graph of V_IN versus V_OUT. The horizontal axis is the input voltage sweeping from 0 to VDD, and the vertical axis is the change in the output voltage.

The ideal VTC is a **step function**: when V_IN is lower than VDD/2, V_OUT = VDD; when V_IN is higher than VDD/2, V_OUT = 0. It's clean and the switching point is exactly VDD/2.Real VTC is not that sharp, it is a smooth S-shaped curve. When V_IN starts to rise from 0, V_OUT remains flat near VDD, PMOS is fully on, and NMOS is fully off. When V_IN approaches V_TH_N (the threshold voltage of NMOS), NMOS begins to conduct weakly and the OUT voltage begins to decrease. In a certain interval when V_IN is close to VDD/2, PMOS and NMOS are turned on at the same time. All are in the saturation zone or linear zone. The inverter exhibits high gain and V_OUT drops sharply. Then when V_IN exceeds VDD - |V_TH_P|, PMOS is completely cut off and V_OUT drops to 0.

The VTC curve gives several key design parameters:

**Switching Threshold V_M**: The point at which V_IN = V_OUT. The ideal is VDD/2. If the driving capabilities of PMOS and NMOS are asymmetric, V_M will be biased towards the stronger side. Too much bias in V_M can result in asymmetric noise margins.

Noise Margin: How high the noise voltage at the input can be without changing the output logic value. Noise margin is defined as the side length of the largest square between the VTC curve and its mirror curve. High noise margin (NM_H) = V_OH_min - V_IH_min, low noise margin (NM_L) = V_IL_max - V_OL_max. The larger the noise margin, the less sensitive the circuit is to power supply noise, crosstalk, and ground bounce. In automotive chips, the EMC (Electromagnetic Compatibility, ability to resist external electromagnetic interference and not interfere with others) environment is extremely harsh. Noise margin is a key specification of the design.

**Transition area width**: The input voltage range corresponding to V_OUT falling from 90% VDD to 10% VDD. The narrower the transition region (steeper the VTC), the higher the gain and the greater the noise margin. The transition region of the CMOS inverter can be within 100mV. This means that the output can flip from logic 1 to logic 0 with only a change in input voltage of about 100mV.

Fan-out: How many subsequent gate inputs can be driven by the output of an inverter. Fanout is determined by drive capability and load capacitance. Load capacitance = N × C_in (input capacitance of driven gate) + wiring parasitic capacitance. An inverter with greater drive capability can drive more gates. But it will also consume more power and area. In high-speed digital circuits, the fanout of gates on the critical path is usually limited to between 3 and 5.

**Propagation Delay (t_p)**: The time difference between the input flipping 50% and the output flipping 50%. t_p is determined by the charge and discharge current and output load capacitance: t_p ≈ C_L × VDD / I_D. Means to reduce delay: increase transistor width (increase I_D), reduce load capacitance (reduce the input capacitance of the driven gate), reduce VDD (but the lower the voltage, the smaller I_D. There is an optimal value).

This is information that can be derived from a simple VTC diagram of a CMOS inverter. Circuit design. From schematic to layout. Essentially it's a trade-off between various parameters on this graph: speed vs power vs area vs noise margin.

## Three Accounts: Where does the power consumption of the chip come from?

The static power consumption of CMOS is close to zero, which is a textbook ideal. Real chip power consumption comes from three sources.

The first item: dynamic power consumption (switching power, Switching Power). This is the largest single expense. Once the inverter flips, the load capacitance C_L at the output needs to be charged or discharged. During charging, current flows from VDD to C_L through PMOS, charging the capacitor from 0V to VDD. The energy is taken out of the power supply, half is stored in the capacitor (½ C_L V²), and the other half is dissipated in the channel resistance of the PMOS and becomes Joule heat. When discharging, ½ C_L V² stored in the capacitor is discharged to GND through the NMOS, and all becomes Joule heat. Therefore, for each complete charge and discharge cycle (0→1→0), the total energy consumption is C_L × VDD².If the chip is running at frequency f, the number of flips per second is approximately f × α. α is the **activity factor** (activity factor), which represents the probability of a complete 0→1→0 flip of a node within one clock cycle. So dynamic power consumption:

P_dynamic = α × C_L × VDD² × f

More than 90% of the power dissipated in digital circuits is typically dynamic power. Typical dynamic power consumption for a Cortex-M4 running at 112MHz, 1.2V is about 50-100 mW. What is this 100mW spent on? It is spent on "charging and discharging the gate capacitance and wiring capacitance of hundreds of thousands to millions of nodes on the chip." Every clock cycle, every microsecond, hundreds or thousands of nodes are flipping.

The code you write is "busier": the main loop keeps running, interrupts keep triggering, and DMA keeps moving data. The higher α, the higher the power consumption. When you do low-power optimization and turn off unused peripheral clocks (clock gating), you are essentially reducing α to zero on that part of the circuit. When the clock stops, those nodes will not flip.

The second item: short-circuit power consumption (through power consumption, Short-circuit Power). CMOS has no DC path in static state. This is for static. But at the moment of switching, when the input voltage is between V_TH_N and VDD - |V_TH_P|, PMOS and NMOS will be weakly turned on at the same time. Like a "draft", current flows from VDD directly through PMOS and NMOS to GND.

The short-circuit energy dissipated by an inverter in a single flip is approximately 5-15% of C_L × VDD². But if the rise/fall time of the input signal is very long (for example, due to insufficient driving capability), this "simultaneous conduction" time window will be lengthened. Short-circuit power consumption may rise to 20-30%. This is why you need to pay attention to the slew rate of the signal when doing high-speed digital design. A gentle slope is not only noisy (because the mid-level PMOS/NMOS has a long common conduction time and is sensitive to external noise), but also consumes power.

The third item: static power consumption (leakage power).

P_leakage = VDD × I_leak

I_leak includes:
- Subthreshold Leakage: Even if V_GS = 0, the transistor still has a weak exponential current tail leaking. Under the 40nm process, the sub-threshold leakage of a single tube is about 1-10 nA. It sounds small, but there are millions to hundreds of millions of transistors on a die. 1nA × 100 million = 100mA. This is the root of the "dark silicon" problem.
- Gate Leakage: Direct leakage from gate to channel caused by quantum tunneling has been greatly suppressed in the HKMG process.
- Junction Leakage: PN junction reverse leakage current from source/drain to substrate. Typically small (fA-pA range), but increases exponentially at high temperatures, with leakage approximately doubling for every 10°C increase in temperature.

Three sources combined:

P_total = α × C_L × VDD² × f + P_short + P_leak

For automotive ECUs, power consumption management in real-world scenarios focuses on these three items:- Drop the voltage by half and the dynamic power consumption drops by a factor of four (because of the VDD² term). This is the fundamental driving force for the core voltage to go down from 5V → 3.3V → 1.8V → 1.2V → 0.9V.
- As the frequency decreases, dynamic power consumption decreases linearly. So MCU has dozens of clock division options: core clock, bus clock, peripheral clock. It allows you to attach different speed clocks to different peripherals. Don't give high-speed clocks to peripherals that do not need to be fully used.
- The activity factor drops to zero: turn off the clock gating of unused modules, and that part of the circuit consumes no dynamic power at all.
- Reduce sub-threshold leakage: use power gating to directly cut off the power supply to unused modules. But power gating is expensive. It takes time to restore power (wake-up delay).
- Dynamic Voltage Frequency Scaling (DVFS): reduces voltage and frequency at low loads and increases at high loads. This is standard on mobile phones, but not common on automotive MCUs. Because automobiles have strict real-time requirements, the switching delay of voltage frequency may not be acceptable.

## Dennard Scaling – a prophecy that came true and then collapsed

In 1974, IBM's Robert Dennard published a landmark paper. He pointed out a beautiful scaling law: if the transistor size is scaled down k times (k > 1), the power density can remain constant.

The derivation is roughly this:

- The transistor size is reduced k times → the area is reduced to 1/k² → k² times the transistors can be placed.
- The voltage also shrinks to 1/k (the electric field remains unchanged. The oxide layer is thinned k times, and VDD is also scaled k times).
- The oxide layer is thinned k times → the gate capacitance C also shrinks to 1/k (C = εA/t, A shrinks to 1/k², t shrinks to 1/k → C shrinks to 1/k).
- The frequency is increased by k times (the signal propagation distance is shortened by k times, but the electric field remains unchanged → the carrier speed remains unchanged, the propagation time is reduced to 1/k → the frequency is increased by k times).

Substituting P = αCV²f:
C → 1/k. V² → 1/k². f → k.
Multiply that up: the power dissipation of a single transistor becomes **1/k²**.

But k² times as many transistors can be put on the chip, so the total power consumption of the chip remains **the same**.

This is Dennard’s Law of Scaling, which explains why from the 1970s to the mid-2000s, chip frequencies soared (a few MHz → several GHz) without the chip’s power consumption exploding. From Intel 8086 (3μm) to Pentium 4 (90nm), each generation of process can achieve higher frequencies under the same power budget.

However, around 2005, around 90nm to 65nm, this law collapsed.

The reason for the collapse is: **The voltage cannot be infinitely reduced. **

The threshold voltage V_TH cannot be scaled proportionally. If V_TH is too low, the sub-threshold leakage current will skyrocket exponentially and the transistor will not turn off. The operating voltage VDD must be higher than V_TH by a certain "overdrive" margin (overdrive) in order to maintain sufficient switching speed. So VDD no longer drops significantly around 1V.

At the same time, the gate oxide layer cannot shrink indefinitely. Quantum tunneling increases sharply below 2nm, and the gate leakage current becomes out of control if the thickness is further reduced. Therefore, DENNARD’s step of “proportional thinning of the oxide layer” also stopped.

When VDD and oxide layer thickness no longer shrink, but the number of transistors is still doubling (the density of Moore's Law continues to increase), the maximum power density begins to increase.  This is the origin of the "dark silicon" phenomenon. You can't have all the transistors on the chip operating at full speed at the same time because the heat generated can't be dissipated. You can only run some areas at full speed, while other areas are shut down or throttled down. The big.LITTLE architecture (ARM) of mobile chips, Turbo Boost and thermal throttling of CPUs are all compromises that were forced after the collapse of Dennard scaling.Automotive chips rarely run at the forefront of process nodes. Most automotive MCUs are still at 90nm to 40nm, and the cutting edge of Dennard scaling collapse is just in front of them. This is actually an advantage.  Using a slightly backward manufacturing process, we get better heat distribution, lower leakage, higher reliability, and a more mature design library. This is the fundamental reason why "mature processes" survive well in automotive electronics.

## Why are automotive chips willing to use a "backward generation" process?

You may be thinking: Why don’t car-grade chips use 5nm? Wouldn't the performance be better?

The answer is: not "can't", but "shouldn't".

First, **leakage**. The sub-threshold leakage of more advanced processes (7nm, 5nm, 3nm) is tens to hundreds of times that of 40nm. Even if a 5nm chip is in standby mode, its static power consumption may be in the hundreds of milliwatts. This is barely acceptable for a mobile phone. You can just go back and charge it at night. It is unacceptable for cars. The total dark current of the ECU of the entire The car after the engine is turned off must be < 10mA (about 120mW), otherwise the battery will be empty overnight. Using 40nm or 90nm as an automotive MCU, static leakage can be controlled at the μA level. The sleep current of the entire chip may be only a dozen μA.

Second, **voltage**. More advanced processes require lower VDD, around 0.7V to 0.9V. But the power supply environment in the car is 12V (passenger cars) or 24V (commercial vehicles). It is already difficult to reduce it to the 1.2V or 3.3V you need, and then to 0.8V. LDO or DC-DC efficiency, noise, reliability will all deteriorate. Moreover, low voltage means that the signal swing of the analog circuit is small and the signal-to-noise ratio decreases. With your 12-bit ADC at 1.2V reference, one LSB is 0.29mV. If using a 0.8V reference, one LSB is 0.20mV. The margins for both thermal and power supply noise are smaller.

Third, **reliability**. Devices with more advanced processes age faster, and degradation mechanisms such as NBTI, HCI, and TDDB (time-dependent breakdown) are more significant in nanoscale processes. A 5nm transistor running 1000 hours of HTOL at 125°C may experience parameter drift of 10-15%. A 40nm transistor might only drift 2-3% under the same conditions. Car-spec chips must keep parameters within spec within 15 years. It’s clear who is more reliable.

Fourth, **maturity**. 40nm has been in mass production for more than ten years. Data from billions of chips have been accumulated, and the entire process window has been explored. Design rules (DRC rules) are verified through hundreds of tapeouts. The model accuracy is very high, and the deviation between SPICE simulation and actual silicon is very small. The yield rate is stable, and a yield rate of more than 90% can be maintained continuously. For Tier-1s doing ASIL-D safetyware: they don’t want “new”, they want “sure”.

Therefore, using 40nm for automotive chips is not backward, but rational.

## My favorite thing when doing failure analysis is slicing

This is my personal experience, failure analysis from the perspective of a yield engineer.

In fab, when the yield is abnormal and parameter testing and optical inspection alone are not enough to determine the root cause, the next step is to do physical failure analysis (PFA). The first step in PFA is usually slicing.

Slicing is to use a focused ion beam (FIB, Focused Ion Beam) to cut a section at a specific position of the die, and then observe the section with a scanning electron microscope (SEM). FIB bombards the surface of silicon with a beam of gallium ions, like an atomic-scale knife. Silicon, silicon oxide, metal, and even packaging materials can be peeled off layer by layer with nanometer-level precision.

The first time I looked at the cross-section of the chip I cut on the SEM, I was shocked.

In the black and white image of the SEM, the chip is no longer a bland black square. It is a sophisticated, layered, and deep micro-city. The bottom silicon substrate is like a gray, uniform ocean. Above is the transistor layer. The gates of each transistor are like rows of neatly arranged buildings. The gate polysilicon is surrounded by silicon oxide. Above that are more than a dozen layers of metal interconnection lines, with copper wires running through the silica medium, like a subway tunnel beneath the city. Between the layers are densely packed tungsten vias (vias), which are vertical channels that connect adjacent metal layers.

This small piece of silicon expands into a three-dimensional, city-like microscopic world under SEM.

One time, a die had a weird failure mode. Full pass for normal temperature testing, and fail for large area testing at high temperature (125°C). Parametric testing pointed to a short circuit from VDD to GND. But which position is short?

We sent the die for Emission Microscopy (EMMI) analysis. To power up the die, there should be a high impedance between VDD and GND. But on the EMMI image, a tiny spot of light appeared. That is the near-infrared photon emitted by the recombination of electrons and holes, and the position of the light spot marks the short-circuit point.

Then FIB cut a section at that location and saw it under SEM.

The spacing between two adjacent metal lines should be ≥ 0.25μm. But on the SEM image, the distance between these two lines is almost zero in a certain section. There is a thin metal bridge spanning the two lines. The bridge is only about 50nm wide and cannot be seen with an optical microscope, nor can it be scanned out in a conventional AOI (automated optical inspection). But under SEM, it couldn’t be clearer. A 50nm wide metal filament shorts VDD and GND.

What causes this? The chemical composition of the bridge was further analyzed using EDX (Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy). Mainly copper with a small amount of tantalum (tantalum is the diffusion barrier material in the copper interconnect process). This suggests that the bridge is formed after copper CMP but before tantalum barrier deposition. It may be that the remaining copper debris from CMP was not cleaned and was "buried" into the dielectric layer in the next deposition process.

This defect does not cause a short circuit at room temperature. Because of differences in thermal expansion coefficients, the bridge may be under slight mechanical stress at room temperature and just break. But when heated to 125°C, copper thermally expands more than the surrounding silica. The bridge was "squeezed" onto the metal wire next to it and connected.

This is why this chip passes at normal temperatures and fails at high temperatures. It's not a bug in the electrical design, it's a flaw in the physical manufacturing.

A speck of dust, 70nm copper debris, can destroy the entire circuit. And this chip was left behind in one of the cleaning steps among hundreds of processes.

Every time I recall that SEM image, of that thin 50nm bridge between two copper wires, I feel a mixture of emotions. On one side is awe: humans can make things so small and so precise. The other aspect is humility: Do anything at an atomic scale, and any tiny misstep is magnified into a fatal flaw.

## From two MOSFETs to one car

One of the most beautiful properties of CMOS logic: infinite complexity can emerge from the repetition of simple rules.

One NMOS + one PMOS = inverter. Inverter × several = NAND gate (two PMOS in parallel + two NMOS in series), NOR gate (two PMOS in series + two NMOS in parallel). NAND gates × dozens = adders, multiplexers, ALUs. ALU + registers + control state machines × thousands = CPU. CPU + on-chip SRAM + Flash + peripherals = MCU. MCU × 100 + CAN/LIN/FlexRay + sensors + actuators = vehicle electronic architecture.

From two MOSFETs to a car running at 120km/h on the road and relying on dozens of ECUs working together, there is no magic in between. Each step is deterministic, understandable, and composed of simple building blocks.

This is the beauty of engineering, not "unanalyzable complexity" but "analyzable complexity". You can go from the top-level model all the way to the bottom-level transistor, and each layer can be rigorously derived, simulated, and predicted. Your car is doing adaptive cruise on the highway. Radar data → CAN message → MCU interrupt → Algorithm calculation → GPIO output → Brake action. This entire chain can ultimately be broken down into the flipping of hundreds of thousands of CMOS logic gates.

Taoism teaches that "Tao generates one, which generates two, two generates three, and three generates all things." In the CMOS world: CMOS → logic gate → functional module → chip → system → everything.

Every abnormal level, every timing violation, every dropped CAN message you see when debugging an ECU. Their root causes can ultimately be traced back to certain MOSFETs not being connected to the correct voltage at the correct time. Because this is the physical nature of all digital circuits: the thin layer of silicon oxide between the input and output of a CMOS inverter.

You are making software, but your software runs on physics. And physics is defined by MOSFETs.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: understand the wisdom of CMOS "complementarity". Why two complementary switches paired together become the ultimate building blocks of digital circuits.

Key takeaways:
1. The essence of CMOS is that "one is on while the other is off": there is no DC path from VDD to GND under static conditions, and the power consumption is almost zero. This is the physical basis for the entire digital world to run on batteries.
2. There are three sources of chip power consumption: dynamic power consumption (CV²f), short-circuit power consumption, and static leakage. The collapse of Dennard scaling tells us that in the nanometer era, the voltage can no longer drop infinitely.
3. It is a rational choice for car-grade chips to use the "backward generation" process: lower leakage, higher voltage tolerance, and slower aging. Reliability over performance.
