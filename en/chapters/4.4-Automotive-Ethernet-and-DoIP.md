# 4.4 Automotive Ethernet and DoIP

## 500MB firmware transferred via CAN - more than two hours

In 2015, a German OEM did a calculation problem.

An OTA firmware package for ADAS domain controller: 500MB. The effective bandwidth of the CAN bus (deducting interframe space, CRC, ACK, stuffing bits, protocol overhead): approximately 500kbps. Transmission time: 500×8÷0.5=8000 seconds, more than two hours. It's actually worse, because there are dozens of ECUs communicating simultaneously on CAN, and your firmware frames have to be ranked behind the brake signal, engine status, and steering angle.

Moreover, Level 3 autonomous driving test vehicles generate TB-level sensor raw data every day, averaging about 100Mbps; even if CAN-FD is used to reach 8Mbps, the difference is still more than an order of magnitude.

There is another issue to consider: CAN is a signal bus. Its frames are broadcast, with only IDs but no addresses, no routes, and no sessions. If you want to flash an ECU, you must first establish a point-to-point connection, which is not supported by CAN.

**The answer is Automotive Ethernet. ** It’s not “faster CAN”, it’s a different species.

## CAN is a signal bus and Ethernet is a data network

**These are two different species. **

What CAN transmits is a "signal": the value of a physical quantity (speed 1847rpm, gear P, brake light ON). You can think of it as a distributed read/write system for shared variables. The address is the CAN ID and the value is the 8-byte payload. No sessions, no connections, no service discovery. The sender does not know who is receiving, and the receiver does not know who is sending (only the ID is known). This is signal level communication.

Ethernet transmits "data": data packets have source IP and destination IP, TCP/UDP ports, and application layer protocol headers (HTTP/SOME/IP/DoIP). It supports addressing (router knows the subnet), routing (RIP/OSPF determines the path), segmentation reassembly (IP fragmentation), flow control (TCP sliding window), QoS (DSCP priority). Ethernet doesn't care whether you're sending engine speed or NBA live broadcasts, it's a pipeline for data.

**This distinction is not academic: it determines the electronic and electrical architecture (E/E Architecture) of the entire vehicle. **

The EE architecture of classic CAN models is the "signal matrix": a huge DBC table (sometimes more than 3000 signals) listing all signals exchanged between all ECUs. Engineers wrote down "who sends what", "who receives what", and "how many milliseconds the cycle is" in the design stage. To add a new ECU, you have to update the DBC matrix of dozens of ECUs in the entire vehicle. It's static, predefined, and fragile.

The EE architecture of Ethernet models is a "service network": based on SOME/IP (Scalable Service-Oriented Middleware over IP). The ECU does not need to know at compile time who will receive its data. It only needs to register a service (such as "CameraStreamService"), and other ECUs dynamically discover and subscribe through SOME/IP-SD (Service Discovery). New sensor coming online? Just subscribe. This is dynamic and scalable.

**The car has changed from a "machine with a CPU" to a "distributed computing system with four wheels." **

## One line, full duplex, 100Mbps

Traditional Ethernet (100BASE-TX) requires two pairs of differential lines: a transmit pair and a receive pair, plus a shield, for a total of four conductors. Cat5e cable diameter is about 5mm. If dozens of Ethernet nodes in a vehicle all use traditional Ethernet, the weight and cost of the wiring harness will be unacceptable.

**100BASE-T1 (IEEE 802.3bw) uses only one pair of unshielded twisted pair (UTP)** and performs full-duplex transmission on the same pair of lines. The conductor cross-sectional area is about 0.35mm² and the total diameter is about 1.5mm, which is 70% thinner than traditional Ethernet cables. This is the diameter of the wire that can pass through the wheel arch.

The separation of sending and receiving relies on **Echo Cancellation**. The principle is this:The transmitter inside the PHY chip drives the signal to be sent (S_TX) onto the twisted pair. At the same time, what the receiver sees on this twisted pair is a mixed signal: S_TX (local transmission signal, "echo" after the change in cable impedance) + S_RX (signal sent from the remote end). The receiver needs to subtract S_TX from the mixed signal, leaving only S_RX.

**The echo canceller is an adaptive FIR filter. ** It passes the local digital transmit signal (known) through a digital filter with adjustable coefficients to generate an estimate of the echo. This estimate is subtracted from the received mixed signal, and what remains is the far-end signal plus residual error. The filter coefficients adaptively converge through the training sequence (pseudo-random sequence) when the link is established, and are continuously fine-tuned during data transmission.

This training process is called "link synchronization". When two 100BASE-T1 PHYs are connected via a UTP pair, they send training sequences to each other and converge their own echo cancellers and adaptive equalizers independently of each other. After convergence, the link is established and valid data begins to be transmitted. The whole process takes about 100-200ms.

**The encoding uses PAM-3**, each symbol has three levels: +1, 0, -1. In theory, each symbol can carry log₂(3)≈1.58 bits. 3B/2T mapping is actually used (3 bit→2 three-level symbols), which is equivalent to 1.5 bit/symbol; at the 66.7Mbaud symbol rate, 66.7Mbaud×1.5=100Mbps. The "encoding overhead" here comes from 3B/2T mapping, not 8b/10b.

Why PAM-3 instead of PAM-2 (two-level) or PAM-4 (four-level)? PAM-2 can only run 66.7Mbps at the same symbol rate, which is less than 100Mbps. PAM-4 can run 133Mbps at the same symbol rate. Although four levels require more precise level determination, in the EMI environment of the car, the eye diagram opening of PAM-4 will close faster. PAM-3 is the optimal compromise between "bandwidth efficiency" and "noise tolerance".

Another ingenious design of PAM-3: with scrambling, the three levels (+1, 0, -1) are statistically nearly uniformly distributed without long-term DC bias, which means there is almost no DC component and no bulky shielding layer is needed to suppress low-frequency radiation.

**On the physical layer, 100BASE-T1 is already a complete DSP system. ** Adaptive equalization, echo cancellation, clock recovery, and PAM-3 decoding are all completed inside the PHY silicon chip, and the power consumption is usually in the order of hundreds of milliwatts. You as a firmware engineer don't see it, and you don't need to see it. But you know it's there. The moment you plug in the Ethernet cable, the DSP core inside the PHY is fine-tuning hundreds of filter coefficients, using the least mean square (LMS) algorithm to converge to echo suppression below −40dB in tens of milliseconds.

## MAC layer: pipeline from PHY to CPU

The output of the PHY chip is MII (Media Independent Interface): a set of parallel signals: 4-bit wide RXD[3:0], 4-bit wide TXD[3:0], RX_CLK, TX_CLK, RX_DV (data valid), TX_EN (transmit enable). The clock of MII is 25MHz in 100Mbps mode, 25MHz×4bit=100Mbps.

The MAC layer is usually a hardware module inside the MCU/SoC. It assembles MII's 4-bit nibble stream into Ethernet frames (adds preamble, SFD, adds FCS/CRC32 check, removes preamble), manages transmit and receive FIFOs, processes flow control PAUSE frames, and generates transmission completion/reception completion interrupts.

For MCUs with ENET such as S32K148, it may only have a 10/100Mbps Ethernet MAC (ENET module). The MAC moves the received frame to the buffer pointed to by the receive descriptor (descriptor ring) in SRAM through internal DMA. Your code gets the frame buffer pointer from the descriptor ring and starts parsing the Ethernet header, IP header, and TCP/UDP header.

**This is the entire link from PHY to socket:**

```
Twisted pair (PAM-3 analog signal)
    ↓
PHY (echo cancellation + adaptive equalization + clock recovery + PAM-3 decoding)
    ↓
MII/RMII (4-bit/2-bit parallel digital)
    ↓
MAC (frame assembly/disassembly + CRC + DMA to descriptor ring)
    ↓
ENET interrupt → ISR → notify protocol stack
    ↓
TCP/IP stack (lwIP/uIP/custom): Verify IP header, reassemble fragments, match TCP ports, verify TCP checksum
    ↓
Socket layer (recv/send)
    ↓
your application code
```## Penetration: A vehicle-mounted UDP Echo Server

The following is an example of running a UDP echo server on an ECU using a simplified protocol stack style (similar to lwIP's raw API). This is not standard lwIP, but shows the data structure of each layer in a bare-metal way:

```c
// ========== Ethernet frame structure (simplified) ==========
#include <stdint.h>
#include <string.h>

static uint16_t ntohs(uint16_t n);
static uint16_t htons(uint16_t h);
static uint32_t htonl(uint32_t h);

// Ethernet header: 14 bytes
typedef struct {
    uint8_t  dst_mac[6];
    uint8_t  src_mac[6];
    uint16_t ethertype;   // 0x0800 = IPv4
} __attribute__((packed)) eth_hdr_t;

// IP header: 20 bytes (no options)
typedef struct {
    uint8_t  ver_ihl;     // Version(4) + IHL(4) = 0x45
    uint8_t  dscp_ecn;
    uint16_t total_len;
    uint16_t id;
    uint16_t flags_frag;
    uint8_t  ttl;
    uint8_t  protocol;    // 17 = UDP
    uint16_t hdr_checksum;
    uint32_t src_ip;
    uint32_t dst_ip;
} __attribute__((packed)) ip_hdr_t;

// UDP header: 8 bytes
typedef struct {
    uint16_t src_port;
    uint16_t dst_port;
    uint16_t length;      // UDP header + total data length
    uint16_t checksum;
} __attribute__((packed)) udp_hdr_t;

// complete framebuffer
static uint8_t rx_frame[1536];  // Maximum Ethernet frame

// ========== IP Checksum ==========
// RFC 1071: 16-bit one’s complement sum
static uint16_t ip_checksum(void *data, uint16_t len)
{
    uint32_t sum = 0;
    uint16_t *p = (uint16_t *)data;
    while (len > 1) {
        sum += *p++;
        len -= 2;
    }
    if (len) sum += *(uint8_t *)p;           // odd byte pad
    sum = (sum >> 16) + (sum & 0xffff);      // Carry is added to the lower 16 bits
    sum += (sum >> 16);                      // One more carry
    return (uint16_t)(~sum);                 // negate
}

// ========== IP pseudo header + UDP checksum ==========
typedef struct {
    uint32_t src_ip;
    uint32_t dst_ip;
    uint8_t  zero;
    uint8_t  protocol;
    uint16_t udp_len;
} __attribute__((packed)) udp_pseudo_hdr_t;

static uint16_t udp_checksum(ip_hdr_t *ip, udp_hdr_t *udp)
{
    uint16_t udp_total = ntohs(udp->length);  // Network byte order → host byte order
    uint8_t buf[sizeof(udp_pseudo_hdr_t) + udp_total + 1];
    udp_pseudo_hdr_t *pseudo = (udp_pseudo_hdr_t *)buf;

    pseudo->src_ip   = ip->src_ip;
    pseudo->dst_ip   = ip->dst_ip;
    pseudo->zero     = 0;
    pseudo->protocol = 17;  // UDP
    pseudo->udp_len  = udp->length;

    memcpy(buf + sizeof(udp_pseudo_hdr_t), udp, udp_total);

    // If the UDP data length is an odd number, 0x00 is added at the end (UDP checksum specification)
    if (udp_total & 1)
        buf[sizeof(udp_pseudo_hdr_t) + udp_total] = 0x00;

    return ip_checksum(buf, sizeof(udp_pseudo_hdr_t) + udp_total + (udp_total & 1));
}

// ========== Byte order conversion ==========
static uint16_t ntohs(uint16_t n) {
    return ((n & 0xff) << 8) | ((n >> 8) & 0xff);
}
static uint16_t htons(uint16_t h) { return ntohs(h); }
static uint32_t htonl(uint32_t h) {
    return ((h & 0xff) << 24) | ((h & 0xff00) << 8)
         | ((h >> 8) & 0xff00) | ((h >> 24) & 0xff);
}

// ========== UDP Echo Server ==========
// Called in ENET receive interrupt
// rx_frame has been filled with a complete Ethernet frame via MAC DMA

#define UDP_ECHO_PORT 0xe001 // 57345, optional port

// ECU's MAC and IP address (assumed already set)
static uint8_t  my_mac[6] = {0x02, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00, 0x01};
static uint32_t my_ip   = 0xc0a8000a;  // 192.168.0.10 (host endian)

void enet_rx_handler(uint8_t *frame, uint16_t len)
{
    // 1. Parse the Ethernet header
    eth_hdr_t *eth = (eth_hdr_t *)frame;
    if (ntohs(eth->ethertype) != 0x0800) return;  // Not IPv4, discarded

    // 2. Parse IP header
    ip_hdr_t *ip = (ip_hdr_t *)(frame + sizeof(eth_hdr_t));
    if (ip->protocol != 17) return;  // Not UDP, discarded
    if (htonl(ip->dst_ip) != my_ip) return; // Not sent to me, discard it

    // 3. Verify IP header checksum
    uint16_t ip_hdr_csum = ip_checksum(ip, (ip->ver_ihl & 0x0f) * 4);
    if (ip_hdr_csum != 0x0000 && ip_hdr_csum != 0xffff) return;

    // 4. Parse UDP header
    udp_hdr_t *udp = (udp_hdr_t *)((uint8_t *)ip + sizeof(ip_hdr_t));
    if (ntohs(udp->dst_port) != UDP_ECHO_PORT) return;  // Not our port

    // 5. Extract UDP data
    uint8_t  *udp_data = (uint8_t *)udp + sizeof(udp_hdr_t);
    uint16_t  udp_datalen = ntohs(udp->length) - sizeof(udp_hdr_t);

    // 6. Construct Echo response: exchange src/dst
    //    Modify directly on the original frame to avoid copying

    // 6a. Swap MAC addresses
    memcpy(eth->dst_mac, eth->src_mac, 6);
    memcpy(eth->src_mac, my_mac, 6);

    // 6b. Swap IP addresses
    ip->dst_ip = ip->src_ip;
    ip->src_ip = htonl(my_ip);
    ip->ttl = 64;     // New TTL
    ip->id = 0x0001;  // Any ID
    ip->hdr_checksum = 0;
    ip->hdr_checksum = ip_checksum(ip, sizeof(ip_hdr_t));

    // 6c. Swap UDP ports
    udp->dst_port = udp->src_port;
    udp->src_port = htons(UDP_ECHO_PORT);
    // UDP payload remains unchanged, it is echo
    udp->checksum = 0;
    udp->checksum = udp_checksum(ip, udp);

    // 7. Send via ENET MAC
    uint16_t eth_len = sizeof(eth_hdr_t)
                     + sizeof(ip_hdr_t)
                     + ntohs(udp->length);
    enet_send_frame(frame, eth_len);
}
```

The moment `recvfrom()` returns, this UDP datagram has completed the following journey: 100BASE-T1 PHY decodes the PAM-3 analog waveform into digital bits → MAC layer verifies FCS → DMA engine moves the frame from the PHY’s RX FIFO to the buffer pointed to by the DMA descriptor of the main memory → LWIP protocol stack strips off the Ethernet header, IP header, UDP header → copy the data payload to your `buf[]` array. All of this has been completed in hardware a few microseconds before you call `recvfrom()`.

**When you ping this ECU from the diagnostic tool and then send a UDP echo: the diagnostic tool sends an Ethernet frame, which passes through the switch, gateway, and vehicle Ethernet, and is parsed into MII by the PAM-3 demodulation and echo cancellation of the PHY at the physical layer. The nibble stream is assembled into a complete frame at the MAC layer and sent to SRAM through DMA. The ENET interrupt triggers the ISR. Your `enet_rx_handler` in the ISR is called, and then you swap the MAC/IP/port and send the data back intact. ** The entire process, if it is a 100Mbps link and the frame length is 64 bytes, from completion of reception to completion of transmission. About 2-3 ms (the main bottleneck is interrupt latency and software processing).

Ethernet solves "how to transmit", but automotive Ethernet also needs to answer "what to transmit" and "why to transmit". A key scenario is diagnosis. If your car has a fault light, the 4S shop's diagnostic instrument needs to read the fault code, flash the ECU, and run an actuator test. CAN can do it, but it takes more than two hours to transfer a 500MB firmware package using CAN. This is the reason why DoIP appears. It is not to replace UDS, but to replace UDS with a faster highway.

## DoIP: Diagnostics moving from CAN to Ethernet

DoIP (Diagnostics over IP, ISO 13400) is a solution to migrate the UDS diagnostic protocol to TCP/IP.

When doing UDS diagnostics on CAN, you are choked by the 8-byte frame length. If the response of a 0x22 service reading a certain DID exceeds 8 bytes, the ISO 15765-2 flow control frame mechanism must be used for multi-frame transmission, which is extremely inefficient: the diagnostic instrument sends a 0x30 flow control frame → the ECU allocates continuous frames (CF) for transmission → a minimum time slot (Stmin) is separated between each frame. When flashing firmware, 500MB needs to traverse approximately 70 million CAN frame interactions.

On DoIP: Pin 3/11 of the OBD-II port serves as the Ethernet activation line; the diagnostic tool discovers the DoIP node on the vehicle through UDP broadcast (destination port 13400), sends a Vehicle Identification Request, receives a Vehicle Identification Response, and obtains the VIN, logical address, and further connection information. The diagnostic tool then establishes a TCP connection (port 13400) to the selected target ECU. Since then, all UDS requests/responses are transmitted through TCP streaming, and the UDS service remains intact, but there is no 8-byte frame length limit. A 0x22 ReadDataByID response can span multiple TCP segments. A single segment payload is about 1460 bytes (standard MTU) and is no longer limited to 8 bytes.

**DoIP DISCOVERY message format (simplified):**

The diagnostic tool sends UDP broadcast:
```
Payload Type: 0x0001 (Vehicle Identification Request)
Version:      0x02
Inverse Ver:  0xfd
...
Payload:      Vehicle Identification Request
```

ECU responds to UDP Unicast:
```
Payload Type: 0x0004 (Vehicle ID Response / Announcement)
VIN:          "WDD..."
Logical Addr: 0x0010 (diagnostic address)
EID:          6 bytes (Entity ID)
GID:          6 bytes (Group ID)
Further Actions: 0x00 = no extra OEM action required (routing activation is still the standard step)
```

The diagnostic tool then establishes a TCP connection (port 13400) to the node that provides the logical address, and then sends a Routing Activation Request; only after receiving 0x10 (Routing Successfully Activated) can this connection carry UDS. Subsequent UDS data format:

```
DoIP Header (8 bytes):
  Version:          0x02
  Inverse Version:  0xfd
  Payload Type:     0x8001 (Diagnostic Message)
  Payload Length:   N

DoIP Payload (N bytes):
  SA:  Source Address (2 bytes)
  TA:  Target Address (2 bytes)
  ... UDS Service + Parameter ...
```**ECU flashing: 500MB firmware is downloaded in 2-3 minutes via DoIP** (100Mbps Ethernet effective net load is about 70Mbps, 500MB÷(70/8)≈57 seconds. Including the time for ECU to erase Flash and write, the total is 2-3 minutes). **Parallel diagnosis: The diagnostic instrument can establish TCP connections to multiple ECUs at the same time**, which is impossible on CAN (CAN is a broadcast bus, only one node can send frames on a physical segment at the same time, and multi-frame communication needs to be scheduled).

DoIP is not an "upgraded version of UDS", it releases diagnosis from "bus topology" to "network topology".

## Gateway ECU: Translator of two worlds

The EE architecture of an actual smart car is as follows:

**CAN domain (real-time control)**: Powertrain CAN (500kbps), Chassis CAN (500kbps), Body CAN (125kbps), Comfort CAN (125kbps). Each CAN segment is driven by an independent CAN controller. Frames are broadcast within their respective segments. Segments are forwarded through the CAN routing function in the Gateway ECU. Only the required signals are forwarded, and the unnecessary ones remain within this segment.

**Ethernet domain (data)**: The infotainment domain and ADAS domain each have an Ethernet switch (Switch), forming a star topology. It runs SOME/IP, DoIP, AVB/TSN. Bandwidth 100Mbps-1Gbps.

**Gateway ECU** sits at the junction of the two domains. Its CAN controller is hung on the power CAN segment to receive engine speed frames; its Ethernet MAC is hung on the on-board Ethernet switch to receive camera video streams. Its SoC (usually a high-performance ARM core such as Cortex-A53/A72) runs a complete protocol stack. The CAN side is signal routing (AUTOSAR COM→PduR→CanIf→Can), and the Ethernet side is TCP/IP+SOME/IP+DoIP.

When you read the engine speed via DoIP from the diagnostic tool:

```
Diagnostic tool → TCP connection → Gateway (Ethernet port)
  → Gateway's SOME/IP layer decapsulates DoIP requests into UDS requests
  → Gateway’s PDU Router routes UDS requests to the CAN domain
  → Gateway's CAN controller (Body CAN) sends CAN frames:
    ID=0x7e0, data=03 22 F4 0C 00 00 00 00
    (UDS physical request 0x22 ReadDataByIdentifier DID=0xf40c)
  → Engine ECU replies to CAN frame:
    ID=0x7e8, data=10 14 62 F4 0C 00 00 00
    (UDS first frame response, indicating that there is flow control)
  → Gateway receives full response via ISO 15765-2 flow control protocol
  → Gateway assembles the UDS response into a DoIP message and sends it back to the diagnostic tool through TCP
```

to track a specific package. The engine ECU (CAN domain) sent a DTC: "Coolant temperature is too high", CAN ID=0x18FEDF00. The gateway's CAN controller receives this frame and triggers a receive interrupt. The MCU transmits the 8-byte data of this CAN frame to the application processor of the gateway through SPI or internal bus. The AUTOSAR Dem/Dcm stack on the application processor converts CAN fault events into UDS DTC format. This DTC is then packaged into a SOME/IP notification message: UDP payload, and the target IP is the T-Box/remote diagnostic server. This IP packet passes through the gateway's Ethernet MAC, 100BASE-T1 PHY, and reaches the T-Box through a single twisted pair. The entire journey: CAN frame → CAN controller → MCU → Application processor → Ethernet stack → 100BASE-T1 PHY → T-Box, in less than 10 milliseconds.

**Gateway is the translator**, which allows the "signal bus" and "data network" to understand each other. This is not a compromise of technical debt, it is a necessity of correct architecture. Real-time control requires deterministic latency and robustness against failures, and CAN gives you both. Data requires bandwidth and routing flexibility, and Ethernet gives you both. The two are not mutually exclusive, they work separately.

## Why Ethernet will not replace CAN (in the foreseeable future)

You may be thinking: If Ethernet is so fast, so flexible, and supports service discovery, why not switch all communications in the car to Ethernet?

Two fundamental reasons.

**1. Certainty. ** CAN's priority arbitration is hard deterministic, and the worst-case delay of the highest priority frame can be accurately calculated (blocking of the longest low-priority frame + frame length + 3-bit interframe spacing). The latency of the TCP/IP stack is non-deterministic, with the retransmission timeout (RTO) ranging from hundreds of milliseconds to several seconds, depending on the dynamic adjustment of the congestion control algorithm (Reno/Cubic/BBR). The brake signal cannot wait for the TCP retransmission timeout. It's not "too slow", it's "unpredictable".**2. Cost and complexity. **The bill of materials (transceiver + controller) of a CAN node is less than $1. A 100BASE-T1 node requires PHY ($3-5), MAC, switch ports, more complex wiring harnesses and connectors, and costs an order of magnitude more. Used on dozens of ECUs in the powertrain and chassis, the extra cost is unacceptable at scale. CAN's ability to solve problems worth more than $1 with transceivers for less than $1 is the reason why it has survived for 40 years.

CAN tube is "alive": the engine does not stall, the brakes do not fail, and the steering does not lock.
Ethernet management is "smart": autonomous driving perception, cloud updates, and infotainment.
This is not about who replaces whom, but who does their best in their respective constrained spaces. 

## Your role is changing too

Ten years ago, you were a "CAN engineer". You sent and received signals to DBC files and CANoe, and your debugging tool was an oscilloscope differential probe. Today, when automotive Ethernet enters mass production, you need to understand differential signaling and TCP retransmission timeout at the same time, diagnose CAN error frame and SOME/IP service discovery failure at the same time, and debug the FlexCAN message buffer (MB) and lwIP pbuf memory pool at the same time.

But you don't become a pure IT engineer. You have a better understanding than network engineers: you know how the PAM-3 eye diagram of 100BASE-T1 will close in the 20A PWM driven EMI environment in the car. You know that if the DCDC high-power charging module in the neighborhood is activated during DoIP flashing, the common mode noise will cause the TCP packet loss rate to soar to 30%, triggering continuous retransmissions. You know that the PHY's PLL lock time extends from 150ms to 400ms on a -40°C cold boot, and your bootloader timeout setting is 300ms.

**You don’t simply “move IT technology to the car”, you re-verify the assumptions of each IT technology within the physical constraints of the car. ** Network engineer assumes "the cable is Cat5e and the ambient temperature is 25°C". The reality you face is "the cable is a single pair of UTP running through the engine compartment, the ambient temperature is -40 to 125°C, and the next door is a 50A EPS motor PWM drive." Same TCP protocol stack, different physical world, different answers.

This is what this book has always wanted to talk about: it does not require you to choose sides, but it requires you to understand the physical constraints on both sides at the same time. The differential arbitration and wire-saving design of CAN come from the electromagnetic hell in the engine room, and the full-duplex switching of Ethernet comes from the bandwidth requirements of the data center. They are not opposing technologies, but different answers to different scenarios from the same physical world. Only if you understand the bottom layer, you don’t need to choose sides on the upper layer.
