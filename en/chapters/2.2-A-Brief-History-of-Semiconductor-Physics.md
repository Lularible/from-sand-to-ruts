# 2.2 A Brief History of Semiconductor Physics

## A strange piece of galena

1874. German physicist Ferdinand Braun did something very ordinary in his laboratory at the University of Würzburg.

He poked a thin metal probe onto the surface of a natural crystal of galena (PbS, lead sulfide), then connected a battery and measured the current flowing through the mineral.

An incredible result appeared: **The current can only go in one direction. **

He swapped the positive and negative poles of the battery. Same probe, same crystal, same contact point. The current changes from the forward milliampere level to the reverse microampere level, a difference of three orders of magnitude. It's not that the material is broken, it's not that there's something wrong with the instrument, it's not that he connected it backwards. He just happened to discover the rectification effect: the contact between the crystal and the metal allows current to pass easily in one direction, but hardly conducts in the opposite direction.

Braun didn't know the term "semiconductor" at the time. He didn't know about the PN node. He did not know that a quantum mechanical process that no one understood at the time was taking place between the lead and sulfur atoms in the galena he touched. He just faithfully recorded this phenomenon, wrote a paper and published it.

The 24-year-old Braun may think this is just a scrap-level discovery. His later career proved his ideas. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Marconi in 1909, not for semiconductors, but for his contributions to wireless telegraphy; the "Braun tube" he invented was the cathode ray tube, the precursor to later CRT monitors and television picture tubes.

The moral of this story is: Great discoveries are often not great at the time. When Braun poked that piece of galena, he had no idea that 130 years later, there would be hundreds of billions of "rectifiers" working around the clock on the earth. It’s just that they are no longer galena probes, but PN junctions with a size of only tens of nanometers engraved on the silicon wafer.

## Atoms, energy levels, energy bands: Why do some materials conduct electricity and others not?

To understand semiconductors, we must first understand the basic principles of electrical conduction.

Atoms are composed of a nucleus and electrons outside the nucleus. Early physics (Bohr's model) imagined electrons as small balls orbiting the nucleus. Each ball can only travel on a specific, discrete orbit. These orbitals are called **energy levels**. The electron cannot stay in the "middle position" between any two energy levels. It is either in this orbit or that orbit. There is no transition state. This is the first disturbing conclusion of quantum mechanics: energy is not continuous but quantized.

When many, many atoms are closely packed together to form a crystal, the situation changes qualitatively. The electron orbit of each atom is subject to the electromagnetic influence of adjacent atomic nuclei, and the energy level will shift slightly. Billions of atoms are arranged together, and each energy level will split into an **energy band** composed of billions of extremely tiny sub-energy levels. You can imagine taking each harmonic (discrete frequency) of a string and putting it into a giant orchestra. Each string has a slightly different tuning, and the result is a continuous range of frequencies.

In crystals, the two most critical energy bands are:

Valence Band: An energy band with lower energy. Electrons in the valence band are tightly bound by atoms. They fill the covalent bonds between atoms and have no ability to move freely within the crystal.

Conduction Band: An energy band with higher energy. If an electron gains enough energy to "jump" into the conduction band, it breaks free from the constraints of the nucleus and becomes a free electron that can roam freely in the crystal. Free electrons = conduction of electricity.

Between these two bands, there is a **band gap** (Band Gap, also called a band gap). In this energy range, no energy levels exist. Electrons cannot exist in the forbidden band. It is either in the valence band (non-conductive) or in the conduction band (conductive). The width of the forbidden band determines whether the material is a "conductor", "semiconductor" or "insulator".

**Metals (Copper, Aluminum, Silver)**: Valence and conduction bands overlap. In other words, the Fermi level falls exactly inside the conduction band. This means that even at absolute zero, there are a large number of electrons naturally in the conduction band. Electrons can move freely without requiring any external energy. Therefore metals are good conductors at any temperature.Insulators (glass, rubber, silica): bandgap greater than 5 eV. The energy required for electrons to jump from the valence band to the conduction band is too great. The energy of thermal motion at room temperature is approximately kT ≈ 0.026 eV (k is Boltzmann's constant, T is the temperature 300K). 5 eV and 0.026 eV, a difference of 200 times. The probability of jumping to the conduction band by thermal excitation is extremely small, so insulators are completely non-conductive under normal circumstances.

**Semiconductors (Silicon, Germanium, Gallium Arsenide)**: Bandgap width between 0.5 and 3.5 eV. Silicon is 1.12 eV, germanium is 0.67 eV, silicon carbide (4H-SiC) is 3.23 eV, and gallium nitride (GaN) is 3.4 eV. At room temperature, a small number of electrons can gain enough energy to jump into the conduction band through thermal excitation. So semiconductors are said to be "somewhat conductive". But here's the kicker: you can actively control its conductivity. **Precisely control the number of electrons in the conduction band through doping, electric field, and illumination. **

Silicon's 1.12 eV is the magic number. If it is too small (like 0.67 eV for germanium), the leakage current at room temperature will be too large and the device will not turn off. If it is too large (like 3.23 eV for SiC), a larger gate drive voltage is required to fully conduct, and the drive circuit is more complex. 1.12 eV, which is exactly the equilibrium point. Nature did not "design" silicon to be the king of semiconductors, but the physical parameters of silicon happened to fall at the most practical point. It's not inevitable, it's lucky.

## PN Junction: The Cornerstone of Semiconductor Physics—What Happens in the Depletion Region

Each atom of pure silicon (intrinsic semiconductor) has 4 outer electrons (tetravalent), and each of the 4 silicon atoms in and around the crystal shares a pair of electrons, forming 4 covalent bonds. At absolute zero, all electrons are trapped in covalent bonds and the crystal does not conduct electricity at all.

But what if you intentionally doped pure silicon with very small amounts of **pentavalent** impurity atoms, such as phosphorus (P) or arsenic (As)? Phosphorus has 5 outer electrons. It replaces a silicon atom in the crystal lattice, uses up 4 electrons to form a covalent bond with its neighbor, and then gains one more electron. This "extra" electron is very weakly bound by the phosphorus nucleus and only requires about 0.045 eV of energy to break free and become a free electron. The thermal excitation energy at room temperature is 0.026 eV, and there is a considerable probability that this electron will be released.

So phosphorus-doped silicon is full of free electrons, and this material is called an N-type semiconductor (N stands for Negative, because free electrons are negatively charged). After donating free electrons, the phosphorus atom itself becomes a positively charged fixed ion. But it is embedded in a crystal lattice and cannot move. Therefore, in N-type semiconductors, there are more negative charges (free electrons) that can move and more positive charges (phosphorus ions) that are fixed.

Conversely, what if you incorporate a **trivalent** impurity, such as boron (B)? Boron only has 3 outer electrons, and when it displaces a silicon atom, it is missing an electron to form the 4th covalent bond. This lack is called a hole. A hole is not a real particle, it is just a location where there should be an electron but there is not. But the adjacent electron can easily jump over to fill the hole, and then a new hole is formed in the original position of the electron. So a hole is equivalent to a positively charged, mobile carrier in the crystal lattice. This material is called **P-type semiconductor** (P stands for Positive). After the boron atom captures an electron (filling the hole), it becomes a negatively charged fixed ion.

Now, take the two pieces of material you just imagined, one P-shaped and one N-shaped, pressed together. What happens at their interface is the basis of all semiconductor devices.

**Diffusion Current**: At the junction, the electron concentration in the N-type region is much higher than in the P-type region. The concentration difference drives the diffusion of electrons from the N-type region to the P-type region. Just like a drop of ink spreads when dropped into clear water. In the same way, the hole concentration in the P-type region is much higher than that in the N-type region, and holes diffuse from the P-type region to the N-type region.After electrons diffuse into the P-type region, they encounter a large number of holes, and **recombination** occurs: the electrons fall into the holes, and both disappear at the same time. After holes diffuse into the N-type region, they encounter a large number of electrons and recombine. In the thin layer near the interface, almost all free carriers recombine. All that remains are those fixed, immobile ions: fixed positive phosphorus ions on one side of the N-type region, and fixed negative boron ions on one side of the P-type region.

This thin layer with almost no free carriers is called the depletion region because both free electrons and holes have been depleted.

**Built-in electric field**: In the depletion zone, fixed positive ions (N side) and negative ions (P side) form a built-in electric field. The direction of the electric field is from N to P, which just prevents more electrons from diffusing from N to P, and also prevents holes from diffusing from P to N. Eventually, diffusion and drift reach dynamic equilibrium and the depletion region reaches a stable width. In silicon materials, typical values ​​are 0.5 to 1 μm.

The potential difference between the two ends of the depletion region is called Built-in Potential, represented by V_bi. For silicon PN junctions, V_bi is typically between 0.6 and 0.8 V. When no external voltage is applied, this built-in potential prevents any net current flow across the junction region.

**Forward bias**: The P terminal is connected to positive voltage, and the N terminal is connected to negative voltage. The external electric field is in the opposite direction to the built-in electric field. The built-in electric field is weakened. The depletion zone narrows. When the external voltage exceeds about 0.6-0.7V, a large number of electrons pour in from the N side, and a large number of holes pour in from the P side. The current increases exponentially. I = I₀ × (e^(V/V_T) - 1). V_T is the thermal voltage, about 26mV.

**Reverse bias**: The N terminal is connected to positive voltage, and the P terminal is connected to negative voltage. The external electric field has the same direction as the built-in electric field, which increases the built-in electric field and widens the depletion zone. Minority carriers (holes) in the N-type region are attracted to the P-side direction of the electric field, and minority carriers (electrons) in the P-type region are attracted to the N-side direction, forming a tiny reverse saturation current. This current is extremely small, typically in the nA to pA range.

**This is a diode** This is the physical essence of the "rectification effect" that Braun happened to discover in 1874. There must be a naturally formed metal-semiconductor junction or grain boundary rectifier junction in the piece of galena he poked. 130 years later, we engrave it on a silicon wafer, shrink it millions of times, and mass-produce it.

## The most common failure modes in yield analysis

In a failure analysis report, what are the most common failure modes? It's not gate oxide breakdown, that's not common on automotive chips because the voltage is not high. It's not a broken metal wire, it's more of a packaging issue. The most common is **PN junction leakage**.

Specifically: the protection diode of a certain I/O pin, or the source-drain junction of a certain internal node, should appear to be open circuit (only pA level leakage current) under reverse bias, but in fact it shows nA or even μA level leakage.

A typical troubleshooting case: the yield of a batch of glass substrates suddenly dropped from a stable 95% to 72%. The defect distribution map shows a strange pattern of concentric rings. The yield rate of the outer ring panel unit is okay, but the inner ring fails in a large area. This spatial distribution is not random. It usually points to non-uniformity on the substrate during a certain process.

The failure analysis team sends a failed panel unit for parametric testing. On the probe station, engineers tie dozens of tungsten probes to the TEG test structure, and use a semiconductor parameter analyzer (such as Keysight B1500A) to measure the I-V curve of each test structure one by one. Finally, the culprit was found: the reverse leakage current of the source-drain junction of a certain group of TFTs was about 200 times higher than the spec. Normal should be between 1-10 pA, this batch got to 2 nA.

2 nA for a single device may not sound like much, but tens of thousands of these devices on a panel unit can cumulatively raise the quiescent leakage current into the milliamp range. The power consumption of the entire screen in standby mode exceeds the specification, and what is even more fatal is: these leakage PN junctions will accelerate degradation at high temperatures, eventually leading to permanent failure.The investigation process lasted three weeks. All process logs were called up: the temperature curve of each furnace tube, the dose and energy readings of each ion implantation, and the batch number of each batch of chemicals. Finally, it was discovered that the ion implanter through which this batch of substrates passed had undergone preventive maintenance (PM) that morning. After maintenance, engineers recalibrated the beam. However, during the calibration process, the reading of the beam monitoring Faraday cup had a momentary jump, which was misjudged as a normal fluctuation by the automatic calibration system. As a result, the dose of boron injected was about 7% higher than the recipe.

7% does not sound like much, but the ion implantation dose determines the doping concentration of the PN junction, and the doping concentration determines the width of the depletion region. The narrower the depletion region, the greater the tunneling current. The extra 7% dose just narrows the PN junction depletion region of this batch of substrates to the leakage threshold.

After the Faraday cup of that ion implanter was recalibrated, the yield for the next batch of substrates returned to 95 percent.

A particle of dust can destroy the entire circuit. This is the most profound lesson I have learned in yield engineering. And "dust" is not necessarily really dust. It could be a miscalculated calibration value, an aged O-ring, an etch time that is 0.01% longer than the time control. The price of doing things at the atomic scale is that your tolerance for error is also at the atomic scale.

## Parameter degradation is not "broken" - it is slowly moving in a bad direction.

The above story reveals a key characteristic of semiconductor failure: most failures do not occur suddenly, but rather slowly drift parameters until they exceed spec.

This is especially important on automotive chips. Because a consumer chip only needs to be passed when leaving the factory, and then there will be no problems during the 2-3 years of use. But a car-standard chip must keep its parameters within spec for 15 years. Even after thousands of temperature cycles (from -40°C cold starts to 125°C in the engine compartment), hundreds of humidity soaks, various voltage shocks.

There are various physical mechanisms for parameter drift: hot carrier injection (HCI), high-energy electrons "bump" into the gate oxide layer, forming trap charges, and gradually raising the threshold voltage. Negative bias temperature instability (NBTI), under high temperature and negative gate voltage of PMOS, the Si-SiO₂ interface generates an interface state, and the threshold voltage drifts in the negative direction. Electromigration (EM), in which the flow of electrons in metal interconnects "pushes" metal atoms, can cause holes or bumps in a 28nm-wide copper wire over decades, and eventually break or short out.

I have seen panel units in the production line after accelerated life testing. They ran for 1000 hours at 150°C at 1.4 times nominal voltage. After taking it out, the parameters of some units have drifted by 10-20%. It's not "bad" yet. But if this is the ECU in a car, ten years later when it is cold-started on a morning at minus 40°C, will that 20% drift in the threshold voltage just cause a timing path to fail?

This is the cruelty of car-grade chip testing: you are not testing "whether it is good or not", you are testing "how long it will take before it breaks down".

## The feud at Bell Labs: three people, a transistor, and a Nobel Prize

After understanding the PN junction, the invention of the transistor was a matter of course.

December 23, 1947, Bell Labs. Physicist John Bardeen and experimental physicist Walter Brattain cut a thin piece of gold foil in half and pressed it onto the surface of an N-type germanium crystal. The gap between the two gold foils is only 50 microns, less than the width of a human hair, and the surfaces of the gold foil and germanium form two metal-semiconductor junctions (Schottky junctions).

When Bardeen and Bratton injected a tiny current into one of the contacts (the emitter), they observed an amplified signal at the other contact (the collector), with a gain of about 100 times, controlling a much larger output current with the weak input signal.

For the first time, humans used solid-state devices (not vacuum tubes) to amplify electrical signals, and the cornerstone of the electronic age was laid.But this device looks ugly. A germanium crystal the size of a fingernail is held in place by a plastic wedge on a metal base. Two pieces of gold foil are pressed onto the surface of the germanium, a spring is used to control the pressure, and the wiring is manually attached with solder. It looked like a broken can opener, but it was the first transistor in human history.

But what textbooks rarely talk about in this truly innovative story is the grudges behind it.

William Shockley was Bardeen and Brattain's supervisor and the head of the Solid State Physics Group at Bell Labs. He proposed the theoretical concept of the "field effect transistor" (FET) as early as the 1940s. Controlling the conductivity of semiconductor surfaces using electric fields. But throughout the 1940s, his experiments failed again and again. The defect density on the semiconductor surface is too high, the electric field is shielded by the trap state, and effective channel control cannot be formed.

In late 1947, while Shockley was meeting in Europe, Bardeen and Bratton made the point-contact transistor. They used a completely different principle than Shockley's FET. It is not electric field control, but minority carrier injection. After Shockley returned to the United States, his first reaction was not to congratulate his colleagues, but to be angry.

Shockley believed that Bardeen and Brattain's devices were not "real" transistors. It is just opportunistic, bypassing the principles that FET should follow and making a "variation". He turned his jealousy into motivation, locked himself in the laboratory, and independently invented the Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) in just a few weeks, using a three-layer semiconductor (NPN or PNP) to achieve current amplification. The working principle of BJT is completely different from that of point contact transistors. It uses base current to control minority carrier injection from emitter to collector. This is a device structure that is more stable, more predictable, and more suitable for mass production.

He wrote his ideas in a notebook and kept them secret until he was convinced that he had "surpassed" Bardeen and Brattain's inventions before agreeing to make them public.

It is said that he later went to Bardeen and Bratton's laboratory, stood in front of their point-contact transistors and looked at them for a long time, and then walked away without saying a word.

In 1956, Shockley, Bardeen and Brattain won the Nobel Prize in Physics together. But at the press conference after the ceremony, the three barely exchanged words. Bardeen later left Bell Labs and went to the University of Illinois to study superconductivity. In 1972, he won the Nobel Prize in Physics for the second time for his BCS superconducting theory (named after Bardeen, Cooper, and Schriever), becoming the only person in history to win the Nobel Prize in Physics twice. Bratton returned to academic teaching, teaching physics at Whitman College until his retirement. Shockley went to California and founded Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory in Mountain View.

Shockley was a physics genius, but he was a terrible manager. He is extremely suspicious and his management style is to publicly humiliate his subordinates. He even suspected that his own employees were sabotaging his research. In 1957, eight of his core engineers ran away en masse and became known as the "Traitorous Eight". These eight people are Gordon Moore (later Moore's Law), Robert Noyce (co-inventor of the integrated circuit), etc. They first joined Fairchild Camera and Instrument Company and founded Fairchild Semiconductor. In 1959, Noyce invented the planar process integrated circuit at Fairchild, which was more suitable for mass production than Kilby's tabletop integrated circuit at Texas Instruments. In 1968, Moore and Noyce left Fairchild to found Intel.

From Shockley Labs to Fairchild Semiconductor to Intel, the entire semiconductor industry in Silicon Valley began with the grievances of these three people and the "defection" of eight people.

The invention of the transistor is not only the pinnacle of science, but also the grayscale of human nature.

## When transistors meet cars

The first areas where transistors changed were communications and computing: telephone switches, radios, mainframe computers. But its penetration into the auto industry has been a quieter revolution.Before the 1960s, cars had little electronics. The ignition system is mechanical platinum contacts and distributor. The contacts are subjected to arc erosion every time they fire and need to be ground or replaced every few thousand miles. The voltage regulator and rectifier are electromechanical. The carburetor is fully mechanical. Radios were transistorized. Japanese manufacturers had a significant lead in producing high-frequency germanium transistors for consumer use in the 1960s. But that can only be regarded as "consumer electronic products installed in the car", not real automotive electronics.

In the 1970s, two events radically changed the landscape. First, two oil crises forced global car companies to pursue fuel efficiency. Second, the U.S. Clean Air Act requires vehicle emissions to be reduced by 90% by 1975. It is impossible for a mechanical carburetor to meet this standard. You need precise control of the air-fuel ratio (λ = 1 ± 1%) and ignition timing, which is almost impossible with purely mechanical means.

The entry of the transistor was timely. Bosch introduced the first electronically controlled fuel injection system, the D-Jetronic, in 1967, which used discrete transistor circuits (not microcontrollers!) to control the injection pulse width based on intake manifold pressure and engine speed. In 1979, Bosch's Motronic system integrated ignition and fuel injection into a digital controller. This is the prototype of the modern engine management system (EMS).

At the same time, transistors also made an appearance in braking systems. In 1978, Bosch launched the world's first mass-produced ABS (anti-lock braking system), installed on the Mercedes-Benz S-Class (W116). The ABS ECU needs to monitor the pulse signals output by the wheel speed sensors of the four wheels in real time. There may be only 48 pulses for one wheel rotation. When it detects that a wheel is about to lock, within a few milliseconds, the ECU must issue instructions to the hydraulic regulator to apply the brakes dozens of times per second. Without transistorized small-signal amplifiers and logic circuits, this response speed would simply not be possible.

From the first point-contact transistor in 1947 to the first mass-produced ABS ECU in 1978, only 31 years passed.

It only takes one generation to go from physical discovery to life saving.

## From the first transistor to 57 billion

1947: Bell Labs, point contact transistor, 1 transistor.

1958: Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments integrated two transistors, several resistors and a capacitor on the same germanium substrate and connected them manually with gold wires to create the world's first integrated circuit. The task his boss gave him was actually to "find ways to reduce the size of the missile electronic system", not to build a computer.

1971: Intel introduces the Intel 4004, the world's first commercial microprocessor. 2300 transistors, 10μm process, maximum clock frequency 740kHz. The PMOS process is used, which is slower than NMOS and consumes more power, but Intel in 1971 could only make PMOS.

2021: Apple M1 Max chip, 57 billion transistors, 5nm process, maximum frequency 3.23GHz. It integrates CPU, GPU, neural engine, memory controller, and display engine, all on one die.

From 2,300 to 57 billion, the number of transistors expanded approximately 24.8 million times in 50 years.

This is a growth curve that has never been seen before in human history, not linear, not quadratic, but exponential, and it has been sustained for 50 years.

## What we think is rough now will also be considered by future generations.

When Braun discovered the rectification effect in 1874, he had no idea that it would be connected to all electronic devices on Earth.

Shockley's speech at the 1956 Nobel Prize Ceremony did not mention "the future of computers" because he was worried that his field effect theory would be preempted by Bardeen and Bratton.

When Kilby built the first integrated circuit in 1958, he thought it was just a technical feat of "making discrete components on a small piece of material to save space." Little did he know that he had just started the largest industry in human history.When Moore proposed "transistors doubling every year" in 1965 (revised in 1975 to double every two years), he had only four years of data points at hand. There are only three or four data points in total to draw a straight line. He later admitted that he had no idea the line would last 50 years.

Great discoveries only solved immediate problems at that time. But their consequences were far beyond what was imagined at the time.

If you are setting up an SPI driver today, you may think that this is not a big deal. It is just writing a few bits to a register. But at the bottom of this SPI drive, there is Braun's rectification effect (1874), Shockley, Bardeen, and Brattain's in-depth understanding of PN junction physics (1940s), Bell Labs' point-contact transistors (1947), Kilby and Noyce's integrated circuits (1958-1959), Dennard's scaling law (1974), and FinFET invention (Professor Hu Zhengming of the University of California, Berkeley, 1999; Hu Zhengming later joined TSMC as CTO), and mass production of EUV lithography (ASML, 2018). 150 years of physical exploration and engineering practice are working for you to "read the register" this time.

Wang Xizhi said in the "Preface to the Lanting Collection": **"Those who come after us see the present as we see the past now." ** We looked at Braun's Galena today and thought it was rough. A piece of ore, a probe, a microammeter. Engineers 50 years from now will also find our 3nm chips rough when they look at them today. But just as Braun's discovery launched an entire era of semiconductors: a small GPIO output mode configuration you debug on an ECU today may be laying the foundation for a certain computing paradigm in the next era.

It's not "maybe", it's definitely, but you don't know what it is yet.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: starting from a piece of galena, we connected the core concepts of semiconductor physics together.

Key takeaways:
1. Band theory explains why some materials are conductive, some are not, and some are "semi" conductive: Silicon's 1.12eV bandgap is a lucky physical parameter. Not too big, not too small, just enough to make it a perfect semiconductor at room temperature.
2. **PN junction is the cornerstone of semiconductor physics**: depletion region, built-in electric field, forward bias, and reverse bias. The rectification effect that Braun accidentally discovered in 1874 became a diode of tens of nanometers on a silicon chip 130 years later.
3. The invention of the transistor is both the pinnacle of science and the grayscale of human nature: Bardeen, Bratton, Shockley. The feud between three Nobel Prize winners gave birth to the entire semiconductor industry in Silicon Valley.
