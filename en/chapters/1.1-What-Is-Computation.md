# 1.1 What Is "Computation"?

## A Room, a Stack of Blank Paper

You are trapped in a room. There is a table, a chair, a stack of blank paper, and a pencil in the room. There is a small window on the wall, and occasionally someone will pass a piece of paper through the window with a number written on it.

You can take a look at the paper, then write on the blank paper, draw a picture, tear it up, and crumple it into a ball. You can also pass a written piece of paper out of the window.

**The question is: Can you tell whether two of the numbers passed in are the same? **

You may say: Isn’t this simple? Write down every number that comes in. When a new number comes, look through the previous records to see if there are any repetitions. But wait, that stack of blank paper has a limit. Assume there are only 100 sheets and only 50 numbers can be written on each sheet. The numbers passed in through the window may be in the thousands.

With limited blank paper (limited memory space) and limited time (before dark), you must give an answer. Can you complete this task?

If there is infinite blank paper and infinite time, this problem can certainly be solved. But in reality, you are always working with limited resources.

This is the essential problem of "computing".

In computer science, this problem is called the set membership problem. When both the memory and the number of searches are less than the length of the data stream, absolutely accurate determination is essentially impossible. This is not a "difficulty", but a ceiling in principle. There are only two realistic ways out: either relax the resource limit or accept an approximate solution. What would a person without a computer do? Maybe arrange the numbers on a sheet of blank paper from smallest to largest, and when new numbers come, break them in half to look up. If you are running out of paper, record only the numbers that appear most frequently. If you really can’t fit it in, give up on precise answers and use probabilistic methods to give approximate results.

**You can’t escape the prison of resources. All computing strategies are essentially "making optimal choices under constraints." **

## Algorithm: The Art of War on White Paper

Let us write down the process of "determining repeated numbers" in the language of engineers. This is not a specific programming language, but **pseudocode** that anyone who understands logic can read, which itself is a "recipe for calculation":

```
Algorithm: Determine duplicate numbers in bounded memory

Input: a string of digital streams D[1], D[2], D[3]... passed in from the window
Resources: N sheets of blank paper, each of which can write M numbers (total capacity C = N × M)
Output: true when duplicates are found, unknown when paper runs out

Steps:
1. Maintain an ordered list S, initially empty
2. For each incoming number x:
    a. Binary search for x in S
        - If found: output true (duplicate found), terminate
        - If not found: continue
    b. If the size of S < C:
        - Insert x into S at the correct position, keeping it in order
        - Continue to the next number
    c. If S is full:
        - Output unknown (the blank paper is exhausted and cannot be determined)
        - Termination
```

You see, this is an algorithm: definite steps, limited resources, and clear termination conditions. There is no inspiration, no epiphany, no "luck" here, every step is mechanical.

What's wrong with this algorithm? Yes, it may output unknown. Under the constraints of limited resources, correctness is no longer "100% correct", but "doing the best within your capabilities". This is real world computing. Every piece of code you run on the ECU is essentially a variant of this algorithm: you have 64KB of stack space, you have a 10ms cycle budget, you have 8-priority preemptive scheduling, and you always make optimal decisions under constraints.

Now let's take a step back. What essence of computing does this model of blank paper + pencil + window capture? It is three things: **input, rules, and output**. You get the input (the number on the note), follow certain rules (binary search + ordered insertion), and produce a certain output (true or unknown). This set of patterns: input, rules, and output has long been traced in the history of human civilization. And it has evolved over thousands of years.

## Abacus, Bones and Gears: Past Lives of Calculation Tools

Before discussing Turing, we need to go back to an earlier starting point: When did humans start "computing"?

**Abacus** appeared in China around 500 BC. It is essentially a manually operated register set; the two beads on the top of the abacus of later generations represent 5, the five beads on the bottom represent 1, and each column is a decimal digit. When you dial the beads, you are changing the value of the "storage unit". The clever thing about the abacus is that it is a computing model of human-machine collaboration. The human is responsible for providing the "algorithm" (formula), and the abacus is responsible for providing "state storage" and "mechanical operation" (carry). It does not have an automated calculator, but it is the first time that humans have outsourced "calculation" from the brain to a physical tool.

Abacus has been used in China for more than two thousand years. But the West took a different path.In 1614, Scottish mathematician John Napier invented logarithms. He also invented Napier's bones, a set of wooden sticks engraved with multiplication tables. By arranging the sticks, you can turn multiplication into addition. This is essentially a "look-up table machine", it does not calculate, it just stores the pre-calculated results for you to retrieve. The Napier bone chip is smart, but it cannot be automated. It is also an "auxiliary ruler", not an "automatic machine".

Next comes the slide rule. In 1622, William Oughtred put two logarithmically scaled rulers together and slid them to perform multiplication and division operations. The principle of the slide rule is a direct application of Napier's logarithms: log(a×b) = log(a) + log(b). When you slide the ruler, you are "doing addition", and the scale tells you "the true number corresponding to the addition result." The slide rule is not good at addition and subtraction, it is only good at multiplication and division. But it dominated engineering for more than three hundred years. The Apollo 11 astronauts took a slide rule to the moon with them in case the navigation computer, which had only 4KB of RAM, failed. **Map complex operations to simple operations, and map precise operations to approximate operations. The idea of ​​computing has had the gene of “dimensionality reduction” from the beginning. **

But this is not enough. These tools only "assist" calculations, not "automatic" calculations. The real turning point came in the 17th century.

## Pascal and Leibniz: Dreams on Gears

In 1642, 19-year-old Blaise Pascal watched his tax collector father struggle with piles of accounts every day in Rouen, France. He decided to build a machine that could automatically add and subtract: the Pascaline.

The heart of the machine is a brass gear set. Each gear has 10 teeth, representing 0 to 9. When you turn the gear to add, every 10 teeth it turns will push the upper gear forward one space through a pawl mechanism, **this is the carry**. Pascal's adding machine is recognized as the world's first mechanical calculator put into use, although it can only do addition and subtraction (multiplication can be achieved by repeated addition, and division can also be achieved by repeated subtraction, but it is very slow). Pascal built about 50 of them, and 9 still exist.

Pascal's machine was beautiful, but it had one serious limitation: it could only add and subtract. Multiplication and division require manual decomposition of operations into multiple additions and subtractions, which actually confuses the boundaries between "calculation" and "operator". Pascal's adding machine did not solve the problem of "automating complex calculations."

Thirty years later, in 1673, Leibniz exhibited an even more advanced machine in Paris: the Leibniz Stepped Reckoner. Leibniz's genius was his design of a "stepped drum," a cylinder carved with nine racks of varying lengths, allowing gears of different digits to mesh with different numbers of teeth. This allowed his machine to do multiplication directly: you entered the multiplicand, turned the crank, and the machine automatically completed the "accumulation" operation. It can even do division.

But more importantly, Leibniz was not just an engineer, he was the first person to equate computing with philosophy. While he was designing this machine, he was contemplating a bigger idea: If numbers could be automatically calculated on gears, would logic work? Does reasoning work? **Can all human knowledge also have a set of "Characteristica Universalis", so that all disputes become a matter of "calculation and it will be clear"? **

Leibniz's machines frequently jammed because they were not up to par with 17th-century precision machining. But the seeds he planted at the philosophical level grew into the entire tree of modern computer science. When von Neumann said, "The computer is a logical machine," he was simply responding to Leibniz.

## Babbage and Ada: Fantasy a century ahead of its time

Pascal and Leibniz built "calculators" that could calculate results based on input values. But the real idea of ​​a "universal computer" came from Britain in the 19th century.Charles Babbage was a very bad-tempered man. He hated London's street organists (who, he said, "polluted the acoustics of the city"), hated publishers, and hated the British government. But he has an unparalleled talent: he can use mechanical parts: gears, levers, cranks, slides to build extremely complex logical systems.

In 1822, Babbage began designing the Difference Engine. The purpose of this machine is to automatically calculate and print mathematical tables (logarithms, trigonometric tables, etc.) using the "difference method". At that time, mathematical tables were completely calculated manually, and the error rate was extremely high. Navigators relied on an incorrectly calculated logarithm table and drifted to places they shouldn't have gone, causing the ship to be destroyed and people to die. Babbage was fed up with this unreliability: “The conscience of the world would be much more peaceful if there were a way to burn erroneous mathematical tables or sink them to the bottom of the sea.”

The principle of the difference engine is very beautiful. Many complex mathematical functions (such as polynomials, trigonometric functions) can be represented by "finite differences". For example, the values ​​of f(x) = x² are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25... The first-order difference is 3, 5, 7, 9... The second-order difference is 2, 2, 2... See, the second-order difference is a constant! So if you know the initial value and the differences at each order, you can generate the entire table by just adding repeatedly. The difference engine is a special machine for "addition + carry + printing".

The British government invested the equivalent of several million pounds in today's money in Babbage. But Babbage gave up on the Difference Engine midway because he already had a grander blueprint in mind: the Analytical Engine.

The design of the Analytical Engine came surprisingly close to that of modern computers. It has **Memory** (Store, used to store numbers), **Operation Room** (Mill, equivalent to the CPU's ALU), **Punch Card Input System** (borrowed from the Jacquard Jacquard Loom), and **Conditional Jump Capability** (different execution paths can be selected based on the calculation results). It was a **general purpose computer**, at least on paper.

Babbage's design was so complex that 19th-century levels of craftsmanship could not have produced it. The tolerances required for mold processing (in the micron range) far exceeded the best machinery available at the time. He spent decades and burned huge fortunes from the government and himself, and the Analytical Engine remained a pile of blueprints.

Here I would like to insert a piece of resonance as an engineer. I used to work as a yield engineer in a panel factory. When a CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition) machine deposits a silicon nitride film on a glass substrate, the thickness uniformity must be controlled within a few percent, which is a stacking accuracy of a dozen to dozens of atoms. The alignment accuracy of the exposure machine must reach the sub-micron level. With such precision, if a particle of dust that is invisible to the naked eye falls on a key graphic, it will be enough to short-circuit the TFT circuit of that panel unit. In serious cases, the piece of film will be scrapped directly. Babbage was unable to build his Analytical Engine essentially because he lived in an era where physical precision could not catch up with logical imagination. Computing is not just a mathematical concept, it requires a physical carrier. If the carrier is not perfect enough, the theory will be a "castle in the air". This is not just a problem in the 19th century. In semiconductor factories in the 21st century, engineers are fighting the same problem every day: **The physical world is always adding sand to the logical world**.

But then again. Although Babbage's Analytical Engine did not create it, it indirectly contributed to the most important programming event in human history.

**Ada Lovelace** (Ada Lovelace) was the daughter of the poet Byron, but her mother was determined to train her to be a mathematician to prevent her from being "overemotional" like her father. Ada met Babbage when she was 17 and became his admirer and collaborator. In 1842-1843 she translated a French paper on the Analytical Engine. But the appendices she wrote (Notes A to Note G) are far longer and more important than the original text. In Note G, she described in detail how to calculate Bernoulli numbers using the Analytical Engine, the first published computer program in human history.Ada saw further than Babbage. Babbage viewed the Analytical Engine primarily as a "supercomputer" for crunching numbers. But Ada wrote in the note: "The Analytical Engine can handle any object that can be represented by symbols... It can compose sophisticated and complex musical works." **She saw that the essence of computers is not arithmetic, but symbolic operations. ** Anything that can be symbolized and processed in a regular manner: numbers, words, musical notes, and even logical propositions are within the range of computers. This insight was a full 100 years early.

The Bernoulli number algorithm in Note G, if you write it using today's pseudo-code, contains loops, conditional branches, variable assignments, and nested iterations, and is not essentially different from the embedded C code you write on the ECU. In 1843, more than ten years after Faraday discovered the principle of electromagnetic induction, the first telegraph line had been built in Britain. But "electricity" is still a mysterious natural force in the eyes of most people. The first incandescent light bulb would have to wait more than thirty years, and computers were a word no one knew about. And Ada has already written an executable algorithm on paper with a quill pen.

Neither Babbage nor Ada lived to see the Analytical Engine implemented. Babbage died in poverty, and the scientific community in London gave him a shabby funeral. But their drawings and notes remain. In 1991, the Science Museum in London completely reproduced a Difference Engine No. 2 based on Babbage's original design and using tolerance levels at the time. It worked and was accurate to 31 significant figures. **It’s not that the design is wrong, it’s that the time has not yet come. **

## Punch Cards: Calculating Off the Desk

Babbage borrowed the idea of punched cards from the Jacquard loom, but it was Herman Hollerith who turned punched cards into a large-scale data processing tool.

In the 1880s, the U.S. Census Bureau faced a crisis. The last manual census took seven years to complete, and by the time the report was published, the data were seven years older. Helenes invented an electromechanical tabulation system: use punched cards to record each person's attributes (gender, age, race, occupation, etc.), and then insert the card into a card reader. There is a row of spring pins in the card reader. When the needle passes through the hole in the card, it will touch the mercury cup below, connect the circuit, and push the counter to move one grid. **This is the first time that "electricity" has officially been involved in calculations, although it is only doing statistics, not logic. **

The 1890 census used Hollenes' system and took only six weeks to compile preliminary results (the complete count took 2.5 years), saving the government $5 million. Helenes made his fortune from then on, and his company later went through several rounds of mergers and was renamed **IBM** in 1924.

The philosophical meaning of punched cards is far-reaching: **information can be separated from the "human brain" and exist on physical media, and can be "read" and "processed" by machines. ** Leibniz's "universal ideogram," found its first industrial practice on punched cards.

Although IBM's tabulating machines, sorting machines, and card punching machines are not yet "general-purpose computers", they have developed input/output subsystems, pulse detection circuits, and relay logic, which later became the organs of real computers.

## What exactly does "compute" mean?

With these historical foreshadowings, we can return to the original philosophical question.

Mathematically, we can define it like this:

**Computation is a set of deterministic rules that act on a given set of inputs and produce a certain output after a limited number of steps. **

There are three key words in this definition.

**Deterministic**: The same input, the same rule, must produce the same output. If you calculate 1+1=2 today, it must be 2 tomorrow. Pascal's gear turns the same angle every time, and your formula for division reads the same word every time. Certainty is the first prerequisite for calculation.

**Limitedness**: Must be completed within limited steps. The calculation is never finished, which means it has not been calculated. Babbage's Analytical Engine was designed with a "conditional jump" to terminate the loop when a certain condition is met, otherwise the gears would keep turning forever. Each task of your ECU must be completed within the specified time, otherwise the operating system will trigger watchdog timeout, reset, and enter safe state.**Mechanical**: Every step of the operation is simple and mechanical, and does not require "inspiration" or "epiphany". In theory, a fool could execute it. The abacus formula, Napier's bone chip look-up table, and punch card pogo pins all meet this requirement.

This definition has an extremely important corollary: **A problem is "computable" if there are mechanical steps to solve it. If there is no such step, it is "uncomputable". **

## From Blank Paper to ECU: computing under resource constraints

Let us make a penetrating mapping between the blank-paper experiment and the real automotive electronics scene.

`ECU resources: 64KB SRAM, 512KB Flash, 112MHz Cortex-M4F. CAN load 85%, MAIN task every 10 ms, fast ISR every 1 ms.`

You are not turning over a blank slate in a room. You're doing the same thing at 112MHz on a piece of silicon smaller than your fingernail.

Every line of C code you write: while loop, if judgment, array access, all directs this "silicon house": this Flash page stores this array, the peripheral register value is compared with this threshold, and if the CAN ID matches, it is copied to the forwarding buffer.

When you write code, have you ever realized that: **Every if statement you make consumes limited computing resources, clock cycles, stack depth, and branch prediction table entries? ** Automotive electronics is different from servers. Just add more memory to the server. The MCU of the ECU is welded to the PCB, and the 128KB SRAM is not enough. You can only optimize the code, but cannot change the chip. The server is not real-time. Even if the ECU's Main Task times out by 1 millisecond, it won't work, because if the signal from the collision sensor does not reach the ESP controller within 5ms, the system will enter a degraded mode and the brake assist will be weakened.

**Limited resources + deterministic rules + limited steps + hard real-time constraints → This is automotive embedded “computing”. **

## The Curse of the Physics Substrate: Why Semiconductor Engineers Are More Anxious than Theorists

As someone who has worked in a semiconductor factory, I have a keen sense of "limited resources" that others don't have.

Theorists say, "If the memory is not enough, add more memory." But in the chip industry, adding memory means adding SRAM area. A 6T SRAM cell occupies approximately 0.3 μm² in the 40nm process. To add 1KB of SRAM, approximately 8192 additional such cells are needed, which is equivalent to occupying an additional 0.0025 mm² on the die. For some chips with a very small die area (such as tens of mm²), this is already a non-negligible overhead. And silicon area is money. Every time it occupies a little more, the cost model of the wafer factory will be recalculated.

More subtly, increasing the area will reduce the yield rate. Defect density (D0) is the lifeblood of a wafer factory. Taking 40nm as an example, typical D0 is 0.5-1.0 defects/cm². For a 10 mm² die, the yield is about 90%. If the design is changed to increase it to 12 mm², the yield will drop to 88-89%. What seems to be a drop of a few percentage points, multiplied by a monthly production capacity of tens of thousands of pieces, means the loss is in the millions of dollars.

So when I saw that Babbage’s Analytical Engine could not be built because of insufficient machining accuracy in the 19th century, I completely understood the feeling: It’s not that the idea was wrong, but that the physical foundation was not good enough.  In the dust-free workshop, we stare at the gas flow of the CVD machine, the sputtering power of the PVD machine, and the focus/exposure parameters of the lithography machine 24 hours a day. On the surface, we are doing "production", but in fact we are fighting against the noise, random fluctuations, and defect density of the physical world.

As an embedded engineer, you may have never entered a clean room. But when you use the MISRA C constraint language on the ECU, turn off the data cache, and lock the memory area, the logic is exactly the same as when I track a particle in the panel factory. **All engineers, whether upstream or downstream, are essentially dealing with "substrate imperfections." **

## To seek the limitless with the limitless

When we ask "what is computing" to the end, we will encounter a wonderful thing: **Computing is actually human beings' efforts to use limited means to grasp the infinite world. **The abacus uses two upper beads + five lower beads. Napier bone chips use numbers engraved on cow bones. Pascal's gears are made from 17th century brass metallurgy and hand filed. Babbage's design, if ever built, would have been a two-ton brass behemoth powered by steam. Helenes' tabulating machine used spring pins and mercury cups. Your S32K144, uses 55nm silicon process, planar transistors, copper interconnects.

**Medium keeps changing. But the three-dimensional structure of "input, rules, and output" has never changed. **

The complex automotive electronic system is just the superposition of layers of this structure: the analog comparator of the CAN transceiver is making the differential threshold determination of the "input". The FlexCAN protocol engine is doing the "rule" operation of the CRC. Your ISR is doing "output" and writing data to the send buffer. The external LED lights up and the final output is given.

Zhuangzi said: "My life also has a limit, but my knowledge has no limit. If I have a limit, I will follow the limitless, and I will be exhausted." The "extreme" he said means "exhausted from exhaustion." But the engineer's answer is: **It is precisely because there is a limit that we must pursue limitlessness. With every inch you take, the world becomes a little bigger. **

This road has been walked by the abacus maker, Napier, Pascal, Leibniz, Babbage, Ada Lovelace and Helene. Now it's your turn.

Leibniz stood in his 17th-century study, looked out the window and said, "We are going to build a machine that will calculate all human knowledge."

**The question is: is this possible? **
