# 1.5 From Turing Machine to ECU

## A devilish computer room

1945, Moore School of Engineering, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia.

A 30-ton beast squats in a machine room of about 167 square meters. Its name is **ENIAC** (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer), the world's first general-purpose electronic computer. It has 18,000 vacuum tubes, 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors, and 6,000 manual switches. Its power consumption was 150 kilowatts, and every time the ENIAC was turned on, the lights in Philadelphia would flash.

The failure rate of vacuum tubes is extremely high, with one breaking every two days on average. ENIAC's maintenance engineers invented a diagnostic process: they learned to "feel" which vacuum tube was dying based on the oscilloscope waveforms of different units during ENIAC operation, and then replaced it in advance. This is the same kind of intuition that automobile engineers use to judge that the valve clearance is too large based on abnormal noises in the engine.

ENIAC can do approximately 5000 additions or 357 multiplications per second. This speed was groundbreaking in 1945, 1,000 times faster than the fastest electromechanical computer at the time (Harvard Mark I). The calculation of a single ballistic trajectory requires 20 hours manually using a desktop calculator, while ENIAC only takes 30 seconds.

But guess how ENIAC is "programmed"?

**By plugging and unplugging cables. ** Dozens of female operators, then called "computers" (a title that later came to refer to the machine itself), climbed up and down the interior of ENIAC, manually plugging jumpers into various jacks and setting the positions of more than 3,000 switches on function tables. Changing a calculation task (for example, from calculating the trajectory of an artillery shell to calculating the shock wave propagation of an atomic bomb), it will take days or even weeks to rewire.

Six of Eniac's female operators are recognized as the world's first programmers: Kay McNulty, Jean Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, and Ruth Lichterman. At the time, hardware (that 30-ton computer room) was considered "high-end work" and was done by men. Programming was considered "paperwork" and was done by women. Looking back sixty years later, software engineering has become one of the highest-paying professions on the planet, and the word "computer" has changed from referring to people to referring to machines.

## Moth: a documented bug

When it comes to programming, you can’t avoid Grace Hopper.

Hopper joined the U.S. Naval Reserve in 1943 and was assigned to Harvard University to work on the Mark I computer; she took a break from teaching mathematics at Vassar College. She was the first to propose the idea of ​​replacing machine code with human-readable English words, an idea that later grew into the COBOL programming language and the entire concept of "high-level programming languages."

In September 1947, Hopper's team was troubleshooting a strange glitch on the Harvard Mark II. The relay doesn't work. They opened the cabinet to inspect and found a moth trapped between the relay contacts. They used tweezers to pick out the moth, taped it to the diary, and wrote next to it:

> *"First actual case of bug being found."*
> The first real case of a "bug" being discovered.

This episode later became widely circulated and became the most famous footnote to the "bug/debug" saying; however, these two words have been used in the engineering world for much earlier. Hopper later liked to tell this story to young people, she would say, "We weren't looking for bugs, bugs came for us."

## Von Neumann: The man who figured everything out on the train

ENIAC demonstrated that "electronic computers could work." But it also shows that "plugging and unplugging cables is a dead end." If the wiring team is busy for several days every time a program is changed, then the calculation is not "universal".The person who solved this problem was John von Neumann. He was probably one of the smartest men of the 20th century. His life is a breathless resume:

- In his early twenties, he published papers on the definition of ordinal numbers and the axiomatization of set theory, which laid the axiomatic foundation of modern set theory.
- Proposed the Hilbert space formalization of quantum mechanics in his twenties, which is the mathematical framework for all quantum mechanics textbooks today.
- 24-year-old proved the Minimax Theorem of game theory, which is the origin paper of game theory.
- Joined the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton at age 29, becoming the youngest tenured professor there.
- Joined the Manhattan Project at the age of 40. His implosion lens design was the key to the successful detonation of the atomic bomb "Fat Man". He not only "calculated the shock wave", he personally went to the desert of New Mexico to observe the first nuclear test, and also made a large number of numerical simulations.

Von Neumann had an almost terrifying mental arithmetic ability. One of his colleagues said something: Once he proposed a numerical solution to a set of partial differential equations at a meeting, and everyone found it a bit complicated after hearing it. He said: "Don't worry, I will do the math." Then he simulated the first 50 steps that the computer needed to perform in his mind, and gave the first few significant figures of the numerical solution. **Von Neumann's brain, a Turing machine running at 40Hz (his own biological clock frequency), requires no paper tape, no vacuum tubes, no electricity. **

He also studied Turing's 1936 paper. At Princeton, Turing also served as his assistant. Von Neumann understood the mathematical significance of Turing machines. But he was more concerned with its engineering implementation. In June 1945, he spent several weeks commuting by train and wrote a 101-page manuscript:

> *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*

## EDVAC Report: 101 Pages Changing the World

Von Neumann did **one thing** in the EDVAC report: he translated the three abstract components of the Turing machine: paper tape, read-write head, and state table into a circuit block diagram that can be implemented with electron tubes, mercury delay lines, and wires.

Turing machine tape → **memory**. Not a serial tape, but a storage space that can be "randomly accessed by address." The address is the coordinates of the memory, and the data is the content of the memory. This feature of random access is crucial. In a Turing machine, if you want to read the 73rd square on the tape, you must first read the first 72 squares. In the von Neumann architecture, you directly set the address line to 73, and the content in that grid appears on the data line. O(1) vs O(n). This is a qualitative change in computing efficiency.

The read-write head of the Turing machine → **Arithmetic unit (ALU) + controller**. Operators are pure "calculations": addition, subtraction, logical AND or NOT. The controller "makes decisions": reads instructions, decodes them, sends control signals, and updates the program counter. The two together are **CPU** (Central Processing Unit, central processing unit).

The state table of the Turing machine → **program, stored in the same memory**. This is the most genius decision. In ENIAC, the program is how the physical cables are connected. In the von Neumann architecture, a program is a string of numbers in memory, no different from the numbers that store data. **Changing the program means changing the contents of a section of memory. ** No sockets or cables required, just replace the paper tape (or the code on the paper tape).

This decision means that the computer has changed from a "physical machine specialized in doing specific calculations" to a "general-purpose machine that can load any program". After von Neumann's architecture was implemented, the same hardware could calculate ballistics, calculate payroll, calculate weather forecasts, and play chess. The only change is that the data in memory is different.

**General computing, from mathematical concept to engineering reality. **

## Five boxes, one heart

Once you understand the classic block diagram of the von Neumann architecture, you will understand the essence of all computers:

```
+----------+     +-----------+
| Memory |<--->| CPU |
|          |     |           |
| Program + Data | | +-------+ |
| | | | Operator | |
+----------+     | | (ALU) | |
                 | +-------+ |
                 | +-------+ |
                 | |Controller | |
                 | +-------+ |
                 +-----------+
                      |
                 +-----------+
                 | Input/Output |
                 |   (I/O)  |
                 +-----------+
```

**Memory** on the ECU is Flash (program) + SRAM (data) + EEPROM (calibration parameters). Your `const` constants go into Flash, and your local variables go into the SRAM stack.**Operator (ALU)** On the ECU is the 32-bit ALU of the ARM Cortex-M4F. The `x + y`, `a & b`, and `c << 4` you wrote in C are all here in the end. ARM's ALU can also do barrel shift + addition merge operations, so the compiler can translate `x = y * 3` into `ADD r0, r1, r1, LSL #1` (r0 = r1 + r1*2), one instruction, one clock cycle. This is the elegance of the RISC architecture.

The core of the **controller** has only one register: the **Program Counter** (PC). PC always points to the "address of the next instruction to be executed". The job of the controller is: fetch the instruction of the PC address → decode → execute → PC points to the next instruction → fetch the instruction of the PC address → ... and repeat until power failure, HALT, or exception.

**Input/Output** On the ECU are CAN controller, SPI, I2C, ADC, GPIO. The pulses emitted by the vehicle speed sensor are captured by the GPIO pin, counted by the internal timer, and converted into rotational speed. This is not "data" entering the "CPU", but the physical world writing information into the CPU's address space.

**Bus** is the "road network" between the five. On the S32K144, there is not one bus, but an AHB bus matrix, using crossbar switches to connect multiple master devices (CPU, DMA) and multiple slave devices (Flash, SRAM, peripherals). DMA can transfer data directly from the ADC data register to SRAM without going through the CPU. This is called "liberating the CPU".

## Von Neumann bottleneck

The von Neumann architecture has a famous flaw: the von Neumann bottleneck.

The bidirectional bus between memory and the CPU is the narrowest channel in the entire computer. The CPU can swallow 32 bits of data in one cycle, but the bus can only transfer one piece of data in each cycle. This is the reason why the cache exists: to store frequently accessed instructions and data from the CPU as close as possible to the ALU.

**The essence of the von Neumann bottleneck is physical distance. ** Electrons travel in a wire at approximately 2/3 the speed of light. With a 1GHz clock period of 1ns, the signal can only travel about 20cm. If your off-chip SDRAM is 10 centimeters away from the CPU, the round trip is 20 centimeters, and you can't touch it at all in this clock cycle. And on-chip SRAM is millimeter away, with much shorter latency. Therefore, the S32K144 Flash controller can output 64-bit prefetch data (2 32-bit instructions) with zero wait. But when accessing the off-chip QSPI Flash, the waiting period immediately jumps to dozens of clocks.

**This is why embedded engineers should care about .data/.bss section placement, LMA/VMA, linker script layout. ** You are fighting the von Neumann bottleneck.

## Harvard Architecture: Another way to combat bottlenecks

The program and data of the von Neumann architecture share a bus. **Harvard architecture** divides instructions and data into two independent buses, and the CPU can fetch instructions and access data at the same time. The ARM Cortex-M series adopts **Improved Harvard**: instructions and data are logically separated (two buses, ICode and DCode), but physically mapped to the same address space. Are you storing instructions or data at 0x00000000? The ICode prefetch unit and DCode load/store unit use different AHB interfaces and are accessed in parallel on the hardware. **Going sideways in the face of physical limits is the core theme of the entire history of computer architecture. **

## Penetrate fetch-decode-execute: a heartbeat of the CPU

Now we point a microscope at the inside of the CPU and look at a complete instruction cycle, the "breathing and breathing" of the CPU.

Assume that the PC points to the Flash address `0x00001234`, which stores a 32-bit Thumb-2 instruction `0xEB000001` (`ADD.W r0, r0, r1`). The following is the behavior of the Cortex-M4 three-stage pipeline:**The first pipeline level: Fetch**. The CPU puts address `0x00001234` on the address bus and issues a read request. The prefetch buffer (Prefetch Buffer) and acceleration logic (Speculative Fetch) inside the Flash controller read adjacent 64-bit instruction data (up to two 32-bit instructions, or several 16-bit Thumb instructions) together and send them to the decoding stage. **This step takes physical time: propagation delay of the address bus + read delay of the Flash storage array + return delay of the data bus. ** On S32K144 at 112MHz, approximately 2-3 HCLK cycles.

**Second pipeline level: Decoding**. The 32-bit Thumb-2 machine code `0xEB000001` is fed into the decoder. The decoder is a set of hard-wired logic, combinational logic, with no clocks and no registers. It splits 32 bits into fields:

```
0xeb000001 = 1110 1011 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 0001

[31:27] = 11101 → 32-bit Thumb-2 instruction prefix
[26:25] = 01 → Instruction type: data processing (register)
[24:21] = 1000 → fixed bit 1 + opcode 000 (ADD)
[20] = 0 → do not update condition flags (S = 0)
[19:16] = 0000 → first operand: R0
[15:12] = 0000 → fixed bit 0 + shift amount imm3 = 000
[11:8] = 0000 → Destination register: R0
[7:4] = 0000 → shift type LSL(00) + imm2(00)
[3:0] = 0001 → Second operand: R1
```

The decoding result is: "**R0 = R0 + R1**", a 32-bit addition. A set of control signals unfolds from this conclusion: the ALU's operation selection is set to ADD, the ALU's A input is connected to R0, the ALU's B input is connected to R1, and the target register address points to R0.

**The third pipeline level: Execute**. The register file (Register File, a fast SRAM with 16 32-bit registers) outputs the values ​​​​of R0 and R1 to the two input terminals of the ALU simultaneously. The ALU's 32-bit full adder accepts two inputs, passes through a logic gate propagation delay (a few hundred picoseconds), and outputs the result R0+R1. The write enable signal writes the output of the ALU back to R0 in the register file. **Meanwhile, the PC points to the next instruction: this 32-bit Thumb-2 instruction takes up 4 bytes, so the address is + 4 (+ 2 for a 16-bit Thumb instruction). ** Cortex-M4 only implements Thumb/Thumb-2, and there is no "fixed 4 bytes" rule like A32.

**One instruction, three pipeline stages are advanced, about three clock cycles. ** The following GPIO example takes STM32F4 as an example (the port register naming and address of S32K144 are different). Your C code `GPIOA->ODR |= (1 << 5)` compiles to:

```asm
LDR r0, =0x40020014    ; Load GPIOA_ODR address
LDR r1, [r0]           ; Read current ODR value
ORR r1, r1, #(1<<5)    ; 5th position 1
STR r1, [r0]           ; Write back ODR
```

Four instructions: get the GPIOA address, read the current value, set the bit, and write back. After about 8-10 clock cycles, the level of the PA5 pin changes from 0V to 3.3V.

**Your C code is not "controlling the hardware", it is driving this fetch-decode-execute pipeline. ** Every beat of the pipeline is performing the same set of mechanical operations: fetching, decoding, executing, updating PC, and endless loop. From the moment you press the compile button to the time the PA5 pin is pulled high, every step in between is deterministic, traceable, and purely mechanical.

## Memory mapped I/O: Why writing an address can light up the LED

There is another less intuitive but extremely powerful feature of the von Neumann architecture: **I/O devices are also mapped into the same address space. **

ARM's default memory map allocates the 32-bit address space (4GB) as follows:

```
0x00000000 - 0x1fffffff  Code（Flash, 512MB）
0x20000000 - 0x3fffffff  SRAM（512MB）
0x40000000 - 0x5fffffff  Peripherals (512MB, peripheral registers)
0x60000000 - 0x9fffffff  External RAM
0xa0000000 - 0xdfffffff  External Device
0xe0000000 - 0xffffffff  System (NVIC, SysTick, MPU, SCB, etc.)
```

When you write `*(volatile uint32_t *)0x40020014 = 0x20` (still taking STM32F4's GPIOA as an example), the CPU does not know that this is a GPIO register. It only knows that this is a "write of 32-bit data at address 0x40020014." The address decoder sees that this address falls in the peripheral range (0x40000000-0x5FFFFFFF), and routes it to the APB bridge (Advanced Peripheral Bus bridge) in the AHB bus matrix, and then to the ODR register of GPIO port A.The output driver circuit inside the GPIO peripheral receives the signal that register bit 5 changes from 0 to 1. It turns on the corresponding PMOS (pull high) transistor, turns off the corresponding NMOS (pull low) transistor, and the pin voltage climbs from 0V to 3.3V in a few nanoseconds. If an LED and a current-limiting resistor are hung on the pin, the LED will light up.

From the assignment statement of the C code to the LED lighting, every step, every layer of hardware, and the charge and discharge of the gate capacitance of every MOSFET are deterministic.  No magic. There is no "operating system doing it for you". On the bare metal, your STR instruction breaks down a two-layer bus bridge (AHB → APB), a GPIO register, a pair of output driver MOSFETs, an external pin, and finally turns into a string of photons escaping from the LED surface and hitting your retina.

**This is penetration. Only when you can penetrate this layer can you truly understand the connection point between "computing" and "physics". **

## Simplification vs Complexity: RISC vs CISC The Battle of the Century

There's an important design philosophy divide between the two worlds of embedded and desktop CPUs that's worth understanding, because it explains why there's ARM in your car and x86 in your laptop.

**The core idea of ​​RISC (Reduced Instruction Set Computer) is that each instruction does only one very simple thing, and the CPU pipelines them extremely quickly. ** The instruction length is regular (traditional ARM instructions are 32 bits, Cortex-M's Thumb-2 is a 16/32-bit mix), and each instruction is one clock cycle (at least in terms of design goals). ARM is RISC. RISC-V is RISC.

**The core idea of ​​​​CISC (Complex Instruction Set Computer) is: use one complex instruction to accomplish many things. **The command length is not fixed. x86 is CISC.

Why ARM wins embedded? Because the most urgent thing about embedded is **power consumption**. RISC is simple → simple decoding logic → fewer transistors → low power consumption → no need for a heat sink. S32K144 runs at full speed 112MHz and consumes about tens of milliwatts. CISC uses microcode to break down complex instructions into internal micro-operations, runs a RISC-like execution engine internally, is externally compatible with old programs, and uses modern technology internally to accelerate it. This is "compatibility for complexity."

For embedded engineers, RISC also has a key benefit: **ARM Thumb-2**: adding 32-bit instructions to the 16-bit Thumb instructions, the code density is about 30%~40% higher than the pure 32-bit ARM mode. The same 512KB Flash can hold one-third more code than pure ARM mode.

## Your ECU is a von Neumann machine (but not that simple)

Now open the data sheet of any automotive MCU (such as NXP S32K144) and look at its block diagram.

```
         +-------------------+
         | ARM Cortex-M4F | ← CPU (calculator + controller)
         |   112 MHz         |
         +--------+----------+
                  |
         +--------+----------+
         | AHB Bus Matrix | ← Multilayer Bus Crossbar Switch
         +--+----+----+-----+
            |    |    |
       +----+ +--+--+ +----+
|SRAM| |Flash| |Peripherals|
       |64KB| |512KB| |    |
       +----+ +-----+ +----+
         ↑       ↑       ↑
       Memory Program + Data I/O
```

SRAM = memory (data). Flash = memory (program + constant data). Peripheral area = I/O interface.

**But wait. Turing's paper tape reads and writes from the same medium, but here the program is in Flash and the data is in SRAM. Is this a von Neumann architecture? **

Strictly speaking, Cortex-M uses the **Modified Harvard Architecture** (Modified Harvard Architecture): There are two independent buses inside the CPU core, the I-Code bus (read instructions) and the D-Code bus (read and write data), allowing simultaneous instruction fetching and memory access. But at the system level, Flash and SRAM are uniformly addressed into a continuous address space (0x00000000 - 0xFFFFFFFF) and accessed using the same AHB bus matrix. So it's von Neumann on "unified addressing" and Harvard on "internal parallel fetch/fetch".**Why is this important to you? ** When you single-step in the debugger, the CPU can read the next instruction from Flash and the operand from SRAM at the same time. This is called the starting point of "instruction-level parallelism". If it is a pure von Neumann (a bus), instruction fetching and memory access must be serial, which is the "Von Neumann bottleneck".

**What about the three elements of a Turing machine? **

| Turing Machine (1936) | Von Neumann (1945) | S32K144 (in your hand) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Paper tape (infinite length) | Storage (memory + disk) | 512KB Flash + 64KB SRAM |
| Read and write head (status + symbol) | CPU (ALU + register) | Cortex-M4F core |
| State table (finite rules) | Program (instruction sequence) | The firmware you burned |

Your `CAN0->IFLAG1 = (1 << 15)` compiles into the STR instruction, targeting address 0x40024030 (FlexCAN's IFLAG1 register). The CPU reads this instruction from Flash and decodes it and finds that it is "write memory". The ALU calculates the target address and the data bus writes the value. The combinational logic inside the CAN module detects the change of the interrupt flag bit, triggers the interrupt controller NVIC, and the CPU enters the interrupt service function. **Every C sentence you write is a chain of cause and effect that can be traced to the end. From code to digital logic to semiconductor physics, there are no breaks, no ambiguities. **

## 80 years, from drawings to roads: a silent relay

Let’s stretch out the timeline and see the whole picture.

In 1936, Turing defined the boundaries of computing using an infinitely long paper tape and a finite state table. In the same year, Church gave an equivalent definition using the lambda calculus.

In 1945, von Neumann sketched out the blueprint of the EDVAC architecture on a train, turning Turing's abstract model into a buildable circuit logic framework. In his report, he even described the ALU's logical structure, instruction format, addressing mode, and I/O scheduling in detail.

In 1947, Bardeen, Bratton, and Shockley of Bell Labs invented the point-contact transistor. It replaces vacuum tubes, is thousands of times smaller, and reduces power consumption by several orders of magnitude.

In 1958, Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments built the first integrated circuit, which combined transistors, resistors and capacitors on the same germanium chip. The following year, Robert Noyce of Fairchild implemented a commercial integrated circuit using a silicon-based planar process. From then on, electronic components were no longer "assembled" but "engraved" on silicon.

In 1971, Intel's Federico Fagin designed and built the Intel 4004, the world's first commercial microprocessor. 2250 transistors, 10μm process, 4-bit data bus. In the 1970s, Fagin used the most primitive tools to manually draw the polyester film of the mask, and then used a physical mask to perform photolithography to carve the CPU into the silicon wafer. In Fagin’s own words: “Back then, you could see every transistor with the naked eye.”

## The Chinese are also building computers

While Fagin was designing the Intel 4004 in California, China was pursuing its own computing path.

In 1960, Xia Peisu led a team to develop China's first independently designed general-purpose electronic computer: the 107 machine. It was built with vacuum tubes, performed about 2500 operations per second, and had only 1K words of memory (about 4KB). Compared with her contemporary American IBM 7090, the performance of the 107 machine was about two orders of magnitude worse. But it is a complete, usable, general-purpose computer designed and manufactured by the Chinese from scratch. Xia Peisu later trained generations of Chinese computer scientists. She was the first transmitter of the "Chinese Computing Path".In 1975, Wang Xuan began an almost crazy project: using computers to process Chinese characters. All computer systems at the time were in English, and ASCII encoding required only 128 characters. There are thousands of commonly used Chinese characters and tens of thousands of total characters. If each word was stored in a lattice, a 64×64 lattice would require 512 bytes, and thousands of words would require several MB of storage, which was an astronomical figure at the time (the mainstream disk capacity at that time was only tens of MB, but memory and floppy disks were measured in KB). Wang Xuan's solution: use outlines to describe glyphs, store not the lattice, but the mathematical curve of the stroke boundary, and then let the computer "calculate" the lattice when outputting. This is "contour + compression", the optimal solution under limited resources. Wang Xuan's Chinese character laser typesetting system allowed China to skip the era of type printing and directly enter electronic typesetting. The baton he handed over is still running today.

In the UK, another path to computing is also sprouting.

In 1985, Acorn Computer released the first ARM processor (ARM1). Sophie Wilson designed the instruction set and Steve Furber designed the hardware. ARM1 barely generates heat after being powered on. Wilson thought the chip was broken, but in fact it just consumed too little power. In 2005, ARM Cortex-M3 was released and has dominated the embedded MCU market ever since. In every car you drive, there may be dozens of Arm Cortex-M cores running simultaneously.

**Turing → Von Neumann → Shockley → Kilby → Noyce → Fagin → Furber & Wilson → Design Team of S32K144 → You. **

The S32K144 you burned on the debugging board is the end of hundreds of years of human scientific accumulation. From Napier's bone chip, to Babbage's Analytical Engine, to Turing's paper tape, to von Neumann's 101-page report, to Fagin's hand-drawn mask, to TSMC's 55nm copper interconnect, every link has been connected. There are no breaks, no miracles, everything is the accumulation of scientific discoveries and engineering iterations. 

After your firmware is burned in, it runs in the chip day after day: processing CAN messages and performing UDS diagnosis. The car it drives also shuttles through the city every day, completing the most ordinary commute to and from get off work. The next engineer who takes over this MCU will open your source code, read your comments, and adjust your calibration data. **The stick you take over will be passed on to the next person one day. **
