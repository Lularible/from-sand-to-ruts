# 2.5 Semiconductor Manufacturing

## A speck of dust can destroy an entire circuit

The first real "engineering" job I had was as a yield engineer. My daily job is to track defects on glass substrates. Which panel units are good, which ones are bad and why.

This job taught me one thing: in semiconductor manufacturing, a speck of dust is a disaster.

It's not rhetoric, it's physical fact.

The line width of semiconductor manufacturing is the width of the smallest line drawn on the substrate using photolithography. In modern chip technology, it is tens of nanometers to several nanometers, and in panel technology, it is several microns. A dust particle floating in the air is about 0.5 to 10 **microns** in diameter. Therefore, the diameter of an ordinary particle of dust may be hundreds to thousands of times the minimum line width of the chip, and several times the minimum line width of the panel.

Imagine you are drawing lines on a piece of A4 paper, and the line width is required to be no more than 1 mm. Then a stone a few centimeters in diameter falls on the paper, which is the equivalent effect of a particle of dust falling on the substrate. It will completely cover a large number of patterns, causing large areas of open circuits, short circuits, and missing patterns.

Therefore, one of the core technologies of fab may not be a specific piece of equipment, but **cleanliness**. In a clean room, the number of particles per cubic meter of air is strictly limited. And a person, a person sitting still, emits a large number of particles (skin debris, fibers, bacteria) into the air every minute. Therefore, all operations in the fab that come into contact with the substrate are automated. Humans wear fully sealed dust-free suits, with only their eyes exposed. It's not about protecting people, it's about protecting the substrate from human contamination.

People are the biggest source of pollution in fab.

## Five-step cycle: "Building a city" on the substrate

The macroscopic process of semiconductor manufacturing is a set of five-step cycles that are repeated many times. Each cycle adds new material to the substrate surface, changes material properties, or removes unwanted material.

**In chip manufacturing**, a three-layer structure is finally stacked on the silicon substrate:
1. **FEOL** (Front End Of Line, front channel): transistor layer. Including well doping, gate oxidation, polysilicon/metal gate, source and drain ion implantation. Make billions of individual MOSFETs on the surface of silicon.
2. **MEOL** (Middle End Of Line, Middle Road): Contact layer. Use metal contacts to bring the source, drain, and gate of the transistor to the surface.
3. **BEOL** (Back End Of Line, back channel): multi-layer metal interconnection line. The copper Damascus process is stacked layer by layer to connect billions of MOSFETs into logic gates, registers, and functional modules according to the design.

**In panel manufacturing**, the process is relatively simple: a TFT array is manufactured on a glass substrate, and then the subsequent display layer is added. The complexity of the panel process is lower than that of the chip, but it also requires core processes such as photolithography, etching, deposition, ion implantation, and CMP.

The entire process relies on five core processes: Lithography, Etch, Deposition, Ion Implantation, and CMP (Chemical Mechanical Polishing). These processes are used in both panel factories and chip factories. The principles are similar, but the scale is different. The line width of panel factories is at the micron level, while the line width of chip factories is at the nanometer level. I have personally seen the application of these processes in panel manufacturing at the panel factory. Below, I will restore the experience of the panel factory from a first-person perspective. Knowledge related to chip manufacturing is interspersed with the introduction of industry background.

## Lithography - using light to draw circuit patterns

### Photoresist: More than just “a layer of glue”

The soul of lithography is not in the light. In **photoresist** (photoresist).

Photoresist is a polymer material that is sensitive to light of specific wavelengths. A photochemical reaction occurs in the exposed area, changing its solubility in the developer. Positive photoresist (positive resist) becomes soluble after exposure, and the exposed areas are washed away after development, leaving a pattern of unexposed areas. Negative photoresist (negative resist) becomes insoluble after exposure, and the unexposed areas are washed away after development.

I have seen the photolithography process in panel factories. The panel factory uses a stepper, and the line width is at the micron level. Photoresist is coated on a glass substrate, exposed through a mask, and developed to form a pattern.The quality of the photoresist pattern determines the quality of the etched pattern. The photoresist sidewalls must be steep (to avoid line width deviation caused by bevel angles), the photoresist thickness must be uniform (to avoid uneven development), and the adhesion between the photoresist and the substrate must be strong (to avoid floating glue during development).

### Resolution: Rayleigh criterion

The resolution of photolithography is determined by the Rayleigh criterion:

CD = k₁ × λ / NA

where CD is the critical dimension (minimum resolvable linewidth), λ is the light source wavelength, NA is the numerical aperture of the projection objective, and k₁ is the process factor.

The shorter the wavelength, the higher the resolution. i-line (365nm ultraviolet light) can achieve a line width of hundreds of nanometers, and DUV (deep ultraviolet, 248nm or 193nm) can achieve a line width of tens of nanometers. The line width of the panel factory is at the micron level, and i-line or slightly shorter wavelength light sources can meet the demand.

**In chip manufacturing** (this is industry knowledge, not personal experience of panel manufacturers), when the line width enters the nanometer level, more extreme technologies are required: immersion lithography (filling liquid between the lens and the wafer to improve resolution), multi-patterning technology (dividing a layer into multiple exposures), EUV (extreme ultraviolet lithography, wavelength 13.5nm). But these technical panel factories don’t need it. The line width of the panel is at the micron level, and a stepper photolithography machine is sufficient.

## My first time looking at panel cross section on SEM

I still remember the first time I looked at the cross-section of the panel I cut on a scanning electron microscope (SEM).

The FIB has been sectioned and an area of ​​about 10μm × 5μm is exposed. I put the sample into the SEM chamber, evacuated it, and turned on the electron beam. The image on the screen gradually became clearer, and a spectacular picture slowly emerged.

The bottom is the glass substrate. On top of the glass substrate is the buffer layer. Not one layer, but multiple layers stacked on top of each other. Silicon nitride, silicon dioxide, and amorphous silicon are stacked layer by layer. The total thickness is less than 1μm. These layers undertake key tasks: blocking metal ions in the glass substrate from diffusing to the upper active layer in subsequent high-temperature processes; improving the interface quality on the backside of polysilicon; some layers have low thermal conductivity, which plays a thermal insulation role during laser annealing and helps polysilicon grains grow.

Above the buffer layer is the core structure of the TFT. I saw multiple layers of processes stacked together: active layer, gate metal layer, gate insulating layer, data line metal layer, contact holes, passivation layer, planarization layer, anode layer, pixel design layer, spacer pillar layer. Each layer is an independent process cycle: thin film deposition (CVD or PVD) → photoresist coating → exposure → development → etching → photoresist stripping → cleaning. Then comes the next level. Repeat this a dozen times.

Under SEM I can see the gate metal lines. Light gray strip. Between the gate and the active layer is the gate insulating layer. The dielectric layer, which is tens of nanometers thick, is barely visible in the SEM image, but I know it is there, taking full responsibility for isolating the gate and channel. Data lines are metal stacks. Sandwich structure, thicker than the gate, carries greater current. The anode is also a metal stack. Can both conduct electricity and reflect light.

Densely arranged vias (vias) are like micro-tunnels, connecting metal traces on different layers. Each through hole is only a few microns in diameter, but the metal wires passing through it must withstand hundreds of milliamps of current. Multiple layers of metal interconnects and insulation layers are stacked one on top of the other. The metal lines are wider as you go up (the upper metal carries more current and a longer distance across the panel) and the spacing is wider.

This is a three-dimensional microstructure with a sense of depth. What I saw was how the circuit diagram grew layer by layer on the substrate. It was not printing or assembly, but deposition, etching, and redeposition at the atomic level. The thickness of each layer of film is accurate to Å (0.1nm), and the width of each metal line is accurate to μm. Thousands of TFTs are arranged on a glass substrate, and the gate, source, and drain of each TFT are aligned within micron-level precision.

If you have never stared at the cross-section of modern micro-nano manufacturing under a microscope, it will be difficult for you to truly understand the weight of the word "precision". It is an engineering miracle built with three-dimensional structures stacked layer by layer on the micrometer or even nanometer scale.

## Etching - plasma "digging"

After the photoresist forms a pattern, the next step is to transfer the pattern to the surface of the substrate. This step is called etching.I have seen the etching process in panel factories. Wet etching uses a chemical solution to dissolve the material, but wet etching is isotropic, with similar etching rates in all directions. In other words, it not only digs down, but also digs sideways. For micron-scale devices, side etching errors can be tolerated. But for finer line widths. Lateral etching will eat up a large portion of the line width and the pattern will be scrapped.

Therefore, **dry etching** (plasma etching, also called reactive ion etching RIE) is used for etching critical dimensions.

The working principle of dry etching: Reactive gas is introduced into the vacuum chamber and a radio frequency electric field is applied to excite the plasma. Reactive radicals in the plasma chemically attack surfaces that are not protected by photoresist. At the same time, the positive ions in the plasma bombard the substrate surface vertically under the action of the electric field. This physical bombardment makes the etching rate on the bottom surface much higher than the side surface. Therefore, dry etching is **anisotropic**, digging quickly vertically and barely moving horizontally.

Another key to etching is selectivity. Ideally, the etchant would only etch the target material and not the photoresist or hard mask, but in reality this is not possible. The selectivity is not unlimited: every time a certain depth of etching is performed, part of the photoresist will be chipped away. If the etching depth is large, the photoresist must be thick enough to support the entire process. Otherwise, after the photoresist is eaten up, the area below will suffer.

**In chip manufacturing** (this is industry knowledge, not personal experience of panel manufacturers), etching requires higher precision. The line width of the chip is at the nanometer level, requiring more precise control of plasma density and ion energy. Some special processes (such as MEMS devices, 3D NAND flash memory) require extremely high aspect ratio etching, and the depth of the trench is much greater than the width, which requires special process cycles (such as the Bosch process, which alternates etching and deposition). But panel manufacturing doesn't require such extreme aspect ratios.

## Deposition - "covering" layer by layer

Semiconductor manufacturing requires the repeated addition of new layers of material to the surface of a substrate. The dielectric layer of the transistor gate, the metal wiring, and the insulating layer between layers all require a **deposition** process.

### CVD: Chemical Vapor Deposition

Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) passes gaseous precursors into a reaction chamber, causes a chemical reaction on the surface of the substrate, and deposits a solid thin film. CVD can deposit a variety of materials: silicon dioxide, silicon nitride, polysilicon, etc.

I've seen PECVD (Plasma Enhanced CVD) equipment in panel factories. Gas enters the chamber through a shower head. The radiofrequency electric field excites plasma and decomposes gas molecules into active free radicals. Free radicals diffuse in the cavity, reach the substrate surface, chemically adsorb and deposit into a thin film.

**Physical process of film formation**:
1. Gas enters the reaction chamber
2. Radio frequency input creates an electric field, decomposes the reactants, and generates active free radicals.
3. Free radicals diffuse to the substrate surface
4. Free radicals react on the surface of the substrate, nucleate first, and form islands.
5. The islands continue to grow, merge and form a continuous film
6. Gas by-products desorb from the substrate surface
7. Gas by-products are pumped out

These seven steps are completed in seconds. Each film formation is an atomic-level game of "island merging." From discrete nuclei to continuous films, from gaseous radicals to solid films. This is how the film "grows".

What is the role of plasma? In ordinary CVD, chemical reactions rely entirely on high temperatures to provide energy. However, high temperatures will change the already prepared doping distribution, and impurity atoms will diffuse at high temperatures, resulting in degradation of device characteristics. PECVD replaces thermal energy with plasma energy provided by a radiofrequency electric field, driving reactions at lower temperatures.

The design of the “shower head” (gas shower head) in the CVD chamber is crucial. It is a panel covered with small holes, and gas is sprayed evenly on the surface of the substrate from the small holes. If the gas distribution is uneven, a certain area will be deposited quickly and a certain area will be deposited slowly, the film thickness will be uneven, and the subsequent process will be uneven. Unevenness in one link will be magnified step by step in dozens of subsequent processes, eventually turning into yield loss.

### PVD - Physical Vapor Deposition (Sputtering)

The physics of physical vapor deposition (PVD, also known as sputtering) are completely different. It is not the product of a chemical reaction, but a physical process.I've seen PVD equipment in panel factories. In a high vacuum chamber, the metal to be made into a thin film is made into a target. Argon gas (Ar) is introduced into the cavity and high voltage is applied to excite the argon gas into plasma. Under the acceleration of the electric field, argon ions (Ar⁺) bombard the target surface with an energy of several hundred electron volts, like shooting the target with atomic-level "cannonballs". The metal atoms on the target surface are "beaten" (sputtered) and then deposited onto the opposite substrate to form a thin film.

This process is called Glow Discharge, a self-sustaining low-pressure gas discharge phenomenon. Argon ions are accelerated in the electric field and bombard the target (cathode); the atoms on the target surface gain energy and fly out, depositing on the substrate (anode).

**Four steps of sputtering film formation**:
1. Glow discharge, Ar⁺ generation: Under high vacuum conditions, a DC electric field is applied to the Ar atmosphere, glow discharge occurs, and Ar⁺ plasma is generated.
2. Ar⁺ bombards the target surface: Ar⁺ bombards the target surface with an energy of several hundred electron volts under the acceleration of the electric field.
3. The target atoms gain energy and fly out: The atoms in the target gain kinetic energy after being hit, break away from the target surface, and fly towards the opposite substrate.
4. The target atoms are attached to the substrate: The flying target atoms reach the surface of the substrate, chemically adsorb and form a thin film.

One of PVD's most ingenious designs is the magnetron. A permanent magnet array is placed on the back of the target to form a ring-shaped magnetic field. The magnetic field traps the electrons in closed orbits near the target surface. The electrons perform spiral motion in the magnetic field, which increases the probability of collision with argon atoms and greatly increases the plasma density and sputtering rate.

In chip manufacturing (this is industry knowledge, not personal experience of panel manufacturers), there is another deposition process: atomic layer deposition (ALD). A core feature of ALD is a self-limiting surface reaction that deposits only one atomic layer thick at a time. After one cycle, the film thickness increases by approximately 1Å (0.1nm). If you want a film with a certain thickness, you have to repeat multiple cycles. You don’t need to control “when to stop” because it will stop by itself. ALD is used in certain situations where atomic level thickness control is required (such as the gate dielectric layer of a chip). Panel manufacturing generally does not require this extreme thickness control accuracy.

## A speck of dust falls on the reticle - the nightmare of repeated defects

This is something I have personally experienced.

We had a batch of glass substrates and there was a strange pattern on the yield map. Almost every panel unit has fail in the same location. No matter where the unit is on the substrate. Center or edge, top or bottom, the fail position is the same.

This "repeated per panel unit" defect distribution usually points to one source: the photomask or reticle. Because during photolithography, there is a pattern area (field) on the mask, and the stepper repeatedly projects the pattern of the same field onto each unit position of the substrate, and one field covers one panel unit. If there is a defect on the mask, a speck of dust, a tiny missing pattern, the defect will be copied to every panel unit on the substrate.

There are hundreds to thousands of panel units on each glass substrate. If a speck of dust falls on the mask, hundreds to thousands of panel units will be scrapped at the same time, and the yield will plummet.

We stopped the lithography machine and sent the reticle for inspection. On the mask inspection equipment, I found that there was a tiny particle attached somewhere in the field, probably at the end of a metal interconnect layer. Particle diameter is approximately 0.5μm. On a chip scale, 0.5μm is huge. It just blocked the light in that area, causing the metal line to break on each die.

How did this particle land on the mask? We reviewed the mask usage records and found that the load port seal (O-ring) of this lithography machine was slightly worn the day before, and a small amount of debris was generated when the mask was transferred. The O-ring material is fluorine rubber, which ages under vacuum and ultraviolet radiation, causing cracks on the surface. Tiny rubber particles in the cracks fell off and were electrostatically attracted to the mask.

The O-ring was replaced, the mask was cleaned, and the yield was restored.

This is the true version of "a speck of dust destroys the entire circuit."

## Ion implantation: "bumping" impurity atoms into the crystal lattice

The purpose of ion implantation: to intentionally introduce impurity atoms into the semiconductor lattice to change the conductivity type (P-type or N-type) and conductivity concentration of the semiconductor.

The working process of the ion implanter: ionize the impurity source, use a mass spectrometer to screen out the precise ion mass, let only the target impurity ions pass, and screen out other ions and neutral particles. These ions are then accelerated with high pressure to form a high-speed ion beam. The ion beam strikes the surface of the substrate, penetrates to a certain depth, and is embedded in the crystal lattice.

The injected dose determines the doping concentration, and the injected energy determines the doping depth. Low energy is used for shallow doping, and high energy is used for deep doping.

After the injection, the crystal lattice was bombarded to pieces. The injected ions broke many bonds and the atoms deviated from the position of the crystal lattice. Therefore, **thermal annealing** must be done after implantation: rapid heat treatment at high temperatures to rearrange the atoms into a perfect lattice, and at the same time allow impurity atoms to occupy alternative positions in the lattice (become electrically active doping atoms).

In panel factories (LTPS process), ion implantation is a key step in forming TFTs. A TFT on a glass substrate requires multiple injections:

- **Channel Doping**: Controls the threshold voltage of the TFT. After boron ion implantation, the threshold voltage becomes positive. A larger gate voltage is required to form the inversion layer.

- **Source-Drain Doping**: Forms the source and drain regions of NMOS and PMOS. Change the conductivity type of the semiconductor to form a PN junction, preventing minority carriers from passing through. After implantation, the contact resistance of the source and drain decreases.

- Lightly Doped Drain (LDD): A thin, lightly doped layer formed between the source/drain and the channel of some TFTs. This thin layer has a large resistance, which can reduce the electric field in the horizontal direction and suppress the "hot carrier effect": carriers are accelerated under a strong electric field, obtain high energy, and destroy the oxide layer.

Multiple injections, each time the ion type, energy, and dose are different. The energy determines the injection depth, with low energy penetrating only a few nanometers and high energy penetrating tens of nanometers. The dose determines the doping concentration, the channel doping is lightly doped, and the source and drain doping is heavily doped.

Each injection is an "atomic shotgun shot," in which ions are accelerated at high pressure and smashed into the crystal lattice.

After implantation, it must be rapidly thermally annealed. The role of annealing is to repair lattice damage (the injected ions break many bonds) and allow impurity atoms to occupy alternative positions in the lattice. If there is no annealing, the lattice is still chaotic and the doping atoms are still in the lattice gap, and the TFT cannot work.

The uniformity of ion implantation is key. The beam must scan the entire substrate surface evenly, otherwise the device parameters at different locations will be different. The electrostatic neutralization system in the beam is also important. The ion beam is positively charged and will accumulate charge when hitting the surface of the insulating substrate, which may break down the dielectric layer.

## CMP: Chemical Mechanical Polishing

Photolithography requires the substrate surface to be extremely flat, otherwise the depth of focus will not be enough, and the photolithography patterns at different locations on the same substrate will have different blur levels. After each layer of film is deposited, the surface may be uneven. The next photolithography process cannot accurately transfer the pattern.

CMP (Chemical Mechanical Polishing) is responsible for smoothing the surface.

The CMP device is a rotating polishing pad (pad), the substrate is pressed against the pad, and **polishing slurry** (slurry) is dropped at the same time. The slurry contains nanoscale abrasives and chemical active agents.

Chemistry and mechanics work together: chemicals soften the surface, abrasive particles remove the softened layer, and the new surface is exposed and softened again by the chemicals. In this cycle, the surface gradually becomes flat.

The difficulty with CMP is that "grinding evenly" is difficult to truly achieve. The removal rate is different under different pattern densities. Areas with large metal patterns are polished faster (dishing) than areas with dense lines. The dielectric layer in densely patterned areas will be excessively abraded (erosion). Optimization of the CMP process: slurry formula, mat hardness, down force, and rotation speed are important tasks for yield engineers.

**In chip manufacturing** (this is industry knowledge, not personal experience of panel manufacturers), CMP is used more widely: back-end metal interconnect layers require CMP to grind the copper lines flat, and via filling (tungsten or copper) requires CMP. The CMP requirements of the chip are more extreme, and the flatness is controlled to nanometer-level deviations. CMP at panel factories is relatively simple and is mainly used for planarization of certain layers.

## In the production line, each glass substrate is the work of one month, hundreds of processes, and hundreds of engineers.

After I left the production line, I occasionally thought about those days under the bright yellow lights.

It takes about a month for a glass substrate to enter the production line and complete all processes. It's not a continuous month, it's a month of waiting, transmitting, processing, and testing between different devices. The whole process goes through hundreds of processes. Each process has specialized equipment, specialized process engineers, and specialized testing steps.

There are hundreds to thousands of panel units on a substrate. There are millions to tens of millions of TFTs per unit. Each TFT has three terminals: gate, source, and drain. If any parameter of any terminal exceeds the spec, the unit will be useless. If any parameter of any process deviates, the entire substrate or even the entire batch of glass substrates may be scrapped.

The value of the substrate continues to grow with the process. The optical substrate costs about tens of dollars, the completed TFT backplane costs about several hundred dollars, and the completed evaporation and packaging costs about several thousand dollars.

Therefore, every process engineer in the production line bears huge costs. An oversight or a wrong parameter setting can result in hundreds of thousands of dollars in losses. If a process drift is not discovered in time, the loss can be millions of dollars.

What I learned in the production line is not how cool "high technology" is, but how difficult "control" is. Arrange a large number of atoms in the way you want, with very small deviations allowed, and it must be continuously replicated on a glass substrate at a rate of dozens of pieces per hour and with high yield. This is not "high technology", this is "highest technology".

It’s one of the most complex man-made systems on the planet, and it’s just one step in providing the core components of a car’s ECU.
