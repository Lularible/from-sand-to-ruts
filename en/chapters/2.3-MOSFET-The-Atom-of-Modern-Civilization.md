# 2.3 MOSFET: The Atom of Modern Civilization

## Faucet Inspiration

Renovate the faucets in your home.

Install a solenoid valve on the water pipe. The solenoid valve does not directly contact the water flow. It uses an electromagnetic coil to generate a magnetic field and pull a diaphragm. The diaphragm presses against the valve seat to block the flow of water, or it relaxes to allow water to pass through. The control end (solenoid coil) and the water flow path are completely isolated with a rubber diaphragm in between.

You use a weak current (tens of milliamperes) and an excitation coil to control a huge flow of water (several liters per second, pressure of several atmospheres).

What you are holding in your hand is a "hydraulic version of MOSFET".

The full name of MOSFET is broken down: M (Metal) is the metal gate, which is equivalent to the coil of the solenoid valve. O (Oxide) is the oxide layer insulator, which is the layer of rubber that isolates the diaphragm from the water flow. S (Semiconductor) is the semiconductor channel below, which is the water pipe itself.

FET (Field Effect Transistor) means: This is a device that uses electric field (not current) to control conductivity. The gate consumes almost no current in the DC state. Because there is a layer of insulator (silicon oxide) between the gate and the channel, theoretically, current cannot flow at all.

This faucet analogy has two profound implications.

First layer: **Isolation**. The control end and the controlled end are not in direct contact, and the control end does not consume the energy of the controlled end. This is very different from a BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor). BJTs are "current controlled" devices, and current must be continuously injected into the base to maintain collector-emitter conduction. The current gain β of an NPN tube is about 100. If you want to drive a collector current of 100mA, the base must continuously provide a current of about 1mA. While MOSFETs are "voltage controlled" devices, the gate requires zero current at DC. The gate is just one pole of a capacitor. It only needs to be charged and discharged once to reach the required voltage, and then it can "maintain" the conductive state without the need for continuous injection of current.

Second layer: **Analog adjustable resistor**. The faucet does not only have two states: "fully open" and "fully closed". Turn it to half open, and the water flow will be half full. MOSFET does not only have two states: "on" and "off". Between "on" and "off", there is a linear region (also called a triode region), and the channel is like a voltage-controlled resistor. The higher the gate voltage, the lower the channel resistance. This "electronically controlled adjustable resistor" characteristic is a huge asset in analog circuit design (such as the output stage of an operational amplifier) ​​and power management (such as the pass tube of an LDO).

## In-depth analysis of NMOS - from band bending to inversion layer

NMOS is made on a P-type silicon substrate. P-type substrate means that most carriers are holes and there are very few free electrons. On the surface of this substrate, two N-type heavily doped regions (N+) are formed through ion implantation, one is called the source (Source) and the other is called the drain (Drain). The source and drain are both electron-rich, but the substrate is hole-rich. Therefore, a PN junction is formed between the source and the substrate and between the drain and the substrate.

Above the substrate between the two N+ regions, a layer of silicon dioxide (SiO₂) is thermally grown (in a dry or wet oxygen atmosphere at 800-1000°C). This layer of silicon oxide is an insulator. It has a bandgap of about 9 eV, making it an almost perfect insulating layer. Then on top of the silicon oxide, a layer of heavily doped polysilicon (or metal) is deposited, which is the **gate** (Gate).

```
Source (S) Gate (G) Drain (D)
         [N+] [Polysilicon] [N+]
           \    SiO₂     |    SiO₂    /
            \============+===========/
             | Channel area |
             | P-type substrate (Body) |
             +======================+
```

When there is no voltage on the gate (V_GS = 0), there are two back-to-back PN junctions between the source and drain. Regardless of whether forward or reverse voltage is applied to the drain, there is always a PN junction in reverse bias, no current can flow, and the NMOS is in a **off** state.

Now a positive voltage is applied to the gate (V_GS > 0), and the gate, oxide layer, and substrate form a capacitor. The gate is the upper plate, the substrate is the lower plate, and silicon oxide is the dielectric. When the gate is positively charged, it repels the positive charges (holes) in the P-type substrate, and the holes are pushed away, leaving behind negatively charged immobilized boron ions. At the same time, the positive electric field of the gate attracts minority carriers (electrons) to the substrate surface below the gate.When V_GS is relatively small, the substrate surface under the gate only has "fewer holes", but a continuous electron layer has not yet been formed. This is called **depletion**: the holes in the surface area are exhausted, leaving a region of negative ions. At this time, if voltage is applied to the drain, there is still no significant current (just leakage).

When V_GS continues to increase and reaches a critical value (threshold voltage V_TH), the electron concentration on the substrate surface exceeds the hole concentration. In other words, the semiconductor type in this area is flipped from P-type to N-type. This is called inversion. The essence of the inversion is that the energy band bending caused by the gate electric field moves the Fermi level at the substrate surface from close to the valence band (P-type characteristics) to close to the conduction band (N-type characteristics).

Now, a continuous N-type conductive path appears between the source (N+) and the drain (N+), which is the **channel**. Electrons can be injected from the source, through the channel, and to the drain. Current can flow freely and the NMOS conducts.

The threshold voltage V_TH itself can be written as an equation:

V_TH = V_FB + 2φ_F + (√(4ε_si q N_A φ_F)) / C_ox

This looks scary, but it's easy to take it apart:

- V_FB (flatband voltage): The work function difference between the gate and the substrate, and the influence of fixed charges in the oxide layer. The work function difference between the polysilicon gate and the silicon substrate usually biases V_FB toward negative values, which is not conducive to NMOS turn-on. The purpose of metal gate technology is to adjust V_FB in the positive direction.
- **2φ_F**: The minimum silicon surface potential required to "flip" the substrate surface to inversion. φ_F is the Fermi potential of the substrate, which is related to the doping concentration. The heavier the doping, the larger the 2φ_F and the higher the V_TH.
- √(4ε_si q N_A φ_F) / C_ox: depletion layer charge term. The higher the substrate doping concentration N_A, the more charge in the depletion layer, and the gate requires additional voltage to overcome it. The larger the oxide capacitance C_ox (the thinner the oxide layer or the higher the high-k dielectric constant), the stronger the gate control, and the smaller this term is.

V_TH is not a value that can be set arbitrarily. It is a physical result determined by the work function, doping concentration, oxide layer thickness, and interface state density.

## PMOS is to reverse everything

PMOS has two P+ source and drain electrodes on an N-type substrate. When Add negative voltage to the gate (V_GS < 0), the positive electric field repels electrons in the N-type substrate and attracts holes to the surface, forming a P-type inversion layer. Holes circulate as majority carriers between the P+ source, P-type channel, and P+ drain.

The carriers of PMOS are holes, and the mobility of holes is about 1/2 to 1/3 that of electrons. Therefore, the driving capability of PMOS of the same size is only about 1/3 of that of NMOS. This is why in CMOS circuits, if you want PMOS and NMOS to have symmetrical driving capabilities (such as an inverter), the width of PMOS is usually designed to be 2-3 times that of NMOS.

Three sentences summarize the core characteristics of a MOSFET: It is a voltage-controlled device with complete insulation between gate and channel. This insulating layer (silicon oxide) has become as thin as 1-2nm in modern processes, about 5 to 10 silicon atoms thick. The conductivity of the channel is continuously adjusted by the gate voltage, and the MOSFET is not just a switch, it is a "voltage controlled resistor".

## A Vt troubleshooting case

One week, an anomaly was discovered in the test workshop: the yield of a batch of glass substrates dropped from the normal 96% to 78%. The defect distribution map is not random bad pixels, but failed panel units evenly distributed on the entire substrate, with the edges and center almost the same. This "field-wide" fail mode usually does not point to particle contamination (particle contamination is generally random or clustered), but to a global process parameter drift.

The failure analysis team sent the failed panel unit for parameter analysis. The semiconductor parameter analyzer scans the TEG test structures one by one. The results came out. The TFT threshold voltage V_TH on this batch of substrates was overall high, on average 0.15V higher than the spec center value.

0.15V doesn't sound like much. But the nominal operating voltage of this generation of technology is low voltage. V_TH goes higher from normal, which means the switching speed slows down and the gate takes longer to open the channel. Because it takes longer for V_GS to rise from 0 to V_TH, the panel will show anomalies if gate latencies on the critical path collectively increase under high refresh rate testing.

What’s even weirder is: the scan test fails at full speed of 112MHz, but it passes when it is reduced to 40MHz. Signal integrity is okay, power supply noise is within spec, temperatures are normal. This is a pure "not enough timing" because the V_TH is too high and the door opens and closes too slowly.

Next is the process I am most familiar with: **Tracing**.

I called up all the process logs for this batch of substrates. Each substrate has hundreds of processes in the production line, and each process has dozens of parameters. Let me first focus on the ion implantation step. Because V_TH is mainly affected by the channel doping concentration (determined by the threshold adjustment implant) and the gate insulating layer thickness (determined by the deposition process). The thickness of the gate insulating layer was measured on an ellipsometer and the data was normal. That was a problem with ion implantation.

The threshold adjustment implant (Vt adjust implant) of this batch of substrates was run on an Axcelis medium-beam ion implanter. I found the injection logs for this device. Beam energy: 15 keV, injection dose: 2.3 × 10¹² cm⁻², consistent with the formula. But the calibration record of the Faraday cup shows a detail: the position reading of the calibration plate (Faraday cup calibration flag) is offset by about 12 μm from the normal value. This means that the beam measurement point is slightly offset in space. The measured beam current is not the entire beam current, but the low current area at the edge of the beam spot. The Faraday cup considers "dose arrived", but this measurement point offset means that the beam distribution on the substrate may be uneven and the local dose may be low.

The local dose is low → the donor doping concentration is low → this should increase V_TH. The actual V_TH is indeed high, but the total dose reading is consistent with the formula, and the fail is uniform across the field, so it is also necessary to check whether the impurities are activated.

Keep checking. I looked at the log of the anneal step again. Rapid thermal annealing (RTA, Rapid Thermal Anneal) after ion implantation is a key step to activate impurity atoms. In the LTPS process, the implanted phosphorus atoms need to "sit" into the correct position in the crystal lattice at high temperatures of 600-650°C to become effective donor impurities. If the annealing temperature is not enough or the time is not enough, the implanted phosphorus atoms are not fully activated. If the donor concentration is equivalently low, V_TH will be high.

Sure enough, the log of the RTA machine showed that the peak annealing temperature of this batch of substrates was about 25°C lower than the set value. The reason is that a tungsten halogen lamp tube in the RTA cavity is aging and the output power is insufficient. The temperature measuring pyrometer detected that the temperature was low and automatically extended the heating time to compensate. However, the "high temperature and short time" characteristics of rapid thermal annealing determine that extending the heating time cannot fully compensate for the decrease in peak temperature. Before the peak temperature is reached, the activation rate of impurities will not increase.

After replacing the lamps, the V_TH of the next batch of substrates returned to spec, and the yield returned to 96%.

It took us a week from discovering the anomaly to finding the root cause. During this week, I spent almost 12 hours a day in the delivery line. Look at the data during the day, wait for the accelerated test results at night, and open a war room to discuss it the next day.

This is engineering, not magic, it's data, physics, elimination, uncompromising doubt, and a little bit of luck.

## The limit of oxide layer: when the insulator is so thin that it leaks electricity

The description "glass a few atoms thick" sounds romantic, but in physics it has a serious consequence: **quantum tunneling**.

When the thickness of the gate oxide layer drops below 2nm, electrons do not need to "climb" the barrier of the oxide layer (the conduction band bottom of SiO₂ is about 3.1 eV higher than silicon), they can directly pass through the oxide layer. The quantum tunneling probability decreases exponentially with the barrier thickness, but when the thickness reaches the 1-2nm level, this probability becomes non-negligible.

The physical image of "passing through" is as follows: electrons have a certain probability wave function on the gate side, and this wave function decays exponentially inside the oxide layer. However, if the oxide layer is thin enough and the wave function has not decayed to zero on the channel side, the electrons have a probability of appearing on the other end of the oxide layer. This is called direct tunneling.

At the 90nm process node, gate leakage current has accounted for a significant proportion of the total chip power consumption. When the gate oxide layer of a MOSFET is 1.2nm, the gate leakage current density is about 1-10 A/cm², which does not sound large, but multiplied by the total gate area of ​​all MOSFETs on the die, it may be tens to hundreds of milliamperes. At 65nm, the traditional silicon dioxide gate dielectric can no longer hold up, and the leakage density has soared to an unacceptable level.

The solution is **High-K Metal Gate** (HKMG, High-K Metal Gate). In 2007, Intel took the lead in mass-producing this new material on the 45nm process (Penryn architecture). The core idea is the capacitance formula:

C = ε₀ × κ × A / t

where C is the gate capacitance, ε₀ is the vacuum permittivity, κ is the relative permittivity, A is the area, and t is the physical thickness.

To maintain the same gate capacitance (i.e. the same ability of the gate to control the channel), the physical thickness t can be larger if you use a higher κ material. As the physical thickness increases, the quantum tunneling probability decreases exponentially, because the tunneling probability ≈ exp(-t × constant).

Silica has a κ value of approximately 3.9. The κ value of the hafnium oxide (HfO₂) selected by Intel is about 20-25, which is about 5-6 times that of SiO₂. This means that the physical thickness of hafnium oxide can be 5-6 times that of silicon dioxide without changing the capacitance equivalent. The tunneling nightmare of 1nm is turned into a safe thickness of 5-6nm with HfO₂.

However, hafnium oxide cannot directly contact the polysilicon gate. The interface state density between the two is too high, which will cause Fermi level pinning and V_TH cannot be accurately controlled. Therefore, the gate material must also be changed from polysilicon to **metal**. Different work function metals correspond to different V_TH. NMOS uses metals whose work function is close to the conduction band of silicon (such as TiAlN), and PMOS uses metals whose work function is close to the silicon valence band (such as TiN). This is where the name "metal grid" comes from. It's not that "metal replaces polysilicon", but "in order to be compatible with high-k materials, metal must be used."

This process change in 2007 kept Moore’s Law alive for another ten years. The MCU you are debugging on your car today, such as the Renesas RH850 series or ST’s Stellar series. Their gate dielectric is already a mature application for HKMG. This process started in 2007, after more than ten years of iteration and optimization, is now the standard for mid-to-high-end automotive MCU products.

## Short channel effect - when the drain starts to steal the work of the gate

The channels of MOSFETs continue to shorten, from 10μm to 1μm to 100nm to 28nm. But when the channel length shrinks to a level comparable to the width of the depletion layer, a serious problem arises: the electric field of the drain begins to affect the source side. 

In long-channel devices, gate voltage is the only effective means to control the channel energy band. The drain voltage mainly drops in the depletion region near the drain and has little impact on the potential distribution near the source.

But when the channel length shrinks to less than about 100nm (depending on the doping concentration and oxide thickness), the electric field from the drain begins to "penetrate" to the source side. This directly lowers the potential barrier between the source and channel, making it easier for electrons to be injected from the source into the channel, even if the gate voltage has not reached V_TH. This is called DIBL (Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering). The consequence of DIBL is that V_TH decreases as the drain voltage increases. You don't want it to turn on, but it is "pulled" into a semi-conducting state by the drain voltage, causing the off state in the digital circuit to no longer be clean.

Another related effect is that the **Subthreshold Swing** (Subthreshold Swing) cannot be reduced. In an ideal long-channel MOSFET, for every approximately 60mV decrease in gate voltage, the subthreshold current decreases by an order of magnitude (60mV/decade). This is because the subthreshold current is mainly determined by the thermal emission mechanism. This limit is naturally given by the temperature dependence of the Boltzmann factor e^(qV_GS/kT). But in short-channel devices, DIBL and drain coupling degrade the sub-threshold swing to 80-100 mV/dec.

what does that mean? If a device's subthreshold swing is 100mV/dec, to get it to go from "on" (1mA) to "off" (1nA, 6 orders of magnitude lower), you need to lower V_GS by about 600mV. And VDD may be only 1.0V. 60% of the voltage space of your "switch" is wasted between "not yet closed" and "already on". And this is a helpless compromise between power consumption and performance, where both parties can only give in.

## FinFET - "stand up" the transistor

In 2011, Intel introduced a revolutionary device structure on the 22nm process: **FinFET** (Fin Field Effect Transistor, also known as Tri-Gate).

Traditional MOSFETs are "planar": the channel extends horizontally across the surface of the substrate, and the gate is directly above the channel, which can only control the channel from above.

FinFET makes the channel area into a silicon "fin" standing on the substrate, with a width of only a few nanometers to more than ten nanometers and a height of tens of nanometers. The gate surrounds the fin from three sides: left, right, and top. Because the gate surrounds three sides of the channel, the gate's electrostatic control over the channel is greatly enhanced.

To what extent is it enhanced? The gate can even "drain" the entire fin. That is, the cross-section of the fin is completely under the control of the gate electric field. There is no point "far" from the gate. This greatly reduces the ability of the drain electric field to couple to the source via the substrate, and DIBL is significantly suppressed. Subthreshold swing improves to nearly ideal 65-70 mV/dec.

The emergence of FinFET has allowed the process nodes to continue to advance from 22nm to 14nm, 10nm, 7nm, 5nm, and 3nm. But the price of FinFET is: a sharp increase in manufacturing complexity. The height and width of the fins must be extremely uniform, the size deviation from fin to fin must not be more than a few atoms, the spacing between fins must be precisely controlled, and the gate must be deposited with uniform high-k dielectric and metal gates simultaneously on three sides. This requires the atomic layer deposition (ALD) process, which "sticks" atoms one layer at a time.

From planar MOSFET to FinFET, the essence is to push the transistor from two dimensions to three dimensions. This is one of the most important structural changes in the history of chip manufacturing.

The next generation is GAA (Gate-All-Around, full surround gate), which goes a step further. Instead of using fins, GAA makes the channels into horizontal "nanosheets" or nanowires. The gate completely surrounds the channel from all four sides, upper left, lower right, completely surrounded. DIBL is almost eliminated. Subthreshold swings can approach the theoretical limit of 60 mV/dec. Samsung has already mass-produced GAA (they call it MBCFET) at the 3nm node, and TSMC is expected to introduce it at the 2nm node.

What FinFET and GAA tell us is not that “the technology is becoming more and more advanced,” but that “human beings’ control over the microstructure of matter has reached the point where devices can be built atom by atom.”

## The smallest building blocks of modern civilization

MOSFET is a milestone in mankind's ability to control matter at the microscopic level. A voltage signal passes through an insulating layer of glass a few atoms thick, summoning a conductive path underneath. This kind of atomically precise control was unimaginable in the days when Braun poked galena.

But what really makes MOSFET rule the world is not itself, but CMOS, which cleverly pairs PMOS and NMOS.

Before we dive into CMOS in the next section, let’s remember one thing: the “atoms” of modern civilization are not silicon atoms, they are MOSFETs. Just as physicists use atoms to combine the physical world, circuit designers use MOSFETs to combine the digital world. From NAND gates to registers, from adders to CPUs, from CAN controllers to the entire vehicle network, everything starts with this one three-terminal device.

What you see on the debug board as "MCU not working" is essentially that some of the tens to billions of MOSFETs are not connected to the correct voltage at the correct time. A signal integrity problem that you might think is a noise problem is actually a MOSFET gate that is connected to the wrong level due to reflections. Power supply noise may make you think it's an LDO problem, but it's actually millions of MOSFETs flipping simultaneously causing di/dt to be too high, inducing voltage spikes in the parasitic inductance of the package. The timing issue that you might think is a software bug is actually a gate delay on a critical path that is over budget due to process variation in V_TH.

Understanding MOSFET is not just about understanding a device. It’s about understanding the physical rules of the physical world that you deal with every day.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: disassembled the MOSFET, the most basic "atom" of modern civilization, to see how it works.

Key takeaways:
1. MOSFET is a faucet that uses an electric field to control current: the gate and channel are completely insulated and consume almost no current under DC, which is completely different from the "current control" of BJT.
2. The threshold voltage V_TH is a physical result determined by the work function, doping concentration, and oxide layer thickness: the annealing temperature is 25°C lower, the doping activation is insufficient, the V_TH changes, and the timing of the entire chip changes.
3. From planar MOSFET to FinFET to GAA, the essence is to push the transistor from two dimensions to three dimensions: when the channel shrinks to the atomic scale, the drain begins to "steal" the work of the gate. Human beings have responded to the challenges of physical limits with more three-dimensional structures.
