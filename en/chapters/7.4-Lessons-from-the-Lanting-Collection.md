# 7.4 Lessons from the Lanting Collection

## AD 353, Shanyin, Kuaiji

It was the third day of the third month of late spring in the ninth year of Yonghe in the Eastern Jin Dynasty.

Wang Xizhi and 41 friends gathered at the Orchid Pavilion in Shanyin, Kuaiji. That day was the Shangsi Festival, a festival where ancient people bathed in water to get rid of bad luck. They sat along a winding river, placed their wine glasses on the water and let them drift down the current. Whoever stops in front of a wine glass must compose a poem. If you can't do it, you will be fined with three measures of wine.

They wrote a total of 37 poems that day. Wang Xizhi drank wine and became a little tipsy. He looked around. The sky was clear and the wind was gentle. Among the high mountains, dense bamboo forests rustle in the breeze. The water of the stream is clear, reflecting the sky above and the passing white clouds. Surrounded by the sounds of friends chanting poems and talking quietly.

But at such a beautiful moment, Wang Xizhi suddenly felt a deep sadness.

This group of people gathered here today, reciting poems, drinking wine, and lamenting the vastness of the world and the prosperity of all things. But one day, these people will all die. The winding river is still there, the mountains are still there, the clouds in the sky are still there, but the people are gone. Today's joy, in the eyes of future generations, is just a bunch of ancient names and distant past events. There are 41 friends here today. Hundreds of years from now, who will still remember their names? Who still remembers the verses they wrote today?

This sudden surge of sadness, he didn't push it aside. He didn't say, "Forget it, don't think about this anymore, just keep drinking." He let the sadness stay. Then he picked up a pen, using a rat-whisker pen, writing on a piece of silkworm cocoon paper, and wrote down everything that was in his heart at this moment.

This is the "Preface to the Lanting Collection". The pinnacle of Chinese calligraphy art. But today we are not here to talk about calligraphy, we are here to talk about words. Let’s talk about a drunken calligrapher who wrote a monologue in front of a group of friends and a stream on a spring afternoon before 1670 that would silence people thousands of years later.

---

## Those words that make people silent

The most shocking paragraph of the whole article appears in the second half:

> *Every time I look at the reasons for people's interest in the past, if they are combined into one, I will not fail to lament in writing, which cannot be expressed in my heart. I know that death and rebirth are just illusions, and mourning for Qi Peng is an illusion. The future looks upon the present, just as the present looks upon the past. Sad husband! Therefore, I listed people from that time and recorded what they said. Although things are different in different times, I am interested in them and they are consistent. Those who view it later will also feel the elegance. *

Translated into vernacular:

**"Every time I read words written by predecessors, it's like there is a talisman and I understand them completely. I can't help but feel sad at these words, and I can't explain why. I know that it is vain to treat life and death as the same, and it is absurd to treat longevity and short life as the same. Later People look at today just as we look at the past. So I recorded the people present today and their poems. Although times will change and things will change, the root of people’s feelings will still be the same. "**

When you read this, were you quiet for a few seconds?

There are four layers of meaning here, each layer hitting you through time.

The first level: "Every time I look at the reasons for people's interest in the past, it seems to be in harmony." There is a spiritual connection between him and the ancients. He read Ji Kang, Qu Yuan, and the unknown authors in the Book of Songs, and he understood them. Not because he is a genius, but because he is a **living person** who has the same joys and sorrows. He also had the confusion of his seniors. The senior's sigh echoed in his heart. This is the feeling you get when you read a memoir about two bombs and one star. You don’t know Deng Jiaxian, but when you see his record of walking towards unexploded warheads in radiation, your heart beats faster. This is the "if one contract".

The second level: "I firmly know that one death and life is an illusion, and the mourning of Qi Peng is an illusion." He doesn't believe in the saying "life or death is the same, it doesn't matter". Death is real. Loss is real. Being forgotten is real. If everything doesn't matter, why do you calculate the timing margin of CAN messages to 3ns today? Because you care. Because "indifferent" is lazy words, and "important" is the truth.The third level: "The future will look at the present, just as the present looks at the past." This is a mirror image that travels through time and space. Today you look at the ancients and lament their hardships. In the future, when future generations look at you, they will sigh in the same way. We quoted this sentence in the previous chapter. But it also has a deeper meaning: **You and the ancients are on the same chain. ** You and Deng Jiaxian, Wang Ganchang and Guo Yonghuai are not from different eras. You are different runs in the same relay race. His thoughts flow through your mind, and your thoughts flow through the next person's mind. Chains are interlocked, and "ruoheyiyi" is the fastening between the rings.

The fourth level: "Those who look at it later will also feel the elegance." This is the core action of the entire "Lanting Collection Preface". Wang Xizhi didn't just sigh. He **did** one thing. He transformed his insights into **records**. He knew that he would die, but he also knew that if he wrote down his feelings at this time, one day in the future, someone would read these words, and this person would understand him.

One day before 1670, when a drunken calligrapher was writing his last sentence, looking at his wet handwriting, he might have been thinking: **"I don't know who you are. You won't know me. But you will understand me."**

And today, when you read this sentence, Wang Xizhi succeeded with you.

## "List the people of the time and record what they said" - Engineer Lanting

Wang Xizhi didn't just sigh. He did one thing. He listed the 41 friends present that day and copied their poems. He knew that these poems might not be immortal masterpieces at the level of Tang poetry and Song lyrics, but that was not important. What was important was that he recorded them.

"List the people of the time and record what they said." These eight words are the most underestimated engineering actions in "Lanting Collection Preface". Wang Xizhi did not let this party live only in the memories of the participants (memories will be blurred and people will die). He turned it into words that can be passed on, copied, and can span time.

For engineers, what does it mean to “list down the people who lived at the time and record what they said”?

Means **Lessons Learned** written after your project. You documented the wrong assumptions in this design: "We assumed that the CAN bus load would always be below 40%, but during a cold start in winter, a large number of ECUs came online at the same time, causing the load to instantly rush to 85%." You recorded the correction plan: "Delay periodic messages in three batches according to priority by 50ms and send them staggeredly." You recorded the basis for your decision: "Choose to add delays instead of adding nodes, because adding nodes requires hardware changes, and the project has frozen BOM." When you were writing this document, there was no one on the opposite side. But you are talking to yourself a year from now, and you are talking to the next generation of maintenance engineers on this project.

It means the **8D report** you wrote after a production accident. D4 (Root Cause Analysis): You are not writing a document, you are saying to yourself a year later: Don’t reanalyze it again. D6 (Permanent Corrective Action): You are not filling out a form, you are saying to future engineers: I have eliminated this problem, please don’t step on it again. You are not writing a "report", you are writing a "letter". Letters to future colleagues.

Means comments in your code. `// Global interrupts must be disabled here because the ISR modifies shared_flag and the main loop reads it. Misalignment would cause silent data corruption. This bug took us four days to track down.` Whoever takes over this code will be silent for two seconds when they see this line of comments. Then say in your heart: thank you. You won't know his name. He won't know about you either. But that doesn’t matter, all you care about is “he doesn’t want to spend 4 days here like me.”

Meaning you write a **technical blog**. You compiled the complete process of debugging CAN bus Bus-Off, from checking the terminal resistance to using an oscilloscope to view the ACK bit to confirming the register configuration of Bus-off Recovery. You spent a weekend writing it out. You don't know who will read it. But you know there must be another engineer in the world scratching his head late at night with the same problem. Your article will appear in his search results. He'll click on it. He can read. He will be saved.

## The 3am handover log

When I was a yield engineer, I had to write a "passdown log" every day: a handover log. A day shift engineer and a night shift engineer handover at 8:30 p.m. The log you wrote will be read by the night shift engineer, and the log written by the night shift engineer will be read by you the next morning.I found this annoying at first. Why should I spend 30 minutes writing a journal entry? Shouldn't it be over if I just talk to him directly and say a few words? Later, I remember clearly that one Friday afternoon when I was about to get off work, a batch of urgent goods was stuck in the final inspection and required SEM review. I said that the sampling and cutting position is the failed unit of the panel unit (34, 52). The slicing direction is along the Y-axis from electrode pad #12 to the center, cutting to about 2μm above the active layer, and stopping to look at the TEM. I wrote these in the passdown log, drew a diagram of the sampling location on the whiteboard, took photos and posted them in the log, and then got off work.

When I came back on Monday morning, the night shift engineer's diary read: "I cut according to your position, and the defect was found: the pin hole of the gate insulation layer. The TEM magnification is 200k, and the defect diameter is about 45nm. The image has been uploaded to the shared folder. Yield impact assessment: The SEM review results of adjacent units of the same batch of substrates are all clean, and it is confirmed to be a random defect."

When I read this, I had a very strange feeling in my heart. Another engineer relied on the words I left on the paper and the picture on the whiteboard to independently complete an accurate SEM defect location at 3 a.m. He doesn’t need my presence. My thoughts flowed into his brain along with a piece of text, and then his hands followed my fingers to complete the slicing.

At that moment I understood: "Listing people of the time and recording what they said" is not a literary figure of speech. It is the basic protocol for engineering operations. You rely on this agreement to keep your mind from being tied to your body forever.

## Relay chain: from Turing to you, from you to the next

Engineering is not a generational thing. Engineering is a relay race.

Any piece of code, any communication protocol, or any chip you see is not created out of thin air. In front of them is a very long chain. In this chain, each node has left its own "record":

**Turing** wrote a paper in 1936: "On Computable Numbers and Their Application to Decision Problems". 36 pages. Defines the boundaries of "computability". Without this paper, there would be no concept of "computer program". In the paper, he described a "universal machine": a paper tape capable of reading any machine description and performing calculations on it. Every line of C code you write now, after being compiled into ARM machine code, still runs within the abstract framework defined by Turing. Turing's 36-page paper from 1936 is the foundation at the bottom of your stack. This is the first leg of the relay chain.

**Von Neumann** wrote a 101-page report in 1945: "Draft Report on EDVAC." In this draft, he defined an architecture in which programs and data share the same memory: programs no longer need to plug jumpers on the wall or change paper tapes, but are stored in the address space like data. Your S32K144's ARM Cortex-M core, with its instruction fetch → decode → execute → write back pipeline bottom layer, still follows the architectural principles of this 101-page report. He left a record. This is the second leg of the relay chain.

**Shockley, Bardeen, and Bratton** wrote experimental data on point-contact transistors in a laboratory notebook at Bell Labs in 1947. In 1948 they were published publicly. From then on, solid-state electronics was born: the leap from vacuum tubes to transistors allowed computers to shrink from a building to a room. The tens of millions of transistors in your MCU today can be traced back to their most primitive ancestor, which was the gold probe poked into the germanium crystal on the Bell Labs workbench. They left records. This is the third stick.

**Kilby** sketched the first integrated circuit in a Texas Instruments laboratory notebook in 1958: five components integrated on a germanium crystal. In the same year, **Noyce** independently invented the integrated circuit based on silicon planar technology. From now on, all components can be made on the same piece of silicon, and the connections do not require manual welding, but are formed on the silicon chip using photolithography + metal deposition in one step. They left records. This is the fourth stick.**Bosch CAN bus design team** released a white paper on the CAN protocol at the SAE conference in 1986. In their paper, they explained the design reasons for the multi-master priority arbitration mechanism, non-destructive conflict detection, and error frames. Bosch licensed the CAN IP core to Intel, which launched the first CAN controller chips in 1987. Today, every ECU in your car is using this protocol. Its frame structure, its bit timing, and its five error detection mechanisms were all defined by those Bosch engineers in the 1980s. They left records. This is the Nth stick.

**AUTOSAR standard setter**: Hundreds of engineers from different Tier-1 and OEMs spent more than ten years arguing, compromising, and iterating, and finally formed a standard framework that allows software from different manufacturers to run on the same ECU. They left records with hundreds of thousands of pages of technical specifications, ARXML schema, and BSW module configuration parameters.

You read their records, you are inspired, and you design based on them. Then you leave a record of yourself: your Lessons Learned, your notes, your blog, your book. Then the next engineer reads your record, is inspired, and moves on.

**This is the relay chain. ** Every node in the chain did the same thing: record their thinking process and pass it on to the people behind them in words. The chain never breaks unless someone fails to record it.

## "Lanting" in the engineering world

"Preface to the Lanting Collection" has many distant relatives in the engineering world. You may recognize them:

**IETF RFC document. ** From RFC 1 in 1969 (which defined the concept of "host software") to more than 9,000 RFCs today: each one is a record of an engineer or a group's thinking on "how this problem should be solved" at a certain point in time. RFC 793: TCP protocol specification written by Jon Postel in 1981, 85 pages. Basic mechanisms such as three-way handshake and sliding window are defined. Network stacks around the world implement TCP based on this document. Jon Postel died in 1998, but RFC 793 lives on. Underlying every HTTPS request on your phone: TLS over TCP, and TCP is based on RFC 793. This person's thinking has never stopped between you. This is his "Lanting".

Some RFCs have become the cornerstone of the Internet. Some were abandoned and marked "Obsoleted by...". Some are full of humor: RFC 1149: "IP over Avian Carriers" (IP datagrams using carrier pigeons) is an April Fool's Day joke, but it is an RFC and it is recorded. And someone actually implemented it in 2001. After running 9 pings on Linux, the pigeon transmission distance was about 5km and the packet loss rate was 55%. This kind of interaction of "write it and someone will do it" is also the "those who read it later will also feel the elegance."

**Linus Torvalds' first Usenet post. ** On August 25, 1991, a Finnish college student posted this paragraph in the comp.os.minix news group: "Hello everybody out there using minix, I'm doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu)..." This was his true feeling at the time: he felt that he was doing a "hobby." Little did he know that 30 years later, this “hobby” would be running on 96% of servers and 80% of mobile phones in the world. The post remains in the discussion list archives to this day: the timid, honest, unpackaged voice of a 21-year-old. This is his "Lanting".**BSD man pages. ** Behind every Unix command, there is a man page. `man 2 open` tells you all parameters, return values, and error codes of the `open()` system call. You don't know the person who wrote it, but he tells you that `O_CREAT | O_EXCL` can create a file atomically. As you read it, the words this person wrote decades ago are helping you solve a problem today.

**"Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software": Charles Petzold. ** A masterpiece that talks about everything from Morse code to computer architecture in layman's language. Petzold didn't invent any new technology, he just retold existing knowledge in his own way. But he did it so well that engineers twenty years later are still recommending this book to newcomers. What he left behind was not new knowledge, but a new way of understanding. This is also a kind of record: not recording “what”, but recording “how to understand”.

You don't need to write something like an RFC. You don’t need to be like Linus to have that first post immortalized by the internet. You don’t need to write a classic like Petzold.

You can just: write down the pitfalls you stepped on in an internal wiki. Write your debugging ideas in a line of comments. Write your design decisions in a Decision Record. Record your experience as a 15-minute internal sharing video. Write the entire process of troubleshooting a CAN Error Passive problem overnight into a three-page PDF and put it in the team's shared folder.

**Anything you leave behind is a continuation of the work of your predecessors and a gift to the work of future generations. ** You thought you were writing an ordinary document. But in reality, what you are writing is a letter to the future. You don't know the recipient. But the day will definitely come: someone, late at night, facing a certain problem, clicks on the record you left. He will read it. He will be silent for a few seconds. Then he will continue your work because you saved him his most precious thing: time.

## Confusion will never go out of style, code will

You sometimes feel: the code I wrote is not worth recording. There will be stronger engineers writing better code in the future. My design experience is nothing, there are better chips and tools in the future. My knowledge system is not worth mentioning, and my knowledge is constantly being updated.

**But your confusion never goes out of style. **

Tradeoffs under limited resources, time allocation in real-time systems, conflicts between security compliance and practical performance, conflicts between hardware limitations and algorithmic ideals, these are not "bugs of this era." They are an eternal theme in engineering practice. It will happen in the 2020s, it will happen in the 2050s, and it will still happen in the 2200s. Because as long as engineering exists and as long as people use limited tools to solve unlimited needs, confusion and trade-offs will never disappear.

You now write a document that records your strategy for allocating CAN buffers and calibration data areas in 128KB SRAM. Fifty years later, an engineer was faced with a real-time analysis problem under a hybrid architecture of a quantum computing interface and a traditional ECU. You see, the problem is completely different. But his confusion is the same as yours: "There is not enough space and time is too tight. How can I achieve the best with limited resources?"

When he reads your document, he won't be tripped up by the number "128KB". He sees your **trade-off thinking**. What you are facing is the physical SRAM capacity, and what he is facing is the coherence time of the quantum state, but the judgments you make when facing scarce resources: prioritization, boundary condition testing, worst-case analysis, and safety margin retention. These ways of thinking can be transferred across time.

**Confusion's ability to travel through time is stronger than code. Confusion is the universal language of engineering. **Think about it the other way around: Anything you write down at work today, do you have to face the same mentality? "What's good about my broken code?" "My bug troubleshooting process is so low that I'm embarrassed to write it down." But people fifty years from now won't care whether your code is elegant or not. What they care about is: how you think in your time, with your tools, and in the face of your confusion. Your weighings, your attempts, your judgments based on limited information, these things travel through time. By today's standards, the mathematical notations in Turing's paper seem primitive by today's standards, but the concept of "computability" has traveled 90 years. It's not that he wrote beautifully, but that he recorded his true thoughts. the same as you.

## You are not just writing code alone

When you write code, you don't write it alone.

You stand on the abstraction of **Turing**, who gave you the concept of "computability". You stand on the architecture of **Von Neumann**, who defined that programs and data share the same memory. You are standing on Shockley's PN junction. Without his junction transistor, you would not even have the silicon foundation of this MCU. You stand on the shoulders of the **Bosch CAN bus design team** who defined the multi-master priority arbitration protocol in 1986, so you don't have to design the in-car communication protocol yourself. You stand on the consensus of **AUTOSAR standard setters**, and you directly use the template that hundreds of engineers have been arguing for more than ten years to finalize. You stand on the debugging notes of your predecessors and mentors, and the pitfalls they stepped on saved you countless hours.

**All the people who have influenced this field in history are behind you. **

When you join this relay, when you record, when you write documents, when you write books, when you record video tutorials, when you reply on Stack Overflow, you change from a "user" to a "contributor". Your name may disappear, but the record you leave behind will become a light that will be lit by future engineers when they explore problems.

**The words you leave will last longer than your ECU. The books you write, the notes you write, and the wiki you write will continue to help others after you are gone. **

This is what "immortality" means in engineering. It is not that the body is immortal, but that thoughts spread along the text and are reborn in other people's brains.

Wang Xizhi wrote before 1670 that "those who read it later will also be impressed by the graceful writing." He did, and as you read this today, you were struck. Then the same goes for you, you can also leave your "Lanting". Those who view it later will also feel the elegance.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: **From Wang Xizhi's "Preface to the Lanting Collection", we read the deepest destiny and highest glory of engineers. **

Key takeaways:
1. **"The future looks at the present, just as the present looks at the past"**. You and Deng Jiaxian, von Neumann, and Turing are not people of different eras, but different bats in the same relay race.
2. **"Listing the people of the time and recording what they said" is the most underestimated engineering move in the engineering world**. Your Lessons Learned, your notes, your handover log, every record is a letter to the future.
3. **Confusion never goes out of style, code does. But your trade-off thinking can be passed across time**: What you leave behind is not perfect code, but a thinking process for making optimal decisions under limited resources.

The book has reached its final stop. From sand to chips, from chips to ruts, from ruts to you.
