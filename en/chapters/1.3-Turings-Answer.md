# 1.3 Turing's Answer

## The man running through Cambridge

1935, King's College, Cambridge. A 23-year-old graduate student was lying on the grass, looking at the sky, thinking about one thing:

**What is "computing"? **

His name is Alan Turing.

There are many labels attached to this young man: genius, weirdo, marathon runner. You won’t see the label “social butterfly” in Turing’s biography. He sometimes wears a gas mask while working in the office, not to protect against viruses but to protect against pollen allergies. He would chain his teacup to the radiator to prevent others from stealing it. He stuttered, but that didn't stop him from thinking about mathematical problems very quickly. He was an extremely fast runner, running 2 hours and 46 minutes at the 1948 London Olympic Marathon Trials, just 11 minutes slower than the winner. When he was training, he would sometimes run all the way from Cambridge to London, about 80 kilometers, just to catch a meeting.

But Turing's most astonishing run was not physical, but mental.

At that time, the mathematical world had just been shocked by Gödel: we knew that some propositions were not provable. But how to judge whether a proposition is provable or unprovable? The Determination Problem (Entscheidungsproblem) proposed by Hilbert asks: Is there a set of mechanical steps that can automatically determine whether any mathematical proposition is true?

To answer this question, you first need to define what a "mechanical step" is.

"Calculate step by step", this sentence is said but not said. You have to give an **accurate mathematical model**. It cannot be vague, cannot use natural language, and cannot use "intuitively obvious".

Turing was lying on the grass, and in his mind he imagined a worker sitting at a table, with a piece of paper divided into squares in front of him, and a table with rules on his hand. He looked down at the symbols on the current grid, glanced at the rules table, then rewritten the symbols on the paper tape, moved the paper tape one space to the left or right, looked up at the symbols on the new grid, and so on. No inspiration, no intuition, no “creative thinking”. Follow every step strictly according to the rules on the table.

This is the prototype of the **Turing machine**.

## A paper tape, a status table

The design of the Turing machine is extremely simple, with only three components:

**Paper tape**: An infinitely long tape, divided into squares. Each square can write a symbol (such as 0 or 1, or it can be blank `_`). The tape can move one space to the left or right. Infinite length is a theoretical assumption. In the physical world, the paper tape certainly has a length. But Turing asked: **If resources are unlimited, where is the boundary of computing? ** First ask about the ultimate boundary, and then see where physical constraints reduce it.

**Read-write head**: Currently facing a certain grid on the paper tape, you can read the symbols in this grid, or you can erase or write a new symbol. Then according to the rules, decide to move the paper tape one space to the left (L) or one space to the right (R).

**Finite State Controller**: There is a finite number of states inside the machine: S1, S2, S3, ... HALT. In each state, depending on the symbol it reads, the machine does three things: writes a new symbol (perhaps the same as the original), moves the tape, and switches to another state (or shuts down).

All possible behaviors can be written in a **state transition table**:

```
Current state | Read symbol | Write symbol | Movement direction | Next state
  S1     |    0     |     1    |    R     |   S2
  S1     |    1     |     0    |    L     |   S3
  S2     |    _     |     _    |    N     |  HALT
```

This table is the **program**. A paper tape, a read-write head, and a state transition table are all the parts of the entire machine. No RAM, no ALU, no cache, no pipeline, no instruction set, just these three.

Looks ridiculously simple? yes. **But Turing's astonishing conclusion is: If a problem has a "mechanical solution", there is a Turing machine that can do it. **

## Turing Machine Practice: Calculating the Addition of Two Numbers

Let's "run" this machine and intuitively feel how it "calculates".

Suppose there are two numbers stored on the paper tape, represented by a series of consecutive 1's. For example, "3" is "111", and "2" is "11". The two numbers are separated by a space `_`. In its initial state, the tape looks like this:

```
... _ _ 1 1 1 _ 1 1 _ _ ...
         ^
      read/write head
```

We want the Turing machine to combine these two numbers into one, that is, 111_11 becomes 11111, achieving 3 + 2 = 5.The state transition table is as follows:

```
Status | Read | Write | Move | Next Status | Description
S1 | 1 | 1 | R | S1 | Swipe right, keep 1 when encountering 1
S1 | _ | 1 | R | S2 | If you encounter a space, fill in 1
S2 | 1 | 1 | R | S2 | Continue to sweep the second number to the right
S2 | _ | _ | L | S3 | When encountering the second space, go back one space
S3 | 1 | _ | N | HALT | Erase the last 1 and stop
```

Execution process (initial tape: `111_11`, read-write head is on the far left):

```
Step 1: S1, read 1 → write 1, shift right, still S1 tape: 111_11
Step 2: S1, read 1 → write 1, shift right, still S1 tape: 111_11
Step 3: S1, read 1 → write 1, shift right, still S1 tape: 111_11
Step 4: S1, read _ → write 1, move right, enter S2 tape: 111111 (space becomes 1)
Step 5: S2, read 1 → write 1, shift right, still S2 tape: 111111
Step 6: S2, read 1 → write 1, shift right, still S2 tape: 111111
Step 7: S2, read _ → write _, shift left, enter S3 tape: 111111
Step 8: S3, Read 1 → Write _, Unmoved, HALT Tape: 11111_
```

**Note**: In step 4, fill the space between the two numbers with 1, and the two numbers will be merged into a string of 1; in step 7, the read-write head retreats to the last 1, and in step 8, erase it. Finally, there are 5 1s left on the tape, which is exactly 3 + 2 = 5. This state machine only handles two non-empty unary numbers; additional states are needed to handle 0 or other edge cases. The focus of the above example is to **feel** the process: each step is doing the same thing: "read symbols → write symbols → move paper tape → change state". Eight steps and the calculation is completed.

The addition just now, written in assembly, is `ADD R0, R0, R1`, one instruction, one clock cycle. But the Turing machine took 8 steps. The individual steps of a Turing machine are much more primitive than CPU instructions, they are "atoms of computation". You can think of it as the "quark" of computing, not because it's fast, but because it's indivisible. Any more complex calculation operations: ADD, MUL, conditional jumps, function calls, can be decomposed into such atomic operations.

## Use code to simulate a Turing machine

Let's go one step further and write a Universal Turing Machine Interpreter in Python. You only need to define the paper tape, state transition table, and initial state, and it can simulate the execution of any Turing machine:

```python
# Universal Turing Machine Interpreter
def turing_machine(tape, transition_table, initial_state, initial_pos=0):
    state = initial_state
    pos = initial_pos
    steps = 0
    max_steps = 1000 # Prevent true infinite loop

    while state != "HALT" and steps < max_steps:
        # Read the current location symbol
        symbol = tape[pos] if pos < len(tape) else "_"

        # Lookup table: In the current state and current symbol, which new state should be transferred to?
        key = (state, symbol)
        if key not in transition_table:
            print(f"Undefined transition: state={state}, symbol={symbol}")
            break

        new_symbol, direction, new_state = transition_table[key]

        # write
        if pos < len(tape):
            tape[pos] = new_symbol
        else:
            tape.append(new_symbol)

        # move
        if direction == "R":
            pos += 1
        elif direction == "L":
            pos = max(0, pos - 1)
        # direction == "N" does not move

        # Switch status
        state = new_state
        steps += 1

    return tape, state, steps

# Example: Binary NOT operation (turn 1100 into 0011)
tape = ["1", "1", "0", "0"]
table = {
    ("S1", "1"): ("0", "R", "S1"),
    ("S1", "0"): ("1", "R", "S1"),
    ("S1", "_"): ("_", "N", "HALT"),
}
result, final_state, steps = turing_machine(tape, table, "S1")
print(f"Result: {''.join(result)}")  # Result: 0011_
```

This interpreter is just over 40 lines long, but it can perform **any** problem computable by a Turing machine. You give it different `transition_table`, and it does different calculations. **Interpreter = fixed hardware, transition_table = replaceable software. **What does it look like? Like the operating system loading different ELF files to perform different tasks, like the CPU taking different machine codes to perform different operations.

This 40+ line Python script is the same thing as the ARM Cortex-M4F core of your S32K144: the physical incarnation of a universal Turing machine.

## Universal Turing Machine: One set of hardware rules them all

Turing's first paper not only defined an ordinary Turing machine, he went a step further and defined a special type of machine: Universal Turing Machine (UTM).

The paper tape of an ordinary Turing machine contains only data. The universal Turing machine has both data and programs on the paper tape. The program is the encoding of the state transition table of a "target Turing machine". The universal Turing machine reads the program on the paper tape and simulates the entire behavior of the target Turing machine.

what does that mean?

**A fixed machine has a fixed state transition table (only a few dozen states are needed) and can perform any calculation. ** You don't need to build an adding machine for addition, a multiplication machine for multiplication, and a sorting machine for sorting. You only need to put the code for the "adding state table" on the paper tape, the universal Turing machine reads it, simulates it, and then your machine becomes an adding machine. You change another piece of paper tape and put a "multiplication state table" on it, and the same machine becomes a multiplication machine again.

**Programs and data coexist on the paper tape. ** When you change the content on the tape, you change the entire world. This is the true birth of the concept of "software".

You go back and look at your ECU. The same NXP S32K144, burned with different firmware (Firmware), plays a completely different role: burning gateway firmware → CAN message routing engine. Burn BCM firmware → Body control module (lights, door locks, wipers). Flashing ABS firmware → anti-lock brake controller.

**The same set of ARM Cortex-M4F physical core, the same set of CAN peripherals, and the same set of SRAM address space. ** The only difference lies in the tens of KB of machine code content burned in Flash. Just like Turing's paper tape, if you change the paper tape, the machine will change its "soul".

**This is the essence of "universal computing": hardware is the body and software is the soul. Changing the soul does not change the body. **

## Church-Turing thesis: an unproven but conclusive consensusWhile Turing was deriving the Turing machine model in Cambridge, across the ocean in Princeton, another logician, Alonzo Church, was also defining "computation" in another way.

Church invented Lambda Calculus. It is not as physical as a Turing machine (paper tape + read-write head), but extremely mathematical: all calculations are the definition and application of functions.

The lambda calculus has only three rules:
1. **Variable**: x is a λ term.
2. **Abstract**: (λx.M) is a λ term that defines a function with parameter x and function body M.
3. **Application**: (M N) is a λ term that applies function M to parameter N.

It's that simple. There is no paper tape, no status table, no read/write head. There are only **three rules**, but it is also **Turing complete**. Any function that can be computed by a Turing machine can also be computed by the lambda calculus.

Although Church's lambda calculus seems "ungrounded", it directly affects modern programming languages. The JavaScript `(x) => x + 1` you wrote, Python's `lambda x: x + 1`, C++'s `[](int x) { return x + 1; }`, and Haskell's `\x -> x + 1` are all direct descendants of Church's lambda calculus. The **λ arrow never disappeared, it just flew from a math paper into the syntax highlighting of a programming editor. **

Turing went to Princeton to pursue a PhD from 1936-1938 (under the guidance of Alonzo Church). Church became his mentor. Turing proved that: **The Turing machine model and the lambda calculus model have equivalent computing capabilities. ** A function is Turing computable if and only if it is λ definable.

This equivalence leads to a more profound proposition: **Church-Turing Thesis**:

> **Any physically computable process can be simulated by a Turing machine. **

The thesis has not been "mathematically proven" because it connects the physical world and the mathematical world, and such transcendent propositions cannot be established by pure mathematical means. But in nearly a century, no one has come up with a counterexample. Quantum computing, DNA computing, neuromorphic computing, they can calculate faster, but the range of calculations does not exceed the boundaries of the Turing machine.

**The Church-Turing thesis is not a theorem, it is a silent consensus of human civilization for nearly a century. ** Every line of code you step on, every CPU instruction you use, and every ELF file you compile is a "yes" vote for this topic in the form of a physical carrier.

## Turing at Bletchley Park: The Collision of Theory and World

Turing was more than a theorist. After the outbreak of World War II, he was called into the British Government Code School and sent to Bletchley Park, the center of British World War II code breaking.

The German army used an electromechanical cipher machine called the Enigma to encrypt their military communications. The Enigma has three wheels and a power strip. Every time a key is pressed, the internal current path changes, so that the same letter will be encrypted into different letters in two key presses. The theoretically possible key space is on the order of 10²⁰, making manual brute force cracking impossible.

Turing designed an electromechanical device called the Bombe in 1940. It is not a general-purpose computer, it is a machine specially designed to crack the Enigma code, but it uses relay logic to implement the algorithm of "automatic search for possible keys". Bombe exploited a key weakness observed by Turing: the Germans sent an encrypted telegram containing the word "WETTER" every morning, which provided the conditions for a known-plaintext attack. Bombe simulates multiple Enigma machines running in parallel, checking every possible wheel setting and filtering out those that can match "possible plaintext" with "intercepted ciphertext".

By the end of the war, Bletchley Park was decoding an average of 3,000 enemy messages a day. Historians estimate that **Turing's Bombe and subsequent cracking efforts shortened World War II by at least 2 years and saved approximately 14 million lives. **But when Turing was alive, all these were state secrets and he could not tell them. His mother only knew him as "doing a little paperwork for the government in town."

Turing's colleague Tommy Flowers built Colossus in 1944, the world's first programmable electronic computer. Colosas was built with 2,400 vacuum tubes and was designed to crack the German military's highest-level "Lorentz" code. Colossus was not a universal Turing machine, it was pre-programmed with a plug patch panel, but it proved that **electronic logic circuits could execute complex algorithms at high speed**. Processes 5000 characters per second.

After the war, the British dismantled all Colossus machines into parts and burned all the drawings, keeping them as top secrets. Those involved in the project were ordered never to mention it. The existence of Colossus was not made public until the 1970s. Turing and Flowers' work at Bletchley Park may be the most influential secret computing project in human history.

## Paper Tapes Go Mold: A Semiconductor Engineer’s Interlude

The paper tape of the Turing machine is an "ideal paper tape": infinitely long, not worn, not faded, not moldy. But real-world storage media is not like this.

In the field of semiconductor manufacturing, SRAM is the most sensitive part of chip yield. SRAM has the highest density. On a nominal 512KB Flash MCU, SRAM cells may occupy 70% of the number of transistors. During online electron beam inspection, it can be seen that the threshold voltage of some SRAM bits has a slight deviation. When the deviation reaches a certain extent, a certain operating voltage point is flipped. This is **Vt mismatch**, caused by random fluctuations of dopant atoms in deep submicron processes: the transistors in the cross-coupled inverter pair of the 6T SRAM cell are not matched enough, and the steady-state point is biased to one side. As soon as read/write noise interferes, the value changes.

**Turing's paper tape is perfect. Your silicone tape is not. **

In the space environment, high-energy particles (cosmic rays) pass through the chip and directly knock over a certain SRAM unit. This is called **SEU** (Single Event Upset). On the ground, alpha particles decaying from trace amounts of uranium/thorium isotopes in the encapsulating resin can also knock over SRAMs. ISO 26262 for automotive electronics requires that ASIL D level ECUs must implement ECC (Error Correcting Code) or lockstep dual-core comparison. In essence, it adds an error correction mechanism to the "paper tape" of the Turing machine.

**Ideal computing models ignore the imperfections of the physical world, and engineers spend their lives compensating for these imperfections. **

## The C code you write is a state transition table

Let's move from Bletchley Park and the production line back to your workplace.

You write a line of embedded C:

```c
int sum = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < 10; i++) {
    sum += i;
}
```

What is the Turing machine equivalent of this code?

- **Tape** = Memory (RAM + Flash). Symbol on tape = value at memory address.
- **Read/Write Head** = Load/Store unit of the CPU. `ldr` is for reading, `str` is for writing.
- **STATE TABLE** = sequence of instructions. Current state = address pointed to by the program counter (PC). Next instruction = PC + 2 or + 4 (Thumb/Thumb-2 instructions vary in length).

The compiler translates this into ARM assembly:

```asm
    mov r0, #0          ; sum = 0
    mov r1, #0          ; i = 0
loop:
    cmp r1, #10         ; Compare i and 10
    bge end             ; If i >= 10, jump out
    add r0, r0, r1      ; sum += i
    add r1, r1, #1      ; i++
    b loop              ; Go back to the beginning of the loop
end:
```

You see, this is a state transition table, just written out using assembly mnemonics. `mov`, `cmp`, `add`, `b`, these four mnemonics are the "high-speed syntax" of the state transition table. What the compiler does is to translate your C code: `if`, `for`, `while` into the state transition table of the Turing machine.

**No exceptions. Every line of code, every bug, and every performance optimization you have written is a fine-tuning on this finite state table. **

## The shock of simplicityThe most shocking thing about the Turing machine is not its power, but its simplicity.

An infinitely long paper tape + a finite state table, gone.

Complex things can be reduced to simple rules. This is a profoundly philosophical and aesthetic insight.

The car you drive may have more than 100 ECUs and tens of millions of lines of code. Thousands of frames of messages are running on the CAN bus every second. The MOST bus carries high-fidelity audio. DoIP diagnostic requests are running on the Ethernet. The time synchronization protocol adjusts the clocks of each node behind the scenes.

If you remove it to the bottom layer: it is all paper tape, read-write head, and status table. 

**The essence is not complicated, complexity is the unfolding of the essence. **

The same goes for building cars. A car has tens of thousands of parts. Materials include steel, aluminum, carbon fiber, rubber, and ceramics. The processes include stamping, welding, painting and final assembly. Suppliers are located around the world. But if you break it down to the lowest level: it's all about atomic arrangement, physical laws, and engineering labor. 

Humans have used atoms to create steel, used steel to create car bodies, used silicon to create chips, and used chips to control the car body. Progression layer by layer is not magic, but the accumulation of scientific discoveries and engineering iterations over hundreds of years.

**The moment you stand on the paper tape, you are already standing at the starting point of human computing civilization. **
