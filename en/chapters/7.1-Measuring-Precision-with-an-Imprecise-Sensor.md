# 7.1 Thought Experiment: Measuring Precision with an Imprecise Sensor

## A group of people on the Gobi Desert

1964, Gobi, northwest China.

A group of engineers gathered around a sensor, frowning. Mission: To measure the precise yield of a nuclear explosion, which requires an accuracy of a few thousandths.

Here’s the thing: the best sensor you have is only 1% accurate.

How can a 1% sensor measure a physical quantity with an accuracy of one thousandth?

You may think this is impossible. A ruler with an accuracy of 1% cannot measure the length with an accuracy of one thousandth. This is elementary school mathematics. But in engineering, the conclusion of the math book is just the starting point. The book says "impossible" because it assumes that you only use the sensor to read the number once and that's it. But what if you read it ten thousand times? What if you used five different measurement methods at the same time? What if you mobilize all physical equations, statistical laws, and circuit topology to turn the entire known physical world into your "second sensor"?

Let’s first clarify what the actual measurement challenges were in 1964. Accurate measurement of the yield of a nuclear explosion is not about inserting a pressure gauge into a mushroom cloud. You need to measure at least three physical quantities at the same time: **The propagation speed of the shock wave** (the change of the front position of the smoke cloud with time through a high-speed camera), **The expansion rate of the fireball** (inversely inferred from the time curve of the radiation brightness), and **The seismic wave travel time near the explosion center**. Each of these three quantities is collected using a sensor: shock waves use an array of pressure sensors placed in the air, fireballs use photocells to record visible and infrared radiation intensity, and seismic waves use surface accelerometers. Each sensor has a nominal accuracy of around 1%. To make matters worse, the Gobi Desert environment: a temperature span of minus twenty degrees to forty degrees above zero, mechanical vibrations caused by sandstorms, and aerosol scattering on the optical path make the actual working accuracy of the sensor far lower than the 1% factory specification.

But in the end, the equivalent is to use them to deduce the results to an accuracy of one thousandth. Because if you misestimate by 0.5%, for the entire weapon design, this error means that you have no idea how much charge to fill in the next experiment and to what point the critical mass should be adjusted.

You might be thinking: find a better sensor. But under the conditions at the time (Cold War blockade, technology embargo, and nascent domestic industrial base), “better sensors” were not an option. This option does not exist.

The answer is not hardware, the answer is methodology.

When you're pushed into this corner, you'll discover something amazing: the sensor is just a "translator" between the physical world and the digital world. If the translator is not good enough, you can use mathematics to recalibrate it, use physical models to supplement it, and use statistical laws to refine it. When hardware reaches its end, math and physics become your new sensors. This is not the patent of the group of people on the Gobi Desert in 1964. This is a secret that every generation of engineers will discover in desperate situations.

When I was doing yield analysis at a panel factory, I had to face the physical limitations of inspection tools every day. There are thousands of panel units on a glass substrate, each with millions of TFTs, and the tools used to detect defects have limited accuracy. AOI (Automated Optical Inspection) can see the particles, but not the 50nm pinhole. Electrical testing can measure TFT parameters, but the physical form cannot be seen. I can see dead pixels in the light test, but I don't know the cause.

The physical limitations of detection tools have forced another method: **Multiple sets of data fusion**. Superimpose the three sets of data from the AOI, electrical test, and lighting test: a pinhole missed by the AOI will appear as a high leakage current in the electrical test, and as a certain pixel not lighting up in the lighting test. Three sets of rough eyes observe from three directions, and the fused positioning is more precise than any single detection.

## Method 1: When you have time, you have precision

Random noise (thermal noise, shot noise) has a crucial mathematical property: it has zero mean. This means that if you sample the same physical quantity multiple times and average it, the noise will cancel each other out.

```
σ_n = σ / √n
```

The rate of decline of the standard deviation is the reciprocal of the square root of the number of samples. Measure 100 times and the accuracy improves by an order of magnitude. Measure 10,000 times and improve accuracy by two orders of magnitude.This formula is elegant. But its premise is also very demanding: the noise must be zero-mean and uncorrelated. For most physical sensors, these two premises hold approximately, but not globally. You need to know the noise characteristics of your sensor and know under what conditions it meets "zero mean, uncorrelated" and under what conditions it does not. This requires a deep understanding of the physical world, not just mathematical formulas.

Give a specific example. For a 12-bit ADC, the theoretical RMS of quantization noise is LSB/√12 ≈ 0.289 LSB. If you sample the same DC signal 100 times and average it (assuming the noise superimposed on the signal is white noise with an amplitude of at least greater than 1 LSB), the effective resolution increases from 12 bits to about 15 bits. About 3 extra bits of precision, no hardware modifications at all. Just take 100 more samples, do an accumulation and shift. This is **oversampling + decimation** (oversampling and decimation). You didn’t spend any money, you didn’t change the chip, you didn’t change the PCB layout. You just increased the sampling frequency from 1kHz to 100kHz, and then traded time for accuracy.

But note that premise: "Noise is white noise and has an amplitude greater than 1 LSB." If your signal is clean without any noise, such as if you are measuring a constant voltage output from a precision voltage reference, oversampling will not increase the effective number of bits. Because each conversion of the ADC falls within the same quantization interval, you will still get the same value after averaging 100 times. You need noise to "jitter" the signal between two LSBs. This is the technique of dithering. Deliberately superimpose a pseudo-random noise of known amplitude at the input end, let the signal jump repeatedly across the quantization boundary, and then oversample it to flatten it out. It sounds contradictory (adding noise to reduce noise), but the math proves it works.

Engineers in the Gobi Desert didn't have ADCs, let alone oversampling. What did they use? The oscilloscope takes pictures, records the waveforms one by one, and then manually reads and calculates the average value. Ten thousand samples are not ten thousand lines of Python scripts, but ten thousand negatives, ten thousand records, and ten thousand calculations. During the exposure, development, and reading of each negative, if you make any mistake in any step, that negative will be discarded and you have to start over. Ten thousand times, not metaphorically, exactly ten thousand times.

Twelve-bit ADC, one hundred times oversampling, pushing the accuracy to fifteen bits. This isn't a feature, it's the end of a long chain. The starting point of the chain is the darkroom in the Gobi Desert, the handwritten readings, the ten thousand repetitions that will never be mentioned again. For every oversampling, there is a layer of forgotten negatives.

## Method 2: "Calibrate" with known quantities - Calibrated transfer chain

If you have a 1kg standard weight, place it on the sensor, record the output, and use this deviation to correct all subsequent measurements. This is **Cross Calibration**.

Essentially, you are using an absolutely accurate reference quantity to draw a "bias map" for an imprecise sensor.

The **sensor calibration** (sensor calibration) you do on the ECU has exactly the same logic as this. When the steering wheel angle sensor passes EOL (off-line inspection), the production line worker presses full left + full right, and the ECU captures the positions of both ends. These two known endpoints are your "standard weights." Then you do interpolation in the middle to compensate for the nonlinearity.

But there’s more to calibration than this. There is a more subtle method: **calibration transfer**. You don't have a direct standard weight, but you do have a transfer standard that can be calibrated by the standard weight. For example, you do not have an accurate pressure source to calibrate the MAP (intake manifold absolute pressure) sensor, but you have a reference barometer on the production line that has been calibrated by a metrology institute. You use it to first calibrate a batch of "gold sample" MAP sensors, and then use these gold samples to calibrate the MAP sensor of each car on the production line. The accuracy will decay step by step, but as long as the attenuation factor at each level is controllable, the final accuracy is still much higher than the original sensor without calibration.

This is a calibration chain. In essence, absolute accuracy is "transported" outward from a known reference point and transferred to the final product like a relay. The entire quality system of the automotive industry. From national standards to metrology institutes to production line tooling to EOL workstations, it is a huge calibration transfer network.Another example is the knock sensor: the "normal vibration" background value of each cylinder at different temperatures is also a reference. The DSP is not detecting "if there is vibration" but "if the vibration deviates significantly from the normal background". This is a variation on reference calibration: your reference is not absolute zero, but the "expected normal value" given the laws of statistics.

When I was doing yield analysis at the panel factory, I felt particularly deeply about this set of products. Every optical inspection machine: KLA, AMAT, Hitachi, has been calibrated by the original factory using standard defective substrates before leaving the factory. Artificial defects of known dimensions are precisely engraved on the calibration sheet using a focused ion beam (FIB). However, after the machine was moved to the production line, the temperature changed by a few degrees, dust accumulated on the optical platform for several months, and the calibration began to drift. We use the daily monitor that comes with the machine to run the automatic calibration procedure, and then use the bare substrate for particle count verification. Every production line engineer knows: the "defect size 0.35μm" you see displayed on the machine today may be 0.30 or 0.40. But by calibrating the transfer chain, you control the uncertainty within a known range. You don't know what the true value is, but you know the error is within ±0.05μm, and that's enough.

## Method 3: Let the common mode disappear by itself - the wisdom of differential measurement

Sometimes the problem is not insufficient accuracy, but that the signal is drowned.

Your sensor outputs a microvolt-level signal, but the electromagnetic interference in the surrounding environment is at the millivolt level, and the signal is completely covered by the noise. At this time, you should not be "hard" with the noise. You should let the noise disappear on its own.

This is the core wisdom of **differential measurement** and **bridge circuit**.

The Wheatstone Bridge is one of the most elegant inventions in the history of engineering. The four resistor arms form a balanced circuit: when the bridge arms are balanced, the output voltage is zero. Any slight imbalance, such as a strain gauge being stretched by 0.01%, will produce a weak differential voltage at the output. However, all four arms change their resistance simultaneously due to changes in external temperature, and this common mode change is automatically offset by the bridge.

At least dozens of sensors in your car use this principle. Inlet pressure sensor (MAP): A silicon micromachined diaphragm with four varistors forming a Wheatstone bridge. The pressure causes the diaphragm to bend, two resistors are compressed, two are stretched, the bridge is unbalanced, and a differential voltage is output. Temperature changes? The four resistors drift together and the common mode cancels. This is at least one order of magnitude more accurate than using a single varistor plus a temperature compensation meter.

The same logic applies to the differential CAN bus. CAN_H and CAN_L are two lines running in parallel, and external electromagnetic interference induces almost identical common mode noise on the two lines. The differential comparator at the receiving end only looks at the difference between CAN_H - CAN_L, and the common mode noise completely disappears in the subtraction. This is why the CAN bus can still communicate reliably in the electromagnetic hell of a car: it's not that you shield it well, it's that the differential allows the noise to hit itself.

From Wheatstone bridges to differential signals, from instrumentation amplifiers to fully differential ADCs, the cornerstone idea of ​​the entire field of precision measurement is only four words: **Let the common mode cancel**. There is no need to eliminate the noise, just make the noise appear exactly the same on both channels and then do the subtraction. The physical world does the hardest steps for you.

## Method 4: Let the physical model help you "guess" - the intuition of Kalman filtering

Sometimes the problem isn't lack of accuracy, it's that you simply can't directly measure the value you want.

Battery SOC (state of charge) is a typical example. You can't take a "SOC sensor" and just plug it in and read how much power is left. SOC is the system state, not a physical quantity measurable by the sensor.

The method of BMS is: **Current integration (ampere-hour method)** accumulates the amount of charge and discharge to give a continuous estimate of SOC; at the same time, it periodically uses the **OCV-SOC curve** (open circuit voltage → power mapping) to make corrections. Two coarse measurements (integrated error of the current sensor and noise of the voltage sensor), fused by a physical model of the system, give an accurate SOC estimate.

This method has a name: **Kalman filter**.

Let me explain in simple language what the Kalman filter is doing, just three words: **Predict → Measure → Update**.You're standing in a dark hallway. You want to know your place. Step 1: You predict where you are now based on the position of your previous step and your step length: "I was at the door last second and took three steps, one step is about 0.7 meters, so I should be about 2.1 meters inward from the door now." But this prediction is uncertain because your step length is not accurate. Step 2: You reach out and touch a door on the wall. You know that the door is exactly 2.5 meters away from the door. This is your **measurement**. But measurement is also imprecise because you don't know if your hand is touching it wrongly. Step 3: You fuse the predictions and measurements based on their **relative confidence**: if your step estimate is accurate but the measurement is rough (for example, you only vaguely touched the door frame), you trust the prediction more; if the measurement is very certain (for example, your palm is completely pressed against the doorknob), you trust the measurement more. In the end, you get "My position is about 2.3 meters", which is closer to reality than if you use pure prediction (2.1 meters) or pure measurement (2.5 meters).

This is Kalman filtering. Predictions come from your modeling of the physical world, and measurements come from sensors. The weight of the fusion comes from statistics: whichever is smaller, the uncertainty of the prediction or the noise of the measurement, has a greater weight. The final output estimate is more accurate than any single sensor.

```
Vehicle speed estimate = wheel speed sensor + IMU (inertial measurement unit) acceleration + GPS speed + vehicle dynamics model
```

Each item alone is not accurate enough, but after fusion, it is accurate enough to be used for ESP slip rate calculation.

There is a more subtle example: **inter-tooth interpolation of wheel speed sensor**. A 48-tooth magnetic tonewheel (48 pulses per wheel revolution) has a tooth period of approximately 1.5 milliseconds at 100km/h. But the control period of ESP is 1 millisecond (ESP needs to know the current instantaneous wheel speed before the next tooth arrives). what to do? Not "wait for the next tooth." It uses the average speed measured between the two teeth last time, plus the "current speed prediction value" calculated based on the last acceleration between teeth, and extrapolates within the 1.5ms of the pulse gap. When the next pulse actually comes, use the measured values ​​to correct the model and recalibrate the acceleration parameters. This is the Kalman filter of the wheel speed sensor. Although it is one-dimensional and does not require a matrix, its soul is exactly the same: prediction + correction.

Furthermore, how accurate can interdental interpolation be? Not only can you measure the time between teeth, you can also use the continuous waveform of the magnetic field change as the magnetic wheel rotates, simulating that the magnetic field continuously passes through the sensing element between the rising and falling edges of each tooth. You can scale from 48 teeth to the equivalent of thousands of virtual teeth. A circle of 48 teeth, accuracy 360°/48 = 7.5°. Together with continuous waveform fitting and Kalman trajectory estimation, the accuracy can be pushed to 0.01°. From 7.5° to 0.01°, it is not a hardware upgrade, but a combination of mathematics and physics.

## Method 5: Let the laws of physics do the subtraction for you - statistical superposition of multiple combustion cycles

Engine knock detection is another perfect example. A piezoelectric sensor (essentially a piece of impact ceramic) is attached to the cylinder. Not only do you capture the characteristic pressure oscillations caused by detonation, you also capture the mechanical vibration of the piston hitting the cylinder wall (about 500Hz-2kHz), the impact of valve seating, the rattling sound of the timing chain, and the low-frequency vibration of the road surface. The knock characteristic signal is in the frequency band 5-15 kHz, but its amplitude, in the most severe case, is only about 5% of the maximum pressure in the combustion chamber. At mild knocks, this may be as low as 1%. The output signal-to-noise ratio of the sensor is often negative in all operating conditions. Noise is louder than signal.

Traditional approach: Set a voltage threshold. If it exceeds the threshold, it will cause knocking, but it is not available at all under all engine operating conditions. The background vibration amplitude at idle is two orders of magnitude lower than at full load at 6000RPM. A fixed threshold either misses detection at low speeds or misjudges at high speeds.The actual practice of the ECU profoundly reflects the synthesis of all the previous methods: First, **bandpass filtering** cuts off all vibration components outside 5-15kHz in the time domain. This is to make frequency components other than the common mode disappear. Second, **statistical threshold detection** does not require a fixed threshold, but allows the ECU to self-learn the mean and standard deviation of each cylinder's "normal combustion noise" under steady-state operating conditions (such as idle speed and cruise control). Define threshold = μ + kσ, where k is a calibrated value weighed by the OEM based on a combination of engine durability and emissions. Third, **multi-cycle superposition**, you continuously observe the knock window for N combustion cycles. The signal in one cycle may be overwhelmed by random noise, but you align the knock signals of N cycles to the same position of the crankshaft angle and do a superimposed average. The noise is attenuated by 1/√N because it is random, and the knock signal remains unchanged because it is deterministic. After 100 cycles of superposition, the SNR improved by a factor of 10.

20dB SNR gain does not rely on better sensors, does not rely on better ADCs, does not rely on shielding. It relies on the idea of ​​​​"you repeat the same thing 100 times, and then align and stack them." The ten thousand negatives in the Gobi Desert also have the same idea.

## First, you need to believe it is possible

A sensor with an accuracy of 1% can measure a physical quantity with an accuracy of one thousandth.

This isn't magic. This requires mathematics (oversampling and statistics), physics (noise modeling and differential cancellation), system theory (Kalman filtering), circuit design (bridges and differential amplification), and a deep understanding of the nature of the sensor.

But first, it requires you to believe it is possible. 

If the group of engineers on the Gobi Desert in 1964 had chosen to give up, "The sensor is not good enough, we can't do it. Let's wait until better sensors come." There would be no conclusion to that project, the precise yield of the nuclear explosion would be unknown, and subsequent defense decisions would not be supported by data.

## Summary

Today we did one thing: **Using a variety of methodologies, from oversampling to Kalman filtering, it was proved that a sensor with an accuracy of 1% can measure physical quantities with an accuracy of one thousandth. **

Key takeaways:
1. **Oversampling + Statistical Average**: Trade time for accuracy. The noise has zero mean. Sampling 10,000 times improves the accuracy by two orders of magnitude.
2. **Differential measurement makes the common mode disappear by itself**: From the Wheatstone bridge to the CAN bus, there is no need to eliminate the noise, you only need to make the noise appear exactly the same on both channels. Then do the subtraction.
3. **The essence of Kalman filtering has only three words: prediction → measurement → update**, fusing multiple rough sensors to obtain an estimate that is more accurate than any single sensor.
