# 2.1 Thought Experiment: Building a Chip in a Primeval Forest

## 100 people thrown into the forest

You are parachuted into a primeval forest, accompanied by 99 people: physicists, chemists, mechanical engineers, programmers, all kinds of talents. Your mission is: **Create a modern CPU from scratch**.

Where are the supplies? Very generous. You have all the natural resources on earth. The wood of the entire forest, the various ores deep underground, the water in the river, the nitrogen and oxygen in the air. You have unlimited sand.

What about tools? Very simple. You can make any tool, but only from things you find in the forest. Stones can be broken, wood can be used to build frames, and vines can be tied up.

No time limit.

**Can you make it? **

Theoretically, if you were given a thousand or ten thousand years, you might be able to come up with a long plan. First make copper, then steel, then make simple machine tools, then use machine tools to make more sophisticated machine tools...climb up one level at a time. But this is not something that “100 people” can do, but requires tens of thousands of people and hundreds of years of social collaboration.

It’s not that you 100 people are not smart enough, it’s because behind chip manufacturing is a complete industrial civilization system. This system cannot be reproduced by a few people or decades. It requires thousands of factories, hundreds of thousands of engineers, and hundreds of years of technology accumulation.

The core problem is: the depth of recursion in modern industry far exceeds the carrying capacity of any small team in a limited time.

Every day you use WeChat on your mobile phone, flash code on the ECU, and compile projects on the computer. These operations are so normal that you can't feel the amazing thickness behind it.

But if you stop and think about it, the S32K144 development board in your hand has the materials on it: Cortex-M4F, 512KB Flash, 64KB SRAM, FlexCAN, LPI2C, LPSPI, and 12-bit ADC, all squeezed onto a 15 square millimeter silicon chip. What is the concept of 15 square millimeters? Your thumbnail is approximately 150 square millimeters. This chip is only one-tenth the size of a fingernail, and contains approximately tens of millions of MOSFETs.

There are tens of millions of them, and the gate oxide layer of each MOSFET is only a dozen atoms thick. The metal connections between each MOSFET are only a few dozen nanometers wide. If the position of any MOSFET is a little off, the entire chip may be useless.

Integrating tens of millions of transistors on a 15mm² silicon wafer, and the critical dimensions of each transistor must be controlled at the atomic scale, **this is chip manufacturing**.

Now back in the forest, you can’t even make a 15mm² flat silicon wafer.

## But what can you create?

But don't be discouraged. You can't build a modern CPU, but you can build a half-adder made of wood and vines. A piece of wood, two sinks, a few floats. When water is injected from tank A and tank B at the same time, the float floats and drives the connecting rod to push the "carry" rod. This is a "hydrodynamic adder". It can only do 1-digit addition at a time and can only calculate once per second, with an extremely high error rate. But it's yours. You and Pascal were on the same starting line when they built the first mechanical adding machine. This is the nature of engineering: start with a little thing that can run, and then pass it down rod by rod, and hundreds of years later there is S32K144.

## From sand to chips, what does it take?

Let’s sort out the complete industrial chain. This is not an "industrial chain" concept. This is a real physical chain. Every chain link is a factory. Each factory requires thousands of workers and engineers.

**The first link: raw materials. ** Sand → Quartz sand → Metallurgical grade silicon (purity about 98%) → Trichlorosilane (SiHCl₃, produced by the reaction of metallurgical grade silicon and hydrogen chloride at 300°C) → Polycrystalline silicon (Siemens method, reduced with hydrogen at about 1150°C, purity 99.9999999%, or nine 9) → Monocrystalline silicon rods (purity up to eleven) 9).The first ring alone requires chemical plants, distillation towers, and single crystal furnaces. The temperature in the single crystal furnace is precisely controlled at 1414°C (the melting point of silicon), and the pulling speed is accurate to millimeters per hour. The molten silicon in the crucible must rotate at an extremely slow speed. Too fast, and the crystal will have dislocations; too slow, and the doping will be uneven. The entire crystal pulling process takes more than ten hours. Any vibrations or temperature fluctuations in the process will ruin the 300 kilograms of single crystal silicon rods.

This was discovered accidentally in the laboratory by Polish scientist Czochralski in 1916. He mistakenly dipped the nib of his pen into molten tin and when he pulled it out, he found a perfect single-crystal wire hanging from it. More than 100 years later, chips around the world still use this method to pull single crystal silicon. It’s just that the parameters have changed from a “pen tip” to a 300mm diameter, 2-meter-long silicon rod.

**The second ring: wafer manufacturing. **Single crystal silicon rods are cut into 0.7mm thin silicon wafers using a diamond wire saw. This process is called "slicing". The diameter of the diamond wire saw is only 120μm, which is about the same as a human hair. The surface of the silicon wafer after cutting is as rough as sandpaper. Then it goes through lapping, etching, and double-sided polishing (CMP), and finally reaches the mirror level. Surface roughness Ra < 0.1 nm。

What is the concept of 0.1nm? The diameter of silicon atoms is about 0.2nm. Therefore, the unevenness on the wafer surface is smaller than one silicon atom.

These steps need to be completed in a clean room (ISO Class 1 clean room). The ISO Class 1 standard is that there should be no more than 10 particles with a diameter greater than 0.1μm per cubic meter of air. For comparison: the average office has approximately 35 million particles above 0.5μm per cubic meter. The hospital's ultra-clean operating room is ISO Class 5 (class 100). You're in a forest and there are hundreds of millions of particles per cubic meter.

In the virgin forest, you can't even build a clean room. Because building a clean room requires HEPA filters, and making HEPA filters requires chemical plants and precision textile equipment. HEPA filter material is ultra-fine glass fiber with a diameter of 0.5-2μm, which requires precise temperature-controlled electric furnace melting, high-precision fiber drawing machine drawing, and then meshing and folding in a clean environment. To build a high-precision fiber drawing machine, you need precision bearings, servo motors, and PLC controllers. And they each need bearing factories, motor factories, and chip factories to manufacture them. To build a chip factory, you need photolithography machines. To build a lithography machine, you need...

This is already an infinitely recursive dependency chain. And you are still in the forest, with only stones and sticks in your hands.

**The third link: photolithography. ** Coat photoresist on the wafer, use deep ultraviolet (DUV, 193nm) or extreme ultraviolet (EUV, 13.5nm) light source to expose through the mask, and develop to form nanoscale integrated circuit patterns.

Lithography machines are the most sophisticated machines on the planet. ASML's EUV lithography machine costs US$380 million, weighs 180 tons, and requires 40 containers, 20 trucks, and 3 cargo planes for transportation. Its light source is a CO₂ laser with a power of 25kW that bombards 50,000 tin droplets per second. Each drop of tin is hit twice, the first time flattening it, and the second time heating it to a plasma state of up to 500,000 Kelvin. The plasma then radiates extreme ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 13.5nm. The conversion efficiency of this light source system from electrical energy to EUV light energy is only about 0.02%. But that's not important. What's important is that it does produce strong enough 13.5nm light.

Light at 13.5nm is absorbed by almost everything. Including air, including glass lens. So the entire optical path of the EUV lithography machine is in high vacuum, using multi-layer molybdenum/silicon Bragg mirrors (each layer is atomically accurate in thickness) to focus and reflect. Each mirror costs hundreds of thousands to millions of euros, and a photolithography machine has dozens of such mirrors. The position deviation between the two layers of photolithography is required to be less than 2nm. To use an everyday analogy: it is equivalent to shooting a laser beam from Beijing and hitting a one-yuan coin in Shanghai. The deviation does not exceed half the thickness of the coin.

This is just the finished product of the photolithography machine. To build a lithography machine, you need optical lenses from Germany's Zeiss (precise to the atomic level), lasers from TRUMPF, precision mechanical frames from VDL in the Netherlands, and thousands of specially-made parts from 5,000 suppliers around the world. Going further upstream: To grind EUV mirrors, Zeiss requires ultra-high purity special glass and a nanoscale ion beam polishing process. To build CO₂ lasers, TRUMPF requires special gases and precision optical cavities. Each level has a new stack "further upstream".

**The fourth ring: etching and deposition. ** Dry etching uses plasma to bombard the silicon surface that is not protected by photoresist, digging out nanoscale trenches. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) "sticks" only one layer of atoms at a time, with thickness control accurate to the Å (0.1nm) level. Ion implantation ionizes boron, phosphorus, arsenic and other impurity atoms, accelerates them to tens to hundreds of keV, and directly "bumps" into the silicon lattice, like shooting at the silicon wafer with an atomic-level shotgun.

These steps are repeated dozens of times. The manufacturing process of a modern CPU involves more than 1,000 process steps. Each step has its own set of equipment, set of chemicals, set of process parameters, and set of testing standards.

**The fifth ring: packaging and testing. ** The wafer is cut into individual chips (die) and wire bonded or flip-chip connected to the packaging substrate. After packaging, it is put on a burn-in board and run for dozens to hundreds of hours at a high temperature of 125°C and 1.4 times the rated voltage to screen out "weak" chips that fail early. Finally, there is the full functional test and electrical characteristics test.

**Ring Six: This is not over yet. ** Going upstream, you also need a photoresist factory. Synthesis of special photosensitive polymer materials. Specialty gas plants are also needed. Silane (SiH₄), phosphine (PH₃), nitrogen trifluoride (NF₃), and tungsten hexafluoride (WF₆) are all ultra-high purity, above 99.9999%. A mask factory is also needed. Electron beams are used to carve circuit patterns on quartz substrates, and an advanced mask costs tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars. Process equipment factories are also needed. Photolithography machine, etching machine, thin film deposition equipment, ion implanter, CMP equipment. And what supports it all: the power system (the power consumption of a fab is equivalent to that of a small city), ultrapure water system (chip manufacturing consumes several thousand liters of ultrapure water per wafer), gas supply system, logistics system, and communication network.

There is also an education and training system. ASML’s optical designers don’t just fall from the sky. They need universities, research institutes, and more than ten years of professional training. TSMC’s process engineers are not created out of thin air. Taiwan began to lay out semiconductor education in the 1970s. Next to the Hsinchu Science Park are Tsinghua University and Jiaotong University. These talent supply systems themselves are part of "industrial civilization".

If you can't handle it, it's not because you're not smart enough, but because the system is too heavy.

## My first week working at the panel factory

This thought experiment is abstract to most people. To me, it's not.

I worked as a yield engineer for several years. Specifically, my job is to track and analyze defects on glass substrates. Which panel units are good and which are bad, why they are bad and how to fix them. This job is called "Yield Engineer" in the industry.

On the first day I reported to the production line, HR gave me a dust-free suit. White coveralls, masks, hair covers, shoe covers, and double gloves. Like all new employees, I spent several minutes fussing around in the locker room getting dressed. Then walk through a wind shower channel. High-pressure airflow blows from all directions, blowing away particles on the body. Then push the door open.

The moment I walked into the yellow light area, I was stunned.

This space is unlike anything I've ever seen. The ceiling, walls, and floors are all bright yellow. This is not a design aesthetic choice because photoresist is sensitive to ultraviolet and blue light, so the lighting filters out all short wavelength light. The entire area was shrouded in a dim, warm yellow light, like eternal dusk.

A low, insistent buzz filled the air. Vacuum pumps, cooling pumps, air circulation systems, and the background sound of hundreds of equipment operating at the same time. The temperature was constant at 21°C ± 0.5°C. Humidity is constant at 45% ± 5%. It's not just about comfort. Small fluctuations in temperature and humidity can affect the thickness of the photoresist, the etching rate, and even the thermal expansion of the glass substrate in the device.

Then I saw the first piece of equipment, a PECVD machine from Applied Materials. It was taller and wider than me, with a row of green-lit touch screens on the front. Dozens of pipes are connected behind it: silane, ammonia, nitrogen, argon, cooling water, vacuum pipes, exhaust pipes, and radio frequency power lines. There is a Load Port on one side of the machine, and the robotic arm is taking out a glass substrate from the sealed substrate transfer box and sending it into the reaction chamber.

The substrate transfer box is a translucent white square box. Each box contains a batch of glass substrates. The entire journey of the substrate in the production line, from photolithography to etching to deposition, is completed in these transfer boxes. The value of each glass substrate continues to grow as the process progresses. In the later stage, a G8.5 generation substrate (approximately 2.2m × 2.5m) will carry dozens of panels, and the total value may exceed 100,000 US dollars. Dropping a teleport box is worth millions of dollars. Therefore, the transfer of all substrates in the production line is automated. The OHT crane system on the aerial track transports the substrate transfer box from one equipment to another without human hands touching the whole process.

I stood in the yellow corridor, listening to the hum of the vacuum pump, and watching the overhead crane sliding silently overhead. I felt like I was not in a factory, but like I was in a spaceship.

(By the way: not the entire factory is yellow, only the photolithography area, where I stood in a daze, is this color. The front and rear sections are both normal white light.)

This is the real environment of semiconductor manufacturing. It cannot be summed up by the three words "high technology". It is a space that pushes the laws of physics to their limits. Every speck of dust is an enemy, every 0.1°C temperature change is a variable, and every piece of equipment is the ultimate application of physics.

That's why I can't make a chip in the forest. Because I have seen the world outside that "forest" with my own eyes.

## Who created whom? The reverse shaping of people by tools

This thought experiment has a deeper meaning.

You want to make chips in the forest, but you find that you must first make a lithography machine. To build a photolithography machine, you must first build precision bearings. To build precision bearings, you must first build a blast furnace and rolling mill. To build a blast furnace, you must first build refractory bricks and a blower. Each level of tools requires the tools of the previous level to build them. You are stuck in a "tool dependency tree" and can't even find the starting point.

But this is precisely the true face of modern industrial civilization: it is not people controlling tools. Rather, tools define what people can do through layers of dependency chains.

An embedded engineer writes code without thinking about "who wrote the compiler's register allocation algorithm." But behind LLVM's register allocator are decades of compiler theory, graph coloring algorithms, and SSA formalisms. These things in turn rely on deeper mathematics and graph theory. The NXP SDK you use is backed by hundreds of NXP engineers in Europe and Asia. The CMSIS called internally by the SDK is maintained by ARM. The design of ARM's instruction set took several more decades. From ARMv4 to ARMv7 to ARMv8-M. ARM's processor core is designed on EDA tools from Cadence or Synopsys. SPICE simulators in EDA tools are built on decades of semiconductor physics models. The semiconductor physical model is the fitting of thousands of experimental data.

Every line of code you write has decades of work by millions of people behind it.

Steve Jobs said repeatedly during his lifetime: "There's no such thing as a lone genius." He created the most "personally heroic" products in the world: Mac, iPod, and iPhone, but he also knew best that behind these products were the collaborative work of thousands of engineers, dozens of countries, and hundreds of suppliers.

If you can't make a chip in a virgin forest, it's not because you're not smart enough, but because the very thing of "smartness" has been structured by the entire industrial civilization. Your IQ, your knowledge, and your way of thinking all depend on this book, that school, that teacher, and that computer. They rely on printing presses, education systems, and chip factories.

You are a person, but your abilities do not belong to "you", but to the entire civilization where you are.

## Why TSMC can. It’s not just a matter of “technology”

When it comes to chip manufacturing, TSMC cannot be avoided. Many people ask: Doesn’t TSMC just use ASML lithography machines? Why can’t other people achieve TSMC’s yield rate when buying the same equipment?

The answer lies in the thickness of the ecosystem.

The first level: accumulation of process development. It has been almost 40 years since TSMC was established in 1987. In the past 40 years, every generation of process development, every yield improvement, and every process parameter optimization have been carried out. All are recorded in their internal craft database. When a TSMC engineer is debugging the 3nm process, he can call up the historical data of 5nm, 7nm, 10nm, and 16nm for reference. This continuity of craftsmanship experience cannot be bought by any latecomer.

The second layer: device collaboration. The photolithography machine and the etching machine do not work independently. If the sidewalls of the pattern after photolithography are not steep enough (the PR profile is not good), the etching cannot be accurately transferred. If the depth of the etched trench is uneven, subsequent deposition will cause problems. The output of each step is the input of the next step. The job of TSMC's process integration engineers (PIE) is to coordinate these dozens of processes. They know how the photolithography parameters need to be adjusted to maximize the etching yield, and they know how the CMP pressure needs to be modified to ensure that the focal depth of the next layer of photolithography is sufficient. This kind of cross-process collaborative knowledge is accumulated through decades of trial and error.

The third level: defect detection. Modern chip manufacturing requires online inspection. After each step, optical or electron-beam inspection equipment is used to scan the wafers for defects. A full-surface inspection of a 300mm wafer can generate tens of gigabytes of data. TSMC has thousands of testing equipment, generating massive amounts of data every day. This data is fed into a defect classification system. Image recognition algorithms are used to classify defects into types such as "particles", "scratches", "pattern loss", and "bridging". Yield engineers then correlate this data with process parameters. It was discovered that "this batch of wafers had an unusually high particle count after etch step 3," which was then traced back to "a problem with the quality of the previous batch of cleaning fluid." This data closed loop: detection → classification → traceability → improvement is the core of yield management.

The fourth layer: supply chain ecology. TSMC is in Taiwan and its supply chain is in Taiwan. Photoresist, special gases, chemical reagents, silicon wafers. Most of the key raw materials have production bases in Taiwan or East Asia. Short distance means short logistics time, low risk of contamination, and low communication costs. Germany's Infineon and Bosch have their own fabs in Dresden, but their supply chains require raw materials from Japan, Taiwan, and the United States. Logistics delays at each link will lengthen the iteration cycle of process development.

TSMC is not a company, TSMC is the crystallization of the East Asian semiconductor ecosystem.

## Then why is a car chip so expensive?

Now that you understand the thickness of the industry chain, look back at the price of automotive MCUs.

One NXP S32K144: a few dollars (about $4 in bulk). A Renesas RH850: a few dollars to a dozen dollars. One Infineon AURIX TC3xx: tens of dollars (high-end models like TC397 are about $84). A Qualcomm Snapdragon Ride (self-driving SoC): a few hundred dollars.

You may think a few dollars is not expensive. But have you ever thought about it: These few dollars contain the global semiconductor industry chain. Silicon purification, wafer manufacturing, photolithography, packaging, and testing. There are hundreds of engineers working on each link, each link is in an ISO Class 1 clean room, and the equipment in each link is worth tens of millions of dollars.

Moreover, car-grade chips have several additional requirements compared to consumer-grade chips: wide temperature range (-40°C to +125°C, while consumer-grade chips are generally 0°C to 70°C), long life (supply guarantee is 15 years, consumer-grade may only have 2-3 years), zero defects (car-grade requirements < 1ppm defect rate, consumer-grade allows ≤500ppm), certification cost (AEC-Q100 full set of tests ran down 3-4 months, plus a total of 9-12 months for preliminary engineering verification; high temperature working life of 1000 hours, temperature cycle of 1000 times, ESD human body model of 2000V, all must be passed in random tests).

For the same wafer, the yield may be 95% when tested using consumer-grade standards, but may fall below 80% when tested using automotive-grade full-temperature range standards. Because at -40°C and +125°C, many die parameters will drift out of the specification. Testing is also more complex, with each die being fully tested at at least two temperature points (low and high temperatures). The certification cycle is longer, and it takes at least half a year from production to obtaining the AEC-Q100 certification report.

So the CAN transceiver you bought for a few dollars should not be too expensive. It is the condensed product of dozens of links in human industrial civilization. What these few dollars buy is decades of knowledge accumulation by tens of thousands of workers in thousands of factories in dozens of countries.

## Chips are the collective work of mankind

We throw 100 people into a virgin forest, with all the natural resources and time, but we can't make a single chip.

What this fact tells us is not that there are “too few people”. Chips are not produced solely by the number of people. What it requires is deep division of labor, historical accumulation and global collaboration.

Chips are not the work of one person. They are the collective work of humankind's industrial civilization that spans hundreds of years.


No country can independently create a modern chip. ASML's EUV lithography machine includes American lasers (Cymer, still operating in California after being acquired by ASML), German optical lenses (Zeiss), Dutch precision machinery (VDL), and Japanese chemical materials. The degree of globalization has reached such a level that any attempt to "de-globalize" will have to pay an unbearable price.

Some people may ask: Does China’s self-developed chips negate the global division of labor?

No.

The purpose of self-developed chips is not to "do everything by ourselves", but to avoid being stuck in key links. The global division of labor in chips is an objective reality and an economic law. It is neither possible nor necessary for any country to be 100% self-sufficient in the entire chain. But every major country hopes to take the initiative in core technologies.

Each of us stands on the shoulders of countless others.

Every line of code you type on the keyboard has been passed down through hundreds of years of scientific accumulation and decades of industrial system construction. You are not a lone engineer. You are a link in the relay of collective human intelligence. The powerlessness you feel when trying to make a chip in the forest. Exactly how you should feel.

Pay tribute to the workers in all aspects. From the excavator operator in the Australian mine, to the technologist pulling single crystals in Germany, to the equipment engineer in the Hsinchu fab who wears dust-free clothes to adjust the machine table, to the doctor who adjusts the EUV optical system in the Netherlands, to the operator doing wire bonding in Malaysia. I also pay tribute to every engineer and worker who works silently on China's wafer factories, equipment factories, packaging factories, and SMT production lines.

You have never met, but your labor is superimposed on the same piece of silicon, forming the physical basis of the ECU in your car.

Without you, there would be no chip. Without chips, there would be no modern world.

## Summary

Today we did something: throwing 100 people into the virgin forest, proving that the chip is not the work of one person, but the work of the human collective.

Key takeaways:
1. Chip manufacturing requires a complete industrial civilization system: from Australian mines to Dutch lithography machines, thousands of factories, hundreds of thousands of engineers, and hundreds of years of technology accumulation. If any link is broken, the chip cannot be manufactured.
2. **The tool dependency chain defines what people can do**: To build a chip, you must first build a lithography machine; to build a lithography machine, you must first build a precision bearing... You are trapped in an infinitely recursive dependency chain.
3. The CAN transceiver you bought for a few dollars is the product of dozens of links in human industrial civilization. Behind each car-grade chip are countless people who have never met it.
